# AEG Weitwinkelobjektiv 17mm f/6.3

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** DE 620,538 C  
**Filed:** 6 August 1932 (patented in the German Reich from this date)  
**Granted:** 3 October 1935 (grant announced); specification issued 23 October 1935  
**Applicant:** Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft, Berlin  
**Title:** Abänderung eines Weitwinkelobjektivs (Modification of a wide-angle objective)  
**Example details:** the single table on page 2, in millimetres, used at 1:1; one drawing sheet

The patent specification names no inventor. Later literature attributes the design to Hans Schulz of the AEG research institute; that attribution does not come from the patent.

The patent describes an objective for cloud photography, long astronomical exposures and similar work, where the 150° to 160° field of earlier wide-angle lenses is not enough and a field of 180° or more is wanted. It contains one numerical example, and that example is modelled here without any change to its radii, spacings or glasses.

The link to a real lens is strong, although it rests on secondary evidence:

1. A surviving camera, sold at auction as an "AEG Weitwinkelobjektiv 6.3/1.7cm" with serial number 301 and dated to about 1935, was accompanied by a copy of the patent document dated 23 October 1935, the issue date of DE 620,538.
2. The example, traced exactly as printed, has a focal length of 16.68 mm, which rounds to the engraved 1.7 cm.
3. The literature describes the AEG lens as five elements in four groups with two diverging menisci ahead of the stop and a cemented group behind it, which is the construction of the example.
4. The traced image circle for a 180° field is 50.1 mm, a full circle inside the 6.5 × 9 cm plates the surviving camera takes.

The patent does not state the focal length, the f-number, the back focus or the field angle of its example. The aperture of f/6.3 is taken from the surviving lens, not from the patent. No factory drawing has been compared, so the model shows the patented design, which the evidence above ties to the lens as built.

## Optical Architecture

The design has five elements in four groups, in the order negative, negative, stop, positive cemented doublet, positive. It is an early fisheye: two strongly bent negative menisci in front of the stop fold a hemisphere of object space into a narrow cone, and a positive system behind the stop forms a real, flat image.

The patent's central idea is to divide the front negative power between several menisci instead of one. With a single negative front lens, it explains, the bundles would enter the stop too far from their original direction, and the steep angles of incidence at that one lens would produce more astigmatism than the positive system behind the stop could correct. In the example the two outer front surfaces share a centre of curvature: r1 has its centre 67.0 mm behind the front vertex and r3 has its centre 66.75 mm behind it. The inner surfaces r2 and r4 are nearly hemispherical, and their centres lie in front of the stop, as the patent requires.

Behind the stop, the patent asks for a large air space between the two positive members, larger than the sum of their centre thicknesses, in order to obtain a flat image. The example has 23.0 mm of air against 9.2 mm of glass in the doublet and 8.3 mm in the last lens. That air space is also at least a quarter of the 90.2 mm vertex length, another of the patent's stated proportions.

Traced as printed, the system has a focal length of 16.68 mm, a back focus of 28.99 mm and a length of 119.19 mm from the front vertex to the image.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

Focal lengths are for each element alone in air, computed from the printed radii, thicknesses and indices.

### L1 (patent lens A): Negative Meniscus, Convex to Object

nd = 1.5400, νd = 59.6. Glass: BaK 2 (as printed in the patent). f = −83.7 mm.

The large front meniscus has a 67.0 mm outer radius and a 26.5 mm inner radius. It gives the first, gentler part of the angular compression. Its outer surface is nearly concentric with the outer surface of the second meniscus, so oblique bundles meet both outer surfaces at similar, moderate angles of incidence. The patent states that the outer radii r1 and r3 are smaller than the length of the whole system, which holds here (67.0 mm and 43.35 mm against 90.2 mm).

### L2 (patent lens B): Negative Meniscus, Convex to Object

nd = 1.5400, νd = 59.6. Glass: BaK 2 (as printed in the patent). f = −32.5 mm.

The second meniscus is the stronger of the two. Its inner radius of 12.26 mm forms the deep, nearly hemispherical cavity that faces the stop across a 24.1 mm air space. The drawing shows this element seated behind the flat rim of the first, with a flat land around its own cavity.

### L3 + L4 (patent member C): Cemented Positive Doublet

L3, biconvex positive: nd = 1.6138, νd = 56.3. Glass: SK 6 (as printed in the patent). f = +12.7 mm.

L4, negative meniscus: nd = 1.6364, νd = 35.4. Glass: F 6 (as printed in the patent). f = −21.2 mm.

The doublet sits directly behind the stop and has a combined focal length of +33.6 mm. The patent specifies a positive lens cemented to a negative lens with the negative lens facing the image. The crown element is biconvex, with a weak front surface (87.6 mm) and a steep cemented surface (−8.3 mm); the flint is a negative meniscus. The 21-point difference in Abbe number across the cemented surface is where the colour introduced by the two front crowns and the rear crown is corrected, and this is the only flint glass in the design.

### L5 (patent lens D): Biconvex Positive

nd = 1.6074, νd = 56.7. Glass: SK 2 (as printed in the patent). f = +60.3 mm.

The last lens stands 23.0 mm behind the doublet. The patent says that the positive member nearest the image is given a long focal length so that it still accepts the marginal bundles, and that this focal length can exceed four times that of the whole system. Here it is 3.6 times the 16.68 mm focal length. The patent adds that this lens may be made aspherical if required; the example is spherical.

## Glass Identification

The patent prints both the glass names and the optical values, so nothing is inferred: BaK 2 (1.5400 / 59.6) for both front menisci, SK 6 (1.6138 / 56.3) and F 6 (1.6364 / 35.4) for the doublet, and SK 2 (1.6074 / 56.7) for the rear lens. The indices are printed as n_d. The BaK 2 and SK 2 values agree with present-day catalogue d-line data for glasses of those names (1.53996 / 59.7 and 1.60738 / 56.65), so the helium d-line is used as the reference.

The palette is four ordinary crowns and one flint of the period. BaK 2 and SK 2 agree with a present-day catalogue entry within the printed precision, so those three elements are traced on the catalogue dispersion curves (a BAK2 entry and N-SK2). SK 6 and F 6 have no exact present-day entry; they keep the printed index and Abbe number and are marked as unmatched in the data file.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent describes no focusing movement and gives one set of spacings, so the model is fixed at infinity. With a focal length under 17 mm at f/6.3, depth of field covers most practical distances for the sky photography the patent has in mind.

## Stop, Clear Apertures and Field

The patent table gives no stop position, no stop diameter and no clear diameters. These are inferred:

- The stop is placed at the front vertex of the doublet. The drawing shows it about 1 mm ahead of that vertex.
- The stop semi-diameter of 3.21 mm is the value that gives f/6.3.
- Every semi-diameter, and the flat lands around the two cavities, are estimates. They are smaller than the drawing suggests for the front element (48 mm against roughly 58 mm) and for the other elements.

With these rims the full f/6.3 beam passes every surface from the axis to a 90° half-field; the front rim begins to cut the beam at about 93°. The patent says that fields up to 210° can be reached with this type of design, but it does not say what field the example itself covers.

Image height grows almost in proportion to field angle: 8.69 mm at 30°, 17.13 mm at 60° and 25.07 mm at 90°. The last value is 4.3 % less than focal length times angle, so the equidistant fisheye projection is the closest standard description. The 50.1 mm image circle in the data file is the traced diameter for a 180° field.

## Patent Values and the Model

| Quantity | Patent | Model |
|---|---|---|
| Radii, spacings, indices, Abbe numbers | 9, 8, 5 and 5 values | all identical |
| Focal length | not stated | 16.68 mm |
| f-number | not stated | 6.3 (from the surviving lens) |
| Back focus | not stated | 28.99 mm (paraxial, infinity) |
| Image plane in the drawing | about 29 mm behind the last surface | 28.99 mm |
| r1 and r3 concentric | required | centres 67.0 mm and 66.75 mm behind the front vertex |
| Air space between positive members | at least a quarter of the vertex length (22.55 mm) | 23.0 mm |

The last gap is the paraxial back focus, since the patent prints none. Longitudinal spherical aberration at the edge of the f/6.3 pupil is −0.12 mm, which is small enough to show that the printed table describes a working lens.
