# Petzval Landscape Lens, Second Form, f/8.1 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 103

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 258, Fig. 103 (data under Fig. 101, p. 250)
**Data after:** Fr. Voigtländer, *Akademiker Professor Dr. Joseph Petzval in Wien beleuchtet vom Optiker Voigtländer in Braunschweig* (Braunschweig: Fr. Vieweg & Sohn, 1859), p. 103 (von Rohr's "Quelle: Fr. Voigtländer 3. 103")
**Designer:** Joseph Petzval
**Manufacturer:** C. Dietzler, Vienna, the holder of the 1857 Austrian patent on the lens in von Rohr's account (pp. 258–261); Petzval made the first examples himself in 1856
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1856

Fig. 103 is captioned "J. Petzval: Landschaftsobjektiv. 2. Form" and stands in von Rohr's section on the landscape
objective, which his margin titles "Das Landschaftsobjektiv (Orthoskop)" (pp. 258–262). The caption prints no table
of its own. It gives the reduction to fD = 100 mm and the computation for 1:8.1 and ω = 30°, and refers the radii,
thicknesses and distances to the table of the first form under Fig. 101 (p. 250), adding only "nur L3 ist
umgekehrt": the negative lens L3 is turned round. Tafel II redraws the lens at natural size as No. 103, with curve
103a (spherical aberration) and 103b (astigmatism).

The model is therefore von Rohr's trial of Petzval's landscape lens of 1856, not a production prescription. Von
Rohr writes that despite every effort he did not succeed in finding the final data (p. 260); after computing the
reversed form, that he believes he does not possess the final data (p. 261); and that the construction details of
the 1856 lens are at present not known exactly at all (p. 262).

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** Under Fig. 101 (p. 250): the radius magnitudes r1 = 22.5, r2 = 17.6, r3 = 185.7,
r4 = 53.5, r5 = 31.5, r6 = 78.2, r7 = 22.8; the thicknesses d1 = 2.5, d2 = 0.6, d3 = 0.6 and 1.2 for L4 (the scan
shows its subscript as 5); the distances b1 = 3.7 (front pair to stop), b2 = 1.2 (stop to L3) and b3 = 1.6 (L3 to
L4); and the indices L1 = L3 = 1.517, L2 = L4 = 1.575. Fig. 103 adds the reversal of L3, so r4 = 31.5 now faces the
stop and r5 = 53.5 faces L4, together with the computation aperture 1:8.1 and the half-field ω = 30°. The text
(p. 261) gives his results for this form: overcorrected back focus, undercorrected focal lengths, smaller
aberrations overall for parallel light, and oblique pencils much improved but still unfavourable, with an image
decidedly convex toward the objective.

**Assumed by von Rohr.** Every thickness and every distance (d1–d4, b1–b3) carries the footnote "Willkürlich
angenommen", arbitrarily assumed. Fig. 101 names Voigtländer (p. 98) for the radii, J. M. Eder (1891, p. 115) for the
glass types and Voigtländer (p. 103) for the lens separation, but the printed separations are still marked as
assumed. The reversal of L3 rests on a statement, which von Rohr notes nobody cited in the whole dispute, that the
1856 lens has the curvatures of the negative lens reversed against Fig. 101 (p. 261); that the statement stands in
the patent text Voigtländer reprinted on p. 103 is an inference from the caption's source line.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 103 and Tafel II: a biconvex crown cemented to a biconcave flint with an almost flat
  rear face; a biconcave crown L3 with its deeper face toward the stop; a positive flint meniscus L4 concave toward
  the stop. The last gap, 88.195 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii of 6.5 mm on all seven surfaces, a heuristic value kept from the delivered model (see Aperture, Field
  and Rims); the stop radius, 5.459 mm, derived from 1:8.1; the f-stop list 8.1–11–16–22–32; the 30° field limit
  (the printed ω); and the element focal lengths, computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The rounded table traces to an EFL of 99.115 mm, 0.89 % short of fD = 100; 0.05 mm
on r1 or r7 alone moves the EFL by about 0.5 mm, and von Rohr computed with his source's data before reducing it to
f = 100 (notes to the plates). At f/8.1 the entrance-pupil radius is 6.118 mm. Scaled to f = 100 and read against
curve 103a at von Rohr's computed heights 3.56, 5.08 and 6.17 mm, the model's back-focus aberration is −0.03,
+0.10 and +0.40 mm (plate +0.03, +0.13, +0.50) and its focal-length aberration −0.31, −0.49 and −0.48 mm (plate
−0.31, −0.45, −0.41). Lengthening r7 by 0.05 mm gives −0.01, +0.14, +0.48 and −0.30, −0.45, −0.41, so the residual
lies inside the radius rounding. On curve 103b, at about 17.5° the model's sagittal and meridional foci lie −1.01 and
+3.66 mm from the paraxial focus (plate −1.05 and +3.7); at about 24° the sagittal focus is −0.82 mm (plate −0.89)
and the meridional focus runs past the plate's +4 mm edge in both. Only the last computed angle disagrees:
the plate's sagittal curve returns to about −0.1 mm, while the model's reaches +0.9 to +1.35 mm between 29.3° and
30°, where the curve is steepest.

## Optical Architecture

The lens has two members about a stop: a cemented positive achromat in front and an air-spaced negative pair behind
it. The front member has a focal length of +59.3 mm and the rear member −148 mm; the rear member stretches the
front member's focus to an EFL of 99.1 mm, with a back focus of 88.2 mm behind a construction only 11.4 mm long.
From the first vertex to the focus the lens measures 99.6 mm, about its focal length.

The rear negative member is the Petzval idea in this design. The front achromat alone has a Petzval sum of
+0.0118 mm⁻¹; the rear member contributes −0.0058 mm⁻¹, so the system sum falls to 0.00596 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius
of about 168 mm or 1.7 f. Reversing L3 leaves the first-order layout and the Petzval sum unchanged (the first form
traces to 99.106 mm) and acts only on the aberrations: the rim value of the back-focus aberration moves from
−1.12 mm in the first form to +0.40 mm, and at 20° the meridional focus moves from +10.8 to +6.2 mm.

The stop behind a positive front member gives pincushion distortion. The chief ray through the stop centre lands
0.4 % beyond f·tan ω at 10°, 1.6 % at 20° and 4.3 % at 30° in this model.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 + L2 — cemented front achromat (D1)

L1: nD = 1.517 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = +19.5 mm.
L2: nD = 1.575 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint, unmatched. f = −27.9 mm.

L1 is a strongly biconvex crown, 2.5 mm thick, its deeper face r2 = 17.6 forming the junction with the flint; at
the 6.5 mm rim its edge is only 0.30 mm thick. L2 is a 0.6 mm biconcave flint with an almost flat rear face
(r3 = 185.7). The pair is one +59.3 mm achromat carrying all of the system's collective power. The same front member
served in 1840 for both the portrait and the landscape combination of Petzval's Satz (p. 249), and the tables agree:
the portrait objective's front radii under Fig. 100 (52.9, 41.4, 436.2) divided by 2.35 give 22.5, 17.6 and 185.6,
with the same two indices (computed here; the thicknesses, assumed in both tables, differ).

### L3 — Biconcave Negative, reversed

nD = 1.517 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = −38.3 mm.

L3 stands 1.2 mm behind the stop. In the first form its weaker face (53.5) faced the stop; here the deeper face
r4 = 31.5 does, and r5 = 53.5 faces L4. Its power is the same either way, so the reversal is a pure bending of the
lens, and it alone moves the back-focus aberration from the strong undercorrection of curve 101a to the
overcorrection of curve 103a. The lens is 0.6 mm thick on the axis and 1.67 mm at the rim.

### L4 — Positive Meniscus, concave toward the stop

nD = 1.575 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint, unmatched. f = +55.5 mm.

L4 is a 1.2 mm flint meniscus with a weak concave face toward L3 (r6 = 78.2) and a strong convex rear face
(r7 = 22.8). Its rear radius is the strongest lever on the spherical aberration: 0.05 mm on r7 moves the EFL by
0.48 mm and the rim back-focus aberration by 0.08 mm. With L3 it forms the −148 mm rear member.

## Glass Selection

| Von Rohr's index | Elements | Hatching on Fig. 103 | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| nD 1.517 | L1, L3 | old crown (L1, L3) | positive front lens; negative rear lens |
| nD 1.575 | L2, L4 | flint (L2; L4 not resolvable) | negative front lens; positive rear lens |

Von Rohr prints only "Glasarten nD", after J. M. Eder (1891, p. 115). The plates' legend hatches flint rising to
the right and old low-index crown falling; on the scan of Fig. 103, L1 and L3 carry the crown hatching and L2 the
flint hatching, while L4's narrow section is not resolvable. The focal length confirms the printed order: exchanging
the indices of L3 and L4 would give f = 165.5 mm instead of 99.1 mm.

No dispersion is printed, not even a barred ν̄; von Rohr's ν̄, where he prints it, is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD) from his
glass table (p. 339), a photographic blue figure rather than the Abbe number. The model keeps nD only, with no Abbe
number and no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

One inference can still be drawn from the powers. In thin-lens terms the flint-to-crown power ratio is 0.683 in the
front pair and 0.685 in the rear pair, so both members meet the thin-lens achromatism condition for the same
dispersion ratio of the two glasses, about 0.68 (for example ν 60 and 41). The rear pair, a negative crown with a
weaker positive flint, has the arrangement an achromatic negative member requires; it is not an inverted achromat.
This is a computed inference, not a source statement.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

Von Rohr computes the lens for 1:8.1 (the first form's combination was tried in 1840 at its full 1:8 without a stop,
p. 251) and for a half-field of 30°. The model sets the iris from that aperture: an entrance-pupil radius of
6.118 mm and a physical stop radius of 5.459 mm, 3.7 mm behind the front member. The f/8.1 axial beam reaches
6.12 mm at r1 and about 5.4–5.5 mm on the rear lenses.

The clear radii are not printed. The model keeps 6.5 mm on every surface, the estimate of the delivered model, under
the least-change rule: the axial beam passes, and the exact chief ray passes at 30° (largest height 3.71 mm at r1).
The oblique pencils are vignetted in the ordinary way: at 30° the front pair cuts 44–49 % of one half of the
meridional pencil and L4 14–18 % of the other half, and a meridional trace passes 96 % of the stop diameter at 10°,
86 % at 20°, 78 % at 25° and 69 % at 30°. The paraxial field is limited by r1 at 45°. At 30° the meridional focus
lies tens of millimetres behind the focal plane, von Rohr's "decidedly convex" image: the 30° limit is the angle he
computed, not a field of usable definition.

Fig. 103 is drawn to scale along the axis: with the 11.4 mm vertex length as scale (32.4 px/mm at 800 dpi), the
stop and the vertices of L3 and L4 fall within 0.1 mm of the table. Its heights are less consistent. The front pair
is drawn to 6.7 mm and the stop opening to 5.0 mm, but the rear lenses only to 4.5 mm; Tafel II, drawn at natural
size, gives 6.6, 5.35 and 4.6–4.7 mm. At 4.5 mm the rear lenses would cut the f/8.1 axial beam to about 83 % of its
diameter, close to 1:9.8, so the drawings cannot set the rear rims for the printed aperture. The first form's
Fig. 101 draws its rear lenses as tall as the front pair.

## Design Heritage and Context

In 1840 Petzval computed three single lenses on a sheet handed to Fr. Voigtländer: lenses 1 and 3 formed the
portrait objective, lenses 1 and 2 the landscape objective, which Voigtländer later called the Orthoskop (Fig. 99,
pp. 249–251). Lens No. 2 appeared on that sheet with two different radius combinations. The landscape combination,
f = 352 mm, was tried by An. Martin at the full 1:8 and set aside: it gave too small an image for landscapes and no
focusing range, and Petzval was dissatisfied with it (p. 251). Fig. 101 takes its radii from Voigtländer's p. 98;
von Rohr computes it from the data "on the slip mentioned" (p. 260), evidently that sheet.

Before mid-July 1854 Petzval was working with the Vienna optician C. Dietzler, who made his portrait objective. At
the suggestion of the k. k. geographical institute (von Rohr queries whether the military one) he took up the
landscape lens again, presented it at the 32nd meeting of German naturalists and physicians in Vienna in September
1856, and showed examples he had finished with his own hands in his private workshop (pp. 258–259). In 1858 he
described a variable lens separation, meant not to change the focal length but to let the photographer introduce
any amount of spherical aberration (p. 260); the model keeps the fixed separations von Rohr assumed.

Voigtländer, by then in Braunschweig, began making the landscape lens from the 1840 data and introduced it as the
Orthoskop, which forced Dietzler to adopt the name as well; Petzval disliked it and would have preferred
"photographischer Dialyt" (pp. 259–260). In the quarrel of 1857/58 in English journals Voigtländer claimed the
right to make it because he had made the same construction in 1840; von Rohr notes that Petzval had in fact given
him the first data for this objective too, and ends the question with a non liquet (p. 261).

**Patent context.** Voigtländer's pamphlet reprints a patent bearing Petzval's signature (p. 103). A patent
attorney's search for the original in Vienna reported on 29 December 1898 that no such patent could be found; von
Rohr then found a note (Kr. Z. 1863, vol. 7, p. 72) that it had been granted to C. Dietzler on 28 December 1857 and
extended to a sixth year, and asked others to publish its text and drawing (pp. 260–261). The patent is context for
the reversed negative lens, not the source of the numbers, which all come from Fig. 101. The connection with
Dietzler later ended because, according to Eder, he did not supervise the making of the objectives closely enough
(p. 262).
