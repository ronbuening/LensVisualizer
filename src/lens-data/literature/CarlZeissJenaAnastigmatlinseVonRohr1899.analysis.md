# Carl Zeiss Jena Anastigmatlinse 1:12.5 (von Rohr 1899, Fig. 140)

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 379, Fig. 140
**Designer:** Paul Rudolph
**Manufacturer:** Carl Zeiss, Jena
**Source dated:** 1899

Von Rohr captions the table "P. Rudolph: Anastigmatlinse 1 : 12.5" and gives its source as "Angaben des Verfassers",
the book author's own information, rather than an earlier publication. The surrounding text places the lens in the
Zeiss series history: the Anastigmat-Satzlinse Series VI of 1893 (1:14.5) was replaced in spring 1895 by a new series,
which von Rohr says was introduced in 1895 as the Anastigmatlinse 1:12.5, Series VII, together with the Satz-Anastigmat
Series VIIa built from two such lenses. His bibliography lists Rudolph's British patent for the series (E.P. 19,509 of
13 October 1894, "on Ser. VII") and Rudolph's 1896 paper on "the new Satz-Anastigmat 1/6.3 of the firm of Carl Zeiss".

The numbers in Fig. 140 are not the patent's. On p. 380 von Rohr says the data of the new lens can be taken from the
patent, and that he gives instead a somewhat later form for single lenses of longer focal length, worked out mainly
with the double objective in view. The design date of this particular form is therefore not stated: it is later than
the October 1894 patent and earlier than the book.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr (p. 379):**

- Radius magnitudes r1 to r5: 14.7, 23.4, 11.2, 7.4 and 15.4 mm. The decimal point of r5 is broken in the scan
  ("15 4"); 15.4 is the only reading that gives a focal length near the stated 100 mm.
- Stop distance b1 = 1.9 mm in front of r1; thicknesses d1 to d4 = 1.3, 1.7, 1.7 and 1.3 mm.
- Indices nD: L1 1.51743, L2 1.61002, L3 1.51156, L4 1.58254. No glass names and no dispersion figures.
- Reduction to fD = 100 mm; rated aperture 1:12.5; computed ("durchgerechnet") for 1:10.5 and ω = 34°.
- On p. 380: within an aperture of about 1:11.4 the spherical aberration is fully corrected and the zone amounts to only
  −0.8 mm; the meridional field is flat, and the sagittal foci lie less than 0.8 mm from it.

**Stated by von Rohr as assumed or estimated:** nothing. The caption notes only that the drawing is approximate
("Die Zeichnung ist nur annähernd genau").

**Added by this model:**

- Radius signs (−, +, −, −, −), read from the figure: the cemented block is a meniscus concave to the stop, L1 thin at
  the centre and L2 thick at the centre. The same sign pattern and element shapes are those of Figure 1 of Rudolph's
  patent (biconcave L1, biconvex L2, concavo-convex collecting L3, convexo-concave dispersing L4), which is context, not
  the source of the numbers.
- The image distance, 112.193 mm, as the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear semi-diameters of 4.8 mm on every face. This is a modelling estimate, not a figure measurement (see Aperture and
  Field).
- The f-stop list 12.5, 16 and 22, starting at the rated 1:12.5.
- The trace field limit at the printed 34°.

**First-order comparison:** the computed focal length is 100.714 mm against the printed 100 mm, a +0.71 % residual
from radii rounded to 0.1 mm. The model's stop gives f/12.5 on that focal length. The traced half-field is 34°, the
printed angle.

## Optical Architecture

The Anastigmatlinse is a single cemented group of four elements behind a front stop: a landscape-lens arrangement in
which the whole block is a meniscus about 6 mm thick, concave toward the stop, with the stop 1.9 mm in front of the
first vertex. The total length from stop to last vertex is 7.9 mm on a 100 mm focal length.

The four elements form two cemented pairs joined at the middle surface r3. Rudolph's principle, set out in the patent
and summarised by von Rohr, is the "contrary gradation of refractive indices" of his 1890 Anastigmat doublet: in one
pair the positive element has the higher index (the "new" Jena-glass pairing), in the other the negative element has
the higher index (the "old" crown-flint pairing). In Fig. 140 the new pair comes first: L1 (nD 1.517, negative) with L2
(nD 1.610, positive), then the old pair L3 (nD 1.512, positive) with L4 (nD 1.583, negative). The middle cemented
surface joins the two positive elements, one of the two simple cases von Rohr names.

Taken separately in air, split at r3, the front pair has a focal length of about +37 mm and the rear pair about −70 mm;
the cemented block as a whole has f = 100.7 mm. The Petzval sum of the model is 0.00193 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about
5.2 times the focal length, which is what lets a single cemented lens correct astigmatism over a wide field.

Von Rohr notes that a lens of this kind needs achromatism only in the end result: how the chromatic correction is shared
between the new and the old pair does not matter. The same element can serve alone or as half of a Satz-Anastigmat;
Fig. 141 on the same page shows two of these lenses placed symmetrically about a central stop (1:6.3, ω = 27½°).

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Biconcave Negative

nD = 1.51743 (no dispersion printed). Glass: unmatched historical glass. f = −17.3 mm in air.

The front element meets the light from the stop with a concave face (r1 = −14.7). It is the lower-index
negative partner of the new pair: its index lies below that of L2, so the cemented surface r2 (+23.4) is a weak
collecting surface.

### L2 — Biconvex Positive

nD = 1.61002 (no dispersion printed). Glass: unmatched historical glass. f = +12.7 mm in air.

The strongest element of the lens and the high-index positive of the new pair; its index lies in the range of the
Jena baryta crowns, although von Rohr names no glass. Its rear face r3 (−11.2) is the middle cemented surface, where
it meets the low-index positive L3. With 1.7 mm centre thickness and two strongly curved faces, L2 reaches zero edge
thickness at a height of 4.97 mm; this, not the drawing, bounds the clear diameter of the whole block.

### L3 — Positive Meniscus

nD = 1.51156 (no dispersion printed). Glass: unmatched historical glass. f = +37.0 mm in air.

A concavo-convex collecting element, concave toward the stop (r3 = −11.2, r4 = −7.4), and the lower-index positive of
the old pair. Across r3 the index falls from 1.610 to 1.512, so this cemented surface carries real refracting power.

### L4 — Negative Meniscus

nD = 1.58254 (no dispersion printed). Glass: unmatched historical glass. f = −26.0 mm in air.

The rear dispersing meniscus and the higher-index negative of the old pair. The cemented surface r4 (−7.4) is the most
strongly curved surface of the lens; with the index rising across it from 1.512 to 1.583 it acts as a dispersing
surface concave to the stop. The rear face r5 (−15.4) closes the meniscus toward the image.

## Glass Selection

Von Rohr prints only the sodium-D index of each glass and gives neither glass names nor a dispersion figure for this
lens. Where his tables do give dispersion, his barred ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), a photographic blue figure defined
by the column heads of his glass table on p. 339; it is not the Abbe number. With nothing printed here, the model
carries nD only, at the D line, and leaves every element as an unmatched historical glass. No modern catalog glass is
substituted, and no chromatic behaviour is claimed.

| Element | nD | Role in the index gradation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L1 | 1.51743 | lower-index negative of the new pair |
| L2 | 1.61002 | higher-index positive of the new pair |
| L3 | 1.51156 | lower-index positive of the old pair |
| L4 | 1.58254 | higher-index negative of the old pair |

The labels in the data file describe these index relationships rather than glass types. Rudolph's patent prints nD and
nG′ for its own examples (in its first example L1 and L3 share one glass of nD 1.510), but those are different glasses
from the ones in Fig. 140 and are not used here.

## Aperture and Field

**Aperture.** The lens is rated 1:12.5, which the model takes as its nominal stop. At that stop the axial marginal ray
rises from 4.03 mm at the stop to 4.53 mm at r5. Von Rohr computed the lens to 1:10.5, but in the rounded prescription
the 1:10.5 marginal ray reaches r2 at 5.11 mm, above the 4.97 mm height at which r2 and r3 meet, so it cannot pass
through L2. That opening is not offered as an f-stop.

**Spherical aberration.** The model's on-axis longitudinal aberration at 1:12.5 is −0.11 mm at the margin and −0.76 mm
at 0.7 of the pupil; the largest zone, about −0.78 mm, matches von Rohr's printed −0.8 mm. The marginal ray crosses
zero at about 1:12.3 rather than his 1:11.4. The difference is within the rounding of the radii: a change of 0.05 mm in
r4 alone moves the 1:11.4 marginal value by about 0.8 mm.

**Clear radii.** Every face carries 4.8 mm, a modelling estimate. The figure draws one square-edged block
with all four elements to the same height, and the model keeps that. Its absolute scale is too rough to measure from:
scaled by the printed glass thickness the drawn rim is about 7.2 mm, by the stop-to-last-vertex distance about 6.0 mm,
while the drawn element thicknesses and stop distance depart from the printed ones by up to a factor of two. Both
readings exceed the 4.97 mm at which L2's edge vanishes, so the drawing cannot set the rims. At 4.8 mm L2 keeps a
0.12 mm edge and the 1:12.5 pencil passes unclipped.

**Field.** The printed half-field is 34°. The chief ray at 34° passes every rim (3.59 mm at r5), and the paraxial
chief-ray limit of the rims is 38.9°, set by r5. The oblique pencil is cut by the rims as the angle grows: a meridional
real-ray scan across the 1:12.5 stop transmits about 93 % at 10°, 82 % at 20° and 65 % at 34°, the upper part of
the pencil being stopped at r2, r4 and r5. This is ordinary vignetting and stays in the model. Von Rohr's statements
about field flatness and astigmatism are his computation and are not re-derived here.

## Design Heritage and Context

Rudolph's Anastigmat of 1890 (D.R.P. 56,109) was a doublet of an old achromat and a new achromat with opposite index
gradations. The Series VI Satzlinse of 1893 made single lenses that could be combined into double objectives; the Series
VII of 1895 went further by cementing the two components of the original doublet into one four-part lens, so that each
single lens is itself corrected for astigmatism and can be paired with another of similar focal length. Von Rohr reports
that Rudolph stepped the focal lengths of the single lenses by a factor of about 1.2 from one number to the next, and
that combining two numbers made sense only for focal lengths that did not differ too much, since the mount of the
shorter lens would otherwise cut the aperture of the pair at larger angles.

The patent context (E.P. 19,509 of 1894, Paul Rudolph of Jena as applicant) describes three arrangements: Figure 1, the
type of Fig. 140, with the two positive elements cemented together; Figure 2, with the two negative elements cemented
together; and Figure 3, two Figure 1 lenses arranged symmetrically. Its first example, normalised to unit focal length,
has radii −0.132, +0.237, −0.116, −0.071, −0.138, an effective aperture of 0.091 and a field of more than 85°; every
value differs from Fig. 140. Von Rohr also notes that the shorter-focus single lenses of the series were in a different
state of correction with respect to Abbe's sine condition.
