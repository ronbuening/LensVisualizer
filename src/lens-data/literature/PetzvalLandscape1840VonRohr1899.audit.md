### Signs and their basis

Fig. 101 and drawing No. 101 on Tafel II show a biconvex L1 cemented to an L2 with a nearly flat rear face, a
biconcave L3 and an L4 meniscus concave toward the object, all four square-edged at one height. That gives r1, r3 and
r5 positive and r2, r4, r6 and r7 negative. Two numerical checks support the reading: with r3 = −185.7 the EFL would be
62.9 instead of 100, and the signed table reproduces curves 101a to within 0.05 mm (below). Drawing No. 101 is to
the table's scale: at 600 dpi its vertex spacings read 2.54, 0.47, 5.06, 0.74, 1.61 and 1.27 mm against the printed
2.5, 0.6, 4.9, 0.6, 1.6 and 1.2 (thin elements read thick by the line width), and its stop bar sits 3.9 mm behind r3
and 1.1 mm before r4 (printed 3.7 and 1.2).

### Rim decisions

No printed field angle to test against, so the rims were checked at several angles at f/8.1. The f/8.1 axial beam
reaches 6.12 mm at r1 and 5.41–5.48 mm on the rear lenses: no on-axis clip. The chief ray passes at every angle up to
45° (heights 3.71, 2.88 and 2.55 mm on r1–r3 at 30°) and is blocked at r1 and r2 by 50°; the paraxial rim limit is
45.1° at r1. One-sided vignetting at 30°: 44 % (r1), 49 % (r2), 31 % (r3), 1 %, 12 % and 16 % (r5–r7). Nothing is
clipped, so the rims stay as staged.

### Field limit

`projection.maxTraceFieldDeg` 15 → 30. The 15° was the package author's exploration window. Von Rohr prints no ω for
this form; 30° is this model's choice, matching the ω = 30° he computed for the second form (Fig. 103, p. 258), which
has the same spacings and stop. The chief ray passes there with ordinary one-sided vignetting, and the limit is stated
as the model's in the analysis.

### Maker

Von Rohr credits Fr. Voigtländer with making this design: Petzval gave him the execution, and he made the lenses for
the trial photographs of autumn 1840, when the landscape combination (352 mm, 1:8) was tested and set aside (p. 251).
After Petzval and C. Dietzler issued the 1856 lens, Voigtländer, in Braunschweig since 1849, began to make the
landscape lens "auf Grund der Angaben vom Jahre 1840" as the Orthoskop (p. 260) and defended that by having made the
construction in 1840 (p. 261). The second form, Fig. 103, is the Petzval–Dietzler lens. Voigtländer is in the maker
registry, so `maker` is set; von Rohr had no production data of the Orthoskop, and the file is not presented as one.
Design date: 1840 (computed by mid-1840, trial lenses in the autumn). The staged key already carries it.

### Patent check (D6)

Von Rohr names one patent in this context, for the landscape lens of 1856, not for this form: a patent bearing
Petzval's signature, printed by Voigtländer (1859, p. 103), granted to C. Dietzler on 28 December 1857 and extended
to the sixth year (Kr. Z. 1863. 7. 72). He gives no number, and a Vienna patent attorney could not find the original
(reported 29 December 1898). An Austrian privilege of 1857 is outside the Espacenet, DEPATISnet and Google Patents
collections, and without a number there was nothing to look up. Result: **no table available**; the book is the only
numerical source for this form.

### Open limitations

- Every thickness and distance, the stop position included, is von Rohr's assumption.
- Lens 2 appeared on Petzval's sheet with two radius combinations (p. 251); von Rohr does not say which one Fig. 101
  shows. Fig. 103 reverses L3, as the 1856 patent text does.
- Rims are an estimate, 0.4 mm smaller than drawn, where the drawing reaches L1's knife edge.
- nD only; no chromatic behaviour is modelled.
- The 30° field limit is the model's, not von Rohr's.
- The maker credit rests on von Rohr's account of the 1840 trials and of Voigtländer's Orthoskop; the coordinator may
  prefer `null` for a design that was set aside in 1840.
