# Steinheil Orthostigmat Type II f/4.02 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 142

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 383, Fig. 142
**Data after:** R. Steinheil, English patent specification, in the excerpt "Steinheil's improvements in lenses", *British Journal of Photography* 43 (1896), No. 1891, pp. 489–490
**Designer:** Rudolf Steinheil
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1893

Fig. 142 is captioned "R. Steinheil: Orthostigmat. Typus II." with the source line "Quelle: R. Steinheil. 3.", which
points to entry (3.) under R. Steinheil in the bibliography (p. 430): D.R.P. 88 505 of 28 November 1893 on the
Orthostigmat Type II, used through the excerpt of the English patent named above. The text says the same: the exact
data of the objective are taken from the English patent specification (p. 383). The lens is drawn again, without the
table, as No. 142 among the portrait objectives of Tafel I, with its spherical-aberration curves (142a) and
astigmatism curves (142b).

Von Rohr's account (pp. 382–384) runs as follows. The Orthostigmat was announced in summer 1893, and its publication
in Germany was long delayed by patent difficulties, an opposition by C. P. Goerz. A first type, conceived by
A. Steinheil and computed by his son R. Steinheil, was filed on 25 March 1893; it coincided with Zeiss's Series VIa
and with the Doppel-Anastigmat, so the application was withdrawn, and C. A. Steinheil Söhne made that Type I in France.
In November of the same year R. Steinheil filed a second type built of two cemented triplets of substantially
different composition, introduced as the Orthostigmat Type II. The English specification contains two constructional
forms. The slower one is the familiar universal lens (Fig. 143); the faster one, printed here, has "the type of a
portrait objective" (p. 383).

Von Rohr knows of commercial use only for the slower form: two series at 1:6.8 and 1:10 sold in Germany under patent
88 505, and a 1:8 form offered from summer 1899 (p. 384). No manufacturer is therefore credited for this fast form.
The patentee is Dr. Rudolf Steinheil of Munich; the design date is that of the patent, 28 November 1893.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** Reduction to fD = 100 mm; computation for 1:4.02 and ω = 29°; the radius magnitudes
r1 = r8 = 47.9, r2 = r7 = 117.6, r3 = r6 = 29.7 and r4 = r5 = 102.4; the thicknesses d1 = d6 = 4.2, d2 = d5 = 5.2 and
d3 = d4 = 0.8; the distances b1 = b2 = 5.2 from each triplet to the stop, which places the stop at the centre; and nD
1.62356 (L1, L6), 1.50786 (L2, L5) and 1.60304 (L3, L4). The text names the glasses and gives their ν̄ (p. 383), and
Tafel I adds the computed zonal curves.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** No value in the table is marked as assumed. His notes to the plates
explain that each lens was computed from the data of its source, usually with five-place tables, and only then
reduced to f = 100 mm; that only the points marked by horizontal lines on the curves were computed; and that the
extreme values quoted in the text are rough estimates. The text's figures for this lens, spherical correction for
about 1:4.5 and zones of about −0.3 mm, are of that kind.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 142 and Tafel I and required by the patent claim: a biconvex outer lens, a crown
  meniscus convex toward the stop, and a biconcave inner lens, the rear triplet mirroring the front.
- The last gap, 86.553 mm, the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii of 14 mm on every face (earlier estimates, see Aperture, Field and Rims), the iris radius derived from
  1:4.02, the f-stop list 4.02–16, the field limit at the printed 29°, and element focal lengths computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The table traces to an EFL of 100.163 mm, 0.16 % long. That is rounding: the patent's
own values reduced without rounding give exactly 100, and 0.05 mm on r1 alone moves the EFL by 0.24 mm. At the model's
EFL the 1:4.02 entrance pupil has a radius of 12.458 mm. The odd aperture is itself a reduction: the patent states
1:4 for a nominal 476 mm but prints a semi-aperture X0 = 59.5 mm, and its data compute to 478.38 mm, so
478.38 / 119 = 4.02.

Curve 142a was computed at three incidence heights, which divide the 1:4.02 pupil into zones of equal area. Read from
the plate and compared with the model scaled to f = 100 (back focus is the solid curve, focal length the dashed):

| Incidence height (f = 100) | 142a, back focus | Model | 142a, focal length | Model |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12.43 mm | +0.36 | +0.39 | +0.15 | +0.16 |
| 10.13 mm | −0.14 | −0.05 | −0.25 | −0.18 |

The outer and inner points agree within 0.03 mm; the unrounded patent values give +0.37 and +0.14 at the margin. The
middle zone is 0.07–0.09 mm less undercorrected in the model, and the patent values do not close that gap
(−0.06 / −0.19), so it is not the table's rounding. The model crosses zero at an incidence height of 10.55 mm (1:4.7),
the plate's interpolated curve near 11.0 mm (1:4.55), in line with the text's "about 1:4.5".

## Optical Architecture

Six elements in two air-separated groups: two identical cemented triplets set symmetrically about a central stop,
5.2 mm from each. Each triplet has a focal length of +176.9 mm; the pair, 10.4 mm apart at the vertices, gives
100.16 mm. Von Rohr writes the cell M N H with the signs − ± + over the letters: M the negative light flint next to
the stop, N the crown and H the positive baryta flint outside.

The patent states the principle behind the cell. An astigmatic correction can only be made at a junction that turns
its concave side to the more strongly refracting medium, a spherical correction only at one that turns it to the
weaker; the half-objective therefore carries the astigmatic correction at its first junction and the spherical
correction at its second, with a positive lens enclosed by a biconvex and a biconcave lens of higher index. Here r2,
concave toward the flint L1 (1.62356 against 1.50786), is the first kind, and r3, concave toward the crown L2
(1.50786 against 1.60304), the second. Surface powers in each cell are +0.01302 (r1), +0.00098 (r2), −0.00320 (r3)
and −0.00589 mm⁻¹ (r4).

The Petzval sum is 0.00684 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of 146 mm or about 1.5 f. Von Rohr reads the computed oblique
pencils as showing that the design chiefly removed the astigmatic difference while leaving a small negative field
curvature, a clear gain over the older portrait systems off axis (p. 384). The symmetry about the stop largely
suppresses coma, distortion and lateral colour.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Biconvex Positive, outer collective

nD = 1.62356 (sodium D), no νd published (von Rohr's ν̄ = 29.6). Glass: baryta flint (von Rohr), unmatched.
f = +55.1 mm.

The outer lens of each triplet holds the strongest collective face, r1 = 47.9, and meets the crown at the weak
junction r2 = 117.6. A flint as the outer positive lens is the notable choice of this form: the claim requires only
that the biconvex and the biconcave lens both refract more strongly than the enclosed crown, and the fast form meets
that with a dense flint where the universal form uses its heaviest baryta crown.

### L2 / L5 — Positive Meniscus, enclosed crown

nD = 1.50786 (sodium D), no νd published (ν̄ = 49.6). Glass: borosilicate crown (von Rohr), unmatched. f = +76.7 mm.

The low-index lens the claim calls the enclosed positive lens, 5.2 mm thick and convex toward the stop. Its front
junction is the astigmatic surface and its rear junction, r3 = 29.7, the spherical one; it is also the only
low-dispersion glass in the cell.

### L3 / L4 — Biconcave Negative, inner flint

nD = 1.60304 (sodium D), no νd published (ν̄ = 29.0). Glass: ordinary light flint (von Rohr), unmatched.
f = −38.1 mm.

A thin lens, 0.8 mm at the axis and 5.3 mm at the 14 mm rim, between the deep junction r3 and the weaker face
r4 = 102.4 that looks at the stop. Its air face is the strongest negative surface of the cell and with r3 supplies the
negative Petzval contribution.

## Glass Selection

| Glass (von Rohr) | Symbol | nD | ν̄ | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Baryt-Flint (baryta flint) | H | 1.62356 | 29.6 | L1, L6 | outer biconvex collective |
| Borosilikat-Crown (borosilicate crown) | N | 1.50786 | 49.6 | L2, L5 | enclosed positive meniscus |
| Gewöhnliches Leichtflint (ordinary light flint) | M | 1.60304 | 29.0 | L3, L4 | inner biconcave lens |

Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), defined by the column heads of his glass table on p. 339. It measures dispersion
toward the photographic blue rather than over the F–C interval, so it is not an Abbe number and is not stored as νd.
The patent prints nF beside nD (1.63498, 1.51351 and 1.61428), but no nC, so it gives no νd either. The model keeps nD
only, with no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

The two flints have almost the same ν̄ (29.6 and 29.0) at indices 0.02 apart, with the crown between them: ν̄M < ν̄N >
ν̄H, which von Rohr notes does not fit his classification of these types. Cut into two achromats, he adds, the cell
groups its glass pairs under the second possibility of Rudolph's anastigmatic principle. The universal form uses
three glasses whose ν̄ rise in step (39.2, 41.6, 44.6).

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The iris sits at the printed central position. Its radius follows from 1:4.02: 11.00 mm in the engine's paraxial
sizing, while the real 1:4.02 marginal ray crosses the stop plane at 11.23 mm. Fig. 142 draws the stop at about
11.3 mm and the incident axial beam at about 12.7–12.8 mm, close to the 12.46 mm pupil.

The printed ω = 29° (an image circle of about 111 mm at f = 100) is the field limit. The chief ray passes every rim
there, at 7.37 mm on r1 and r8 and 3.4–6.0 mm inside; the outer rims cut the oblique pencil on one side by 15–34 %,
ordinary vignetting that is kept. The paraxial rim limit is 45.8°, set by r1 and r8. Curve 142b was computed at about
17°, 24° and 29°: the focusing-screen shifts for the sagittal and meridional pencils are −2.7 and −1.8 mm at 17°,
−4.8 and −1.0 mm at 24°, and −6.5 and +4.1 mm at 29°. The astigmatic difference grows from 1 mm to more than 10 mm
over that range, which is why von Rohr regrets having computed rays of too great an inclination. The curve was not
compared numerically with the model.

| r1–r4 | 14.0 | 12.46, 12.26, 11.86, 11.53 | 13.4 | 13.4–13.6 |

The 14 mm rims are earlier estimates, not figure measurements. Both drawings show each triplet as one cylinder
about 0.5 mm smaller; at 13.5 mm the axial beam still passes and the chief ray still reaches 29°, with 4–5 % more
one-sided vignetting. The rims were kept under the least-change rule.

## Design Heritage and Context

D.R.P. 88 505 ("Objectiv für photographische Zwecke", Dr. Rudolf Steinheil in München, patented from 28 November
1893, issued 4 September 1896; the DEPATISnet copy is the second printing of 12 September 1899) is context here, not
the source of the numbers. It prints two numerical examples with nD and nF: a universal objective of 493 mm at 1:6.2,
the lens of Fig. 143, and the 476 mm, 1:4 objective that von Rohr reduces in Fig. 142. Scaled by its computed focal
length, the second example gives 47.87, 117.68, 29.69 and 102.47 for the radii and 4.18, 5.23, 0.84 and 5.23 for the
spacings: von Rohr's table to within his 0.1 mm rounding, with the same three indices.

The patent's first form had a wider commercial history. D. Kaempfer of Voigtländer & Sohn had found the same type
independently, and the two firms agreed to share the right to make the objective protected by patent 88 505, which
Voigtländer sold as the Collinear, Type I (p. 384). Von Rohr describes no production version of the fast portrait
form.
