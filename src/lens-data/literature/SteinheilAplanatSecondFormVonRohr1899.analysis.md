# Steinheil Aplanat, Second Form, f/7.7 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 111

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 298, Fig. 111
**Data after:** J. M. Eder, *Die photographischen Objektive, ihre Eigenschaften und Prüfung* (Halle: W. Knapp, 1891), p. 73 (von Rohr's "Quelle: J. M. Eder 1. 73")
**Designer:** Adolph Steinheil (Hugo Adolph Steinheil, 1832–1893)
**Manufacturer:** C. A. Steinheil Söhne, Munich (the family firm run by Adolph and Eduard Steinheil from 1865, pp. 289, 295)
**Source dated:** 1899

Fig. 111 is captioned "A. Steinheil: Aplanat, 2. Form". Von Rohr discusses it on pp. 297–298 of the historical
part of his book, in the section on A. Steinheil's slower objectives, after the original Aplanat of 1866 (Fig. 110,
p. 294). He reduces the data to fD = 100 mm and computes the lens for 1:7.7 and a half-field ω = 30°. Tafel II
redraws it at natural size among the universal objectives as No. 111, with curves 111a (spherical aberration) and
111b (astigmatism).

The design date is not known. Von Rohr states that the Aplanat types he takes from Eder's Handbuch could no longer
be dated exactly and that the date of their introduction appears to be unknown (p. 297). He calls it the second
form and compares it with "the type of 1867", the patent design of Fig. 110, so it follows that design in his
sequence; Eder's book of 1891 is the latest date its publication allows. The file therefore carries no design
year.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The three radius magnitudes as symmetric pairs (r1 = r6 = 26.3, r2 = r5 = 11.0,
r3 = r4 = 38.3), the glass thicknesses d1 = d4 = 1.05 and d2 = d3 = 2.0, the cell-to-stop distances
b1 = b2 = 9.0, the two nD values (1.61912 outside, 1.58027 inside), the reduction to fD = 100 mm, the computation
aperture 1:7.7 and the half-field ω = 30°. The text adds the dispersion ratio of the two glasses and the results of
his ray trace: spherical correction at 1:9.1 with a longitudinal zone of only about −0.12 mm, and at 30° a
meridional image that meets the focal plane with a 7 mm astigmatic difference to the sagittal image.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Nothing. The caption marks no value as assumed. According to his
notes on the plates, he traced every lens with the data of the named source and only then reduced the data and
results to f = 100, so the table is a rounded reduction of Eder's numbers.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 111, from Tafel II and from the text: each cell is a negative outer meniscus
  cemented to a positive inner meniscus, both convex toward the object in front and concave toward the stop; the
  rear cell mirrors the front.
- The last gap, 87.125 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints no back focus.
- Clear radii of 6.85 mm on all six surfaces, a heuristic value set by the chief ray at 30° and bounded by the
  drawings (see Aperture, Field and Rims). The stop radius, 5.873 mm, is derived from 1:7.7.
- The f-stop list 7.7–9.1–11–16–22–32, the 30° field limit (the printed ω) and the element focal lengths, computed
  thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The rounded table traces to an EFL of 99.437 mm, 0.56 % short of 100 mm. The f-number
is f/7.7 at the model's own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 6.457 mm. The longitudinal spherical aberration is
+0.341 mm at the rim of the f/7.7 pupil, −0.017 mm at 0.85, −0.137 mm at 0.7 and −0.128 mm at 0.5; the marginal ray
crosses zero at 1:8.94 and the zone reaches −0.148 mm near 0.6 of the pupil. Von Rohr gives zero at 1:9.1 and a
zone of about −0.12 mm, and curve 111a crosses zero at an incidence height near 5.5 mm (model 5.56 mm). The small
shift lies within the rounding: 0.05 mm on r1 moves the EFL by 0.8 % and the zero crossing between 1:8.55 and
1:9.38, and 0.05 mm on r2 moves it between 1:8.36 and 1:9.66. A Coddington trace along the exact chief ray gives a
meridional focus 0.12 mm behind the paraxial plane at 30° and an astigmatic difference of 7.06 mm, matching the
printed 7 mm.

## Optical Architecture

The lens is a holosymmetric doublet, the type Steinheil chose for the Aplanat as he had for the Periskop: two
identical cemented cells face each other across a central stop, each made of two flint menisci with the
higher-index glass outside (p. 296). Each cell is a weak positive meniscus of +180.9 mm, about 1.8 times the focal
length of the pair, and the cells stand 18.0 mm apart. The total length from first to last vertex is 24.1 mm,
against 24.4 mm for the first form; von Rohr notes that the whole instrument became somewhat shorter.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 26.3 | air → 1.61912 | +0.0235 | outer collective face |
| r2 = 11.0 | 1.61912 → 1.58027 | −0.0035 | deep cemented junction, weakly dispersive |
| r3 = 38.3 | 1.58027 → air | −0.0152 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The symmetry about the stop removes coma, distortion and lateral colour at unit magnification and keeps them small
for distant objects; for the Aplanat, von Rohr notes, the freedom from distortion rested on the symmetry alone. The
computed chief-ray image height at 30° is 57.65 mm against f·tan ω = 57.41 mm, about 0.4 % of pincushion
distortion in this model. The design work lies in the spherical, chromatic and astigmatic correction of the single
cell. The Petzval sum is 0.00714 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 140 mm or 1.4 f. The meridional image is the
one held close to the focal plane: in the model it falls 1.33 mm short of the plane between 20° and 23° and
returns to it at 30°, while the sagittal image lies 6.94 mm short at 30°. Von Rohr finds the oblique pencils
corrected much as in the first form, with somewhat less astigmatism and somewhat more field curvature (p. 298).

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L4 — Negative Meniscus, outer flint

nD = 1.61912 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: higher-index flint, unmatched. f = −31.4 mm.

The outer meniscus is convex toward the object in front (r1 = 26.3) and much more deeply curved at its cemented
face (r2 = 11.0), so it thickens from 1.05 mm on the axis to about 2.5 mm at the rim. Its outer face carries the
cell's collective power. Placing the higher-index flint outside follows the Aplanat's rule from the patent form
on. L4 mirrors it in the rear cell, convex toward the image.

### L2 / L3 — Positive Meniscus, inner flint

nD = 1.58027 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: lower-index flint, unmatched. f = +25.9 mm.

The inner meniscus is the collective lens of the cell, 2.0 mm thick on the axis and concave toward the stop
(r3 = 38.3). Its front face shares the deep radius r2 with L1. Because the index step across r2 is only 0.039, that
junction has little paraxial power, but its strong curvature acts on the marginal rays and on the oblique pencils.
It is the strongest lever on the zonal spherical aberration in this model: 0.05 mm on r2 moves the zero crossing
between 1:8.36 and 1:9.66. With the printed radii the lens reaches a knife edge at a radius of 7.20 mm, which caps
the clear radius of the whole cell. L3 mirrors it in the rear cell.

## Glass Selection

| Von Rohr's description | nD | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher-index flint, outside | 1.61912 | L1, L4 | negative outer menisci |
| Lower-index flint, inside | 1.58027 | L2, L3 | positive inner menisci |

Von Rohr prints no glass names for this form. The text describes the Aplanat as built from pairs of flint menisci
with the more strongly refracting glass outside (p. 296); both drawings hatch the outer lens as flint, while the
inner lens carries the hatching that the plate legend assigns to old low-index crown. The text and the index of
1.58 point to a lighter flint, and the labels follow the text.

Von Rohr also prints no dispersion for either glass. His source gave, for the whole Eder series, only the ratio of
the yellow-to-violet dispersions of the two glasses: 1.213 in the type of 1867 and 1.244 in this form (p. 297), with
1.216 for the Landschaftsaplanat that follows. He infers that the wider spacing of the glasses (an nD difference of
0.039 against 0.033 for the first form, 1.61603 and 1.58329) also brought a larger ν-difference, but notes that the
ratio does not allow a numerical check. With no dispersion published, the model keeps nD only, with no Abbe number
and no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic. Steinheil himself saw an advantage of the Aplanat in
the close agreement of the two flints' dispersion in the blue and violet (p. 296).

## Aperture, Field and Rims

Von Rohr computes the lens for 1:7.7, a little faster than the 1:8 of the first form. The model sets the iris from
that aperture: the entrance-pupil radius is 6.457 mm at the model's EFL and the physical stop radius 5.873 mm. The
f/7.7 axial beam reaches 6.46 mm at r1 and 5.72 mm at r6. Both drawings show the incident axial beam as two lines
6.56–6.58 mm from the axis, the 1:7.7 pupil at f = 100 (6.49 mm), and draw the stop opening at about 6.1 mm.

The printed half-field is ω = 30°, a 60° field, and von Rohr's 30° results concern narrow pencils along the chief
ray. The exact chief ray at 30° crosses r1 and r6 at 6.824 mm, so the rims are set to 6.85 mm on all six surfaces.
The oblique beams are heavily vignetted: a meridional trace computed for this model passes about 80 % of the stop
diameter at 10°, 62 % at 15°, 43 % at 20°, 23 % at 25°, 10 % at 28° and only the chief ray at 30°. Even at
7.15 mm, just inside the knife edge of the inner menisci, it would pass only 6 % at 30°, because the edge rays of
the full stop cross the cell surfaces 9.5–12 mm from the axis there. Of the Landschaftsaplanat that follows, von
Rohr notes that large fields are worked with strong stopping down, if only to avoid vignetting by the lens rims
(p. 299).

Fig. 111 and Tafel II are drawn to scale: their vertex spacings agree with the printed thicknesses and gaps to
within about 0.3 mm. Both draw each cell square-edged to about 7.9–8.1 mm, with the inner meniscus running out to
an edge at the rear corner. With the printed radii that edge falls at 7.20 mm, so the drawings support the 6.85 mm
rims but not a radius past 7.2 mm.

## Design Heritage and Context

A. Steinheil began computing the Aplanat in the winter of 1865/66, immediately after L. Seidel had given him
trigonometric formulas for tracing rays through a lens system (pp. 293–294). The Aplanat was completed in the
summer of 1866, a Bavarian patent was taken on 12 November 1866 and, according to von Rohr's bibliography, granted
on 14 January 1867; Eder (1891, pp. 71–72) reproduces it. In a letter of 2 March 1867 the firm asked Th. Ross to
take out patents and handle sales in the English-speaking countries, having patented the lens only in France,
Austria and Bavaria (p. 295). The first form, Fig. 110, comes from Eder p. 71, where Eder reproduces the patent,
and von Rohr discusses it as the patent design: 1:8, r 25.3 / 10.4 / 35.9, d 0.8 / 1.3, b 10.1 and nD 1.61603 /
1.58329, with a zone of −0.34 mm. Its glasses, thicknesses and spacing all differ from Fig. 111, so the patent
is context here, not the source of these numbers.

In early July 1867 A. Steinheil had Seidel present a report to the Munich Academy that describes two further
Aplanat types, one like the patent design at 1:7 and a Landschafts-Aplanat at 1:24, but von Rohr could not connect
them to Eder's undated examples (pp. 296–297). The second form is the first of the undated examples that follow
the patent form. Its glasses lie further apart (dispersion ratio 1.244 against 1.213), and its zone is much
smaller: −0.12 mm at 1:9.1 against −0.34 mm at 1:8. Von Rohr follows it with the Landschaftsaplanat (Fig. 112,
1:10.5) and the Weitwinkelaplanat for landscapes (Fig. 113, 1:17.7), which carry the Aplanat type to ever wider
fields at smaller apertures.
