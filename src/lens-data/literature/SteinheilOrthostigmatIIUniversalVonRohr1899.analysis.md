# Steinheil Orthostigmat Type II f/6 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 143

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 383, Fig. 143
**Data after:** R. Steinheil (caption "Quelle: R. Steinheil. 3."; bibliography p. 430: D.R.P. 88 505 of 28 November 1893, used through the English patent abstract "Steinheil's improvements in lenses", B. J. 1896, vol. 43, No. 1891, pp. 489–490)
**Designer:** Rudolf Steinheil
**Manufacturer:** C. A. Steinheil Söhne, Munich
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1893

Fig. 143 is captioned "R. Steinheil: Orthostigmat. Typus II." Von Rohr discusses it on pp. 382–384, in the
section on German and Austrian optics. The first Orthostigmat, applied for on 25 March 1893, collided with the Zeiss
Series VIa and Goerz's Doppel-Anastigmat; its application was withdrawn and the firm made that type in France. In
November of the same year Rudolf Steinheil applied for a different type built from two cemented triplets, introduced
as the Orthostigmat Type II. Von Rohr takes its exact data from the English patent and reports that the specification
holds two forms. Fig. 142 is the faster portrait form; Fig. 143, the form modelled here, is the slower one, which on
p. 384 he calls "the generally known form of the universal lens". He reduces it to a focal length of 100 mm and
computes it for 1:6.0 and a half-field ω = 28°.

The bibliography entry behind "Steinheil 3" names the German patent D.R.P. 88 505, "Objectiv für photographische
Zwecke", and says von Rohr used the abstract of the English patent printed in The British Journal of Photography in
1896. On the basis of patent 88 505, two series of the Orthostigmat Type II were sold in Germany at 1:6.8 and 1:10,
and from the summer of 1899 a 1:8 form as well (p. 384). Von Rohr knew the commercial use only of this slower form.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The radius magnitudes as four symmetric pairs (r1 = r8 = 35.0, r2 = r7 = 28.3,
r3 = r6 = 15.8, r4 = r5 = 41.0), the glass thicknesses of each cell (4.1, 5.1, 0.8), the cell-to-stop distances
b1 = b2 = 5.06, the three nD values, the reduction to fD = 100 mm, the computation aperture 1:6.0 and the half-field
ω = 28°. Page 384 adds the glass names, von Rohr's dispersion figures ν̄ and the results of his ray trace: spherical
correction reached for about 1:6 with zones of not more than about −0.5 mm, and a meridional focal curve more
strongly curved than the sagittal one. Tafel II redraws the lens as No. 143 with the computed curves 143a and 143b.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Nothing. The table marks no value as assumed: it is the patent's
first example reduced to f = 100 and rounded to 0.1 mm (0.01 mm for b). Von Rohr's notes on the plates state that
he traced each lens with the source's own data and only then reduced data and results to f = 100.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 143 and confirmed by the patent claim: a biconvex outer lens, a positive middle lens
  and a biconcave lens next to the stop in each cell. The rear cell mirrors the front one.
- The last gap, 85.498 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints no back focus.
- Clear radii of 10 mm on every surface, a heuristic value kept from the package that agrees with the drawing. The
  stop radius (7.034 mm) is derived from 1:6.
- The f-stop list 6–8–11–16, the 28° field limit (the printed ω) and the element focal lengths, computed thick in
  air.

**Printed against modelled.** The printed table traces to an EFL of 99.587 mm, 0.41 % short of the stated 100 mm.
The residual is rounding, not a transcription error: the patent's unrounded values scaled to f = 100 trace to
100.000 mm, and the rounding of r3, r4, r1 and d2 accounts for most of the difference. The f-number is f/6 at the
model's own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 8.299 mm. The longitudinal spherical aberration at the f/6 pupil is
−0.101 mm at the rim, −0.489 mm at 0.85, −0.517 mm at 0.7 and −0.343 mm at 0.5 of the pupil; the marginal ray
crosses zero near 1:5.9 and the worst zone is −0.53 mm. This matches the text. Von Rohr's curve 143a, computed from
the unrounded data, lies up to about 0.17 mm further toward positive values, most at the margin (see Aperture, Field
and Rims). The model traces the chief ray out to the printed 28°.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a symmetrical double lens: two identical cemented triplets face each other across a central stop,
10.12 mm apart. The patent claim defines the cell: a positive lens enclosed by a biconvex lens and a biconcave lens,
both more strongly refracting than the enclosed lens. The patent places the astigmatic correction at the first
cemented surface, which turns its concave side toward the more strongly refracting glass, and the spherical
correction at the second, which turns its concave side toward the weaker one. Von Rohr files this form under 1aα in
his scheme of Rudolph's anastigmatic principle (p. 383), with the dispersion order ν̄M < ν̄N < ν̄H.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 35.0 | air → 1.61003 | +0.0174 | outer collective face |
| r2 = −28.3 | 1.61003 → 1.51874 | +0.0032 | collective junction, concave toward the denser L1 |
| r3 = −15.8 | 1.51874 → 1.56370 | −0.0028 | dispersive junction, concave toward the lighter L2 |
| r4 = 41.0 | 1.56370 → air | −0.0137 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

Each cell has a focal length of +168.8 mm, about 1.7 times that of the pair. The Petzval sum of the objective is
0.00431 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 232 mm or 2.3 f. Each cell is 10.0 mm thick on the axis, and the length
from first to last vertex is 30.12 mm.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Biconvex Positive, outer collective

nD = 1.61003 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 44.6. Glass: heaviest baryta crown, unmatched. f = +26.3 mm.

The densest glass of the system carries most of the cell's positive power at its outer face. Its rear face is the
collective junction r2 with the lighter L2; with the patent's rule this is the surface that corrects astigmatism.
At the 10 mm rim the lens keeps an edge of 0.8 mm, about what Fig. 143 shows. L6 is its mirror image.

### L2 / L5 — Positive Meniscus, enclosed middle lens

nD = 1.51874 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 41.6. Glass: crown with high dispersion, unmatched. f = +60.5 mm.

The lowest-index glass forms the thick enclosed lens that the patent claim names. Both its faces are convex toward
the stop. The front junction with L1 lowers the index and collects; the rear junction r3 = −15.8, the most strongly
curved surface in the system, raises the index into L3 and disperses. That rear junction is the spherical correction
surface. L5 mirrors it in the rear cell.

### L3 / L4 — Biconcave Negative, inner lens

nD = 1.56370 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 39.2. Glass: baryta light flint, unmatched. f = −20.1 mm.

The thin flint closes each cell toward the stop. Its stop-side face (r4 = 41.0, concave toward the stop) is the main
negative contribution to the Petzval sum. The lens is only 0.8 mm thick on the axis and 5.6 mm at the rim. L4 is its
mirror image.

## Glass Selection

| Von Rohr's name | Symbol | nD | ν̄ | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schwerstes Baryt-Crown (heaviest baryta crown) | H | 1.61003 | 44.6 | L1, L6 | outer high-index collective |
| Crown mit hoher Dispersion (crown with high dispersion) | N | 1.51874 | 41.6 | L2, L5 | low-index enclosed positive lens |
| Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | M | 1.56370 | 39.2 | L3, L4 | medium-index negative inner lens |

Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), the dispersion figure defined by the column heads of his glass table on
p. 339. It measures dispersion toward the photographically active blue (G′ line) rather than across the F–C
interval, so it is not an Abbe number and cannot be converted to νd. The patent prints nF beside each nD (1.61759,
1.52561 and 1.57160 for H, N and M) but no nC, so no Abbe number can be formed from it either. The model keeps nD
only, with no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

The three figures differ little (39.2 to 44.6), and the order ν̄M < ν̄N < ν̄H is what places the form in von Rohr's
group 1aα. The index steps do the main work: H against N gives the collective junction with a falling index, N
against M the dispersive junction with a rising index. The companion form of Fig. 142 uses an ordinary light flint,
a borosilicate crown and a baryta flint instead, with ν̄M < ν̄N > ν̄H, which von Rohr finds outside his scheme.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

Von Rohr computes the objective for 1:6.0. The model sets the iris from that aperture: the entrance-pupil radius is
8.299 mm at the model's EFL and the physical stop radius 7.034 mm. The f/6 axial beam reaches 8.30 mm at r1 and
7.22 mm at r8, inside the 10 mm rims. The production series were rated 1:6.8, 1:8 and 1:10 (p. 384), so the
computed aperture is a little faster than any commercial form von Rohr names.

The printed half-field is ω = 28°, a 56° field. The 10 mm rims pass the chief ray at 28°, with chief heights of
7.42 mm at r1 and r8; the paraxial rim limit is 35.4°. At 28° the outer rims r1 and r2 cut about 55 % of one half
of the meridional beam, which is ordinary rim vignetting for a symmetrical objective of this length. The field
is limited to the printed 28° without widening any rim.

The 10 mm rims come from the package as an estimate and agree with the drawing. Fig. 143 is drawn at about natural
size for f = 100: its vertex length scans at 29.7 mm against the table's 30.12 mm. Both cells end square, every
surface of a cell at one height, at 10.0 mm on the page scale and 10.2 mm when scaled from the vertex length. The
lines drawn in front of the lens show the incident axial beam at 8.4 mm, the 1:6 pupil, and the stop sits centrally
15.1 mm behind the front vertex. The patent drawing of the same example shows a wider cell, about 10.7 mm on the
f = 100 scale; the model follows von Rohr's figure.

The plates record von Rohr's own trace, computed with the unrounded source data. Curve 143a gives the longitudinal
aberration at incidence heights of about 8.3, 6.8 and 4.8 mm as roughly +0.05, −0.42 and −0.40 mm, with the drawn
minimum near −0.48 mm at 5.9 mm. The patent values scaled to f = 100 give +0.04, −0.45 and −0.40 mm at those heights,
and the rounded table −0.12, −0.52 and −0.42 mm: rounding moves the marginal value by about −0.16 mm. Curve 143b gives
the focusing-screen shifts for the oblique pencils at about 17°, 23° and 28°: sagittal −1.9, −3.4 and −4.5 mm,
meridional −2.4, −4.6 and −6.8 mm. Von Rohr reads the larger meridional curvature as a lens separation that is
slightly too small; a changed separation would remove the astigmatism and, he expects, leave only a slight field
curvature.

## Design Heritage and Context

The patent is context here, not the source of the numbers in the file. D.R.P. 88 505, granted to Dr. Rudolf
Steinheil of Munich with effect from 28 November 1893 and issued on 4 September 1896, prints two numerical examples.
The first, for a focal length of 493 mm at 1:6.2, gives R0 = R14 = +173.0, R2 = R12 = ±139.66, R4 = R10 = ±77.805
and R6 = R8 = −202.47 mm, thicknesses 20, 25 and 4 mm and a distance of 50 mm between the cells, with nD and nF for
each glass. Its nD values are those of Fig. 143. The example traces to an EFL of 494.15 mm; scaled to 100 mm it gives
r = 35.01, 28.26, 15.75 and 40.97, d = 4.05, 5.06 and 0.81, and b = 5.06, which von Rohr rounds to his table. The
second example (476 mm, 1:4) carries the glasses of Fig. 142. The patent drawing, checked here in its 1899 second
edition, confirms the shapes and signs read from Fig. 143.

The cell can be read as two achromats sharing the middle lens: a new achromat outside (dense crown against a lighter
glass across a collective junction) and an old achromat toward the stop (light crown against a denser flint across a
dispersive junction). In the patent dispute E. von Höegh argued that this type follows from the Doppel-Anastigmat
cell by interchanging its lenses (p. 385). Von Rohr grants that the derivation is possible but notes that the patent
office recognised an inventive step for von Höegh, Steinheil and Kaempfer alike.

Von Rohr also reports (p. 384) that D. Kaempfer of Voigtländer & Sohn had found the type of this first patent form
independently, with trial lenses dating back to July 1892. The two firms agreed on a joint right to make the objective
protected by patent 88 505, which Voigtländer sold as the Collinear Type I in Series II at 1:6.3, III at 1:7.7 and IV
at 1:12.5. The symmetrical layout keeps coma, distortion and lateral colour small, so the design work lies in the
spherical, chromatic and astigmatic correction of the single cell; the patent states that each half is corrected on
its own.
