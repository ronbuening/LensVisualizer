# Von Höegh Doppel-Anastigmat f/8 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 138

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 376, Fig. 138
**Data after:** E. von Höegh, D.R.P. 74 437 of 20 December 1892 (von Rohr's "Quelle: E. von Höegh 2", bibliography p. 415)
**Designer:** Emil von Höegh
**Manufacturer:** Optische Anstalt C. P. Goerz, Friedenau / Schöneberg bei Berlin (licensed to Ross & Co., London)
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1892

Fig. 138 is captioned "E. von Höegh: Doppel-Anastigmat". Von Rohr discusses it on pp. 375–377 of the historical part
of his book, in the section on German and Austrian optics. Emil von Höegh had published on the spherical correction
of telescope objectives in 1886 and worked on photographic objectives in Paul Wächter's workshop in Friedenau before
joining C. P. Goerz; on 15 May 1893 he presented the new objective in a lecture to the Berlin Verein zur Förderung
der Photographie. The data source named under the figure, von Höegh 2,
is the German patent D.R.P. 74 437 of 20 December 1892, "Sphärisch, chromatisch und astigmatisch corrigirtes
Objectiv", granted to C. P. Goerz in Schöneberg bei Berlin. Von Rohr reduces it to a focal length of 100 mm and
computes it for 1:8.0 and a half-field ω = 36°.

The lens is a symmetrical double objective: two identical cemented triplets face each other across a central stop.
Goerz issued it as the Doppel-Anastigmat in two series, Series III at 1:7.7 as a universal lens and Series IV at
1:11 for reproduction work (p. 377), and Ross & Co. in London took a manufacturing licence (pp. 377–378). The design
was later sold by Goerz under the name Dagor.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** All eight radius magnitudes as four symmetric pairs (r1 = r8 = 19.1, r2 = r7 = 22.7,
r3 = r6 = 8.3, r4 = r5 = 20.5), the three glass thicknesses of each cell (3.1, 0.8, 1.9), the cell-to-stop
distances b1 = b2 = 2.3, the three nD values, the reduction to fD = 100 mm, the computation aperture 1:8.0 and the
half-field ω = 36°. Page 375 adds the glass names and von Rohr's dispersion figures ν̄. Page 376 states the results
of his own ray trace: spherical correction complete for an aperture of about 1:7.8, a zonal residual of not quite
−1.0 mm, astigmatism strictly corrected at ω = 30° and only a very small departure from a flat field.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Nothing. Unlike several other tables in the chapter, this one marks
no thickness or index as assumed. It is a reduction of the patent example to f = 100, rounded to 0.1 mm; the patent
comparison below shows the rounding.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 138 and from von Rohr's description of the cell: a high-index biconvex lens outside,
  a medium-index negative middle lens and a low-index collective meniscus next to the stop. The rear cell mirrors the
  front one.
- The last gap, 91.289 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints no back focus.
- Clear radii of 6.2 mm on every surface, a heuristic value kept from the package. The stop radius (5.367 mm) is
  derived from 1:8.
- The f-stop list 8–11–16–22, the 36° field limit (the printed ω) and the element focal lengths, computed thick in
  air.

**Printed against modelled.** The printed table traces to an EFL of 98.44 mm, 1.56 % short of the stated 100 mm.
That residual is rounding, not a transcription error: the system is unusually sensitive to its radii
(0.1 mm on r4 moves the EFL by about 1.8 %, on r3 by 1.2 %), and the patent's own values divided by 2.4 trace to
100.1 mm. The f-number is f/8 at the model's own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 6.153 mm. The longitudinal
spherical aberration at the f/8 pupil is −0.605 mm at the rim, −1.074 mm at 0.85, −1.008 mm at 0.7 and −0.634 mm
at 0.5 of the pupil; the marginal ray crosses zero near 1:7.45. Von Rohr reports zero near 1:7.8 and a zone just
under −1.0 mm, and the unrounded patent values reproduce that (zero near 1:8.0, zone about −0.9 mm), so the shift is
again the 0.1 mm rounding. The model traces the chief ray out to the printed 36°.

## Optical Architecture

Each cell is one of the two cemented three-lens types von Höegh describes. In the type adopted for the objective,
the medium-index middle lens has negative power and is enclosed by a weakly refracting collective meniscus on one
side and a high-index biconvex lens on the other. Von Rohr classifies the cell as form 2aα in his scheme of
Rudolph's anastigmatic principle (p. 375). The patent claim he quotes rests on the graded refractive indices of the
three glasses in each separately corrected cell.

The construction can be read as two achromats sharing the middle lens. Von Rohr notes that if the cell is divided by
a suitably curved surface into two achromatized parts, the "old achromat" next to the stop (low-index crown L3 with
the higher-index flint) has a longer positive focal length than the "new achromat" further out (high-index crown L1
with the lower-index flint). The two cemented junctions carry the opposite roles that Rudolph's principle needs:

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 19.1 | air → 1.61310 | +0.0321 | outer collective face |
| r2 = −22.7 | 1.61310 → 1.56804 | +0.0020 | weak collective junction, new achromat |
| r3 = 8.3 | 1.56804 → 1.51497 | −0.0064 | strong dispersive junction, old achromat |
| r4 = 20.5 | 1.51497 → air | −0.0251 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

Each cell has a focal length of +171.8 mm at the model's scale, about 1.75 times that of the pair, and the two
equal cells sit 4.6 mm apart. The Petzval sum of the whole objective is 0.00282 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about
354 mm or 3.6 f: a low enough sum for a nearly flat field once astigmatism is corrected, which is the result von
Rohr reports at 30°. The total length from first to last vertex is 16.2 mm.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Biconvex Positive, outer collective

nD = 1.61310 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 44.7. Glass: heavy barium silicate crown, unmatched. f = +17.4 mm.

The high-index outer lens carries most of the cell's positive power at its strongly curved outer face (r1 = 19.1).
Its rear face meets L2 in a weak collective junction, so with the front part of L2 it forms the new achromat. The
high index allows the strong outer curvature with a lower Petzval contribution than an ordinary crown would give.
L6 is its mirror image in the rear cell.

### L2 / L5 — Biconcave Negative, middle lens

nD = 1.56804 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 41.2. Glass: baryta light flint, unmatched. f = −10.6 mm.

The middle lens is the flint partner of both outer elements. Its index lies between the two crowns, so its front
junction with L1 is weakly collective and its rear junction with L3 is strongly dispersive. That rear junction,
r3 = 8.3 mm, is the most strongly curved surface in the system and, as the one strongly dispersive cemented surface
of the cell, its main lever on spherical aberration and astigmatism. The system's sensitivity to this radius shows
it: ±0.1 mm on r3 moves the focal length by 1.2 % and the aperture of full spherical correction from about 1:7.4 to
between 1:6.7 and 1:8.4.
L5 mirrors it.

### L3 / L4 — Positive Meniscus, inner collective

nD = 1.51497 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 44.2. Glass: soft silicate crown, unmatched. f = +25.7 mm.

The low-index meniscus sits next to the stop, convex toward L2 and concave toward the stop. With the rear part of L2
it forms the old achromat. Its concave inner face (r4 = 20.5) is the main negative contribution to the Petzval sum.
Because its convex face is so strongly curved, the printed radii bring the lens to a knife edge at a radius of about
6.29 mm, which fixes the largest clear radius the inner surfaces can have. L4 mirrors it in the rear cell.

## Glass Selection

| Von Rohr's name | Symbol | nD | ν̄ | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schweres Barium-Silikat-Crown (heavy barium silicate crown) | H | 1.61310 | 44.7 | L1, L6 | outer high-index collective |
| Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | M | 1.56804 | 41.2 | L2, L5 | medium-index negative middle lens |
| Weiches Silikat-Crown (soft silicate crown) | N | 1.51497 | 44.2 | L3, L4 | low-index collective meniscus |

Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), the dispersion figure defined by the column heads of his glass table on
p. 339. It measures dispersion toward the photographically active blue (G′ line) rather than across the F–C
interval, so it is not an Abbe number and cannot be converted to νd. The patent prints nG′ beside each nD (1.62683,
1.58182 and 1.52663 for H, M and N), and those pairs give back von Rohr's three ν̄ values. The model therefore keeps
nD only, with no Abbe number and no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

The ν̄ ordering, ν̄H > ν̄M < ν̄N, is what lets the medium-index flint serve both crowns. The heavy barium crown pairs
with a lower-index flint in the new achromat and the soft crown with a higher-index flint in the old achromat.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

Von Rohr computes the objective for 1:8.0. The model sets the iris from that aperture: the entrance-pupil radius is
6.153 mm at the model's EFL and the physical stop radius 5.367 mm. The f/8 axial beam reaches 6.15 mm at r1 and
5.77 mm at r8, inside the 6.2 mm rims. Goerz's Series III production lens was rated 1:7.7 (p. 377).

The printed half-field is ω = 36°, a 72° field. The 6.2 mm rims pass the chief ray at 36°, with chief heights of
4.93 mm at r1 and r8, and the paraxial rim limit is 40.9°. The oblique beams are heavily vignetted: a meridional
trace computed for this model passes about 66 % of the stop diameter at 20°, 42 % at 30° and 25 % at 36°. The field
is therefore limited to the printed 36° without widening any rim.

The 6.2 mm rims come from the package as an estimate, not from the drawing. Fig. 138 is drawn to scale for the
printed axial distances: its vertex spacings, scaled from the 16.2 mm total length, agree with the table to within
about 0.2 mm. It draws the outer lenses L1, L2, L5 and L6 to about 7.6 mm, with L1 tapering almost to an edge at the
rim, and the inner menisci ending in a knife edge below the outer rim. The patent's free lens opening of 36 mm at
f = 240 mm, about 7.5 mm on the f = 100 scale, agrees with the drawing. Outer rims of that size would roughly double
the transmitted meridional beam at 36°. The inner surfaces cannot exceed about 6.29 mm with the printed radii.

## Design Heritage and Context

The patent itself is context here, not the source of the numbers in the file. According to the Google Patents text
of DE 74437 C, it prints one numerical example: the cell radii 45.835, 54.324, 19.853 and 49.088 mm, glass
thicknesses 7.334, 1.833 and 4.584 mm, an air space of 11.00 mm between the cells, nD and nG′ for each glass,
an equivalent focal length of 240 mm with a largest effective aperture of 30 mm (about 1:8), and the foci of
central, zonal and marginal axial rays in two colours. Divided by 2.4 and rounded to 0.1 mm, these give von Rohr's
table, except r2 = r7: the patent value reduces to 22.64 mm, where von Rohr prints 22.7. The difference changes the
EFL by about 0.1 % and is kept as printed. The paraxial back focus of the patent example, 223.26 mm, agrees with the
patent's central-ray focus of 223.275 mm. The patent text also states that each cell is corrected on its own and
can be used alone, at about 1.75 times the focal length of the pair.

Von Rohr treats the priority question with Paul Rudolph at length (pp. 376–377). Rudolph's Zeiss anastigmatic
single lens of the same type 2aα dates from the end of 1891 and was made as Series VI at 1:14.5. His English
application of 22 April 1893 reached the British patent office before Goerz's complete specification of 2 June
1893, and before the German Doppel-Anastigmat application was laid open on 5 May. Von Rohr concludes that Rudolph
(end of 1891) and von Höegh (end of 1892) invented the type independently. He contrasts their aims: Rudolph sought
the best correction of the single cell, von Höegh the best performance of the doublet. He also notes that von Höegh
later published astigmatic curves for an improved Doppel-Anastigmat, but without data he could use.

The symmetrical arrangement removes coma, distortion and lateral colour at unit magnification and keeps them small
at infinity, so the design work lies in the spherical, chromatic and astigmatic correction of the single cell, the
three corrections the patent title names. The same separate correction is what makes each cell usable on its own
as a longer single lens. Goerz's
energetic promotion of the new type led in the summer of 1893 to a public dispute over comparison methods with the
Zeiss anastigmat doublets, fought in the Photographische Mittheilungen and The British Journal of Photography
(p. 377).
