# Voigtländer Petzval Landscape, First Form f/8.1 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 101

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 250, Fig. 101
**Data after:** Fr. Voigtländer for the radii and the lens spacing (*Akademiker Professor Dr. Joseph Petzval in Wien beleuchtet vom Optiker Voigtländer in Braunschweig*, 1859, pp. 98 and 103) and J. M. Eder for the glass kinds (*Die photographischen Objektive*, 1891, p. 115)
**Designer:** Joseph Petzval
**Manufacturer:** Voigtländer (Fr. Voigtländer in Vienna made the trial lenses of 1840; von Rohr reports that he later made the landscape lens from the 1840 data in Braunschweig as the Orthoskop)
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1840

Fig. 101 is captioned "J. Petzval: Landschaftsobjektiv. 1. Form." Von Rohr discusses it in his section on Petzval
and his time (pp. 248–262). In 1839, as it seems, A. von Ettingshausen asked his Vienna colleague Joseph Petzval to
compute a new and faster objective intended above all for landscapes. By the middle of 1840 Petzval had computed three
lens systems, numbered 1, 2 and 3 on a sheet he handed to the optician Fr. Voigtländer. Fig. 99, after Voigtländer,
shows the arrangement: lenses 1 and 2 form the landscape objective, lenses 1 and 3 the portrait objective (Fig. 100).
This first form is the combination 1 + 2, Petzval's front lens followed by a negative rear pair.

Voigtländer made the lenses quickly enough for trial photographs in the autumn of 1840 (p. 251). Only the portrait
combination passed the practical test made by An. Martin. The landscape combination, of 352 mm (13 in) equivalent
focal length and used without any stop at its full opening of 1:8, was set aside: it gave images too small for
landscape work and no latitude in focusing, and Petzval himself was dissatisfied with it. Von Rohr adds that lens 2
appeared on the sheet with two different radius combinations; he does not say which one Fig. 101 reproduces.

The second form, Fig. 103 on p. 258, is the lens Petzval presented in Vienna in 1856 and the Vienna optician
C. Dietzler made; it differs from this one only in the orientation of the negative lens. Voigtländer, who had moved
to Braunschweig in 1849, then began to make the landscape lens "on the basis of the data of 1840" under the name
Orthoskop, which opened the Orthoskop dispute of 1857/58 (pp. 259–261). The maker credit rests on these two
statements. Von Rohr never found the final production data of either lens, so the file is the computed design of
1840, not a measured Orthoskop.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The seven radius magnitudes (22.5, 17.6, 185.7, 53.5, 31.5, 78.2, 22.8 mm), the glass
thicknesses 2.5, 0.6, 0.6 and 1.2 mm, the distances b1 = 3.7 (front lens to stop), b2 = 1.2 (stop to L3) and
b3 = 1.6 mm (between the rear lenses), nD = 1.517 for L1 and L3 and 1.575 for L2 and L4, the reduction to fD = 100 mm
and the computation aperture 1:8.1. He prints no field angle. On p. 260 he reports the result of his ray trace: the
axial curves 101a on Tafel II, where the intercept and focal-length deviations run alike but are both considerably
undercorrected for yellow light, and oblique pencils with extraordinarily large errors even with the stop as near the
rear lens as possible, the meridional intercepts always much longer than the sagittal ones. Because of these defects
he did not plot the oblique results.

**Assumed by von Rohr.** All seven thicknesses and distances, the stop position included, carry his footnote
"Willkürlich angenommen" (arbitrarily assumed). Only the radii and the glass indices come from his sources.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 101 and from the natural-size drawing No. 101 on Tafel II: a biconvex crown cemented
  to a nearly plano-concave flint, a biconcave crown, and a flint meniscus concave toward the object. The signs are
  checked numerically: a negative r3 would give f = 63 mm instead of 100, and the signed table reproduces curves 101a.
- The last gap, 87.945 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii of 6.5 mm on every surface, a heuristic value, and the stop radius of 5.458 mm derived from 1:8.1.
- The f-stop list 8.1–11–16–22–32, the 30° field limit, and the element focal lengths, computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The table traces to an EFL of 99.106 mm, 0.89 % short of 100. No single value moved
within its last-digit rounding reaches 100 (r1 would need 22.59, r7 −22.89), but the radii together, each moved by at
most 0.05 mm, span EFLs up to 100.5 mm, so the residual lies within the rounding of the table. The model is f/8.1 at
its own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 6.118 mm. Curves 101a, read from Tafel II to about ±0.02 mm, against the
model's exact trace:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.17 (1:8.1) | −1.07 | −1.11 | −1.24 | −1.28 |

The model follows von Rohr's curves within 0.05 mm. The chief ray passes to about 45°; the 30° limit is this model's
choice and is explained below.

## Optical Architecture

The objective has four elements in three air-separated groups, mounted as two cells. A positive cemented front lens
(f = +59.3 mm) is followed, 4.9 mm behind, by an air-spaced negative pair (f = −146.9 mm), with the stop between them,
1.2 mm in front of the rear pair. This is the positive–negative arrangement of a Galilean telescope, in keeping with
von Rohr's account that Petzval regarded the rear lenses of his set as eyepieces to the front objective (p. 249). The
rear principal plane lies 0.24 mm behind the first vertex, and the distance from the first vertex to the image,
99.35 mm, almost equals the focal length.

The negative rear pair is what makes the design a field corrector rather than a plain achromat. The Petzval sum of
the front lens alone is 0.0118 mm⁻¹; the rear pair contributes −0.0058 mm⁻¹ and halves it, leaving 0.0060 mm⁻¹, a
Petzval radius of 168 mm or 1.7 times the focal length.

The front lens is the portrait objective's front lens. The radii of Fig. 100, 52.9, 41.4 and 436.2 mm, divided by
2.351 give this table's 22.5, 17.6 and 185.7 mm (ratios 2.351, 2.352 and 2.349), and von Rohr's assumed thicknesses
5.8 and 1.5 mm scale to the 2.5 and 0.6 mm used here. The ratio agrees with the focal lengths of the two 1840 lenses,
352 and 149 mm (2.36), as the shared front lens of Petzval's set requires.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Biconvex Positive, front crown

nD = 1.517 (sodium D), no dispersion published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = +19.5 mm.

The collective member of the system. Its strongly curved rear face (r2 = −17.6 mm) is cemented to L2. With von Rohr's
assumed centre thickness of 2.5 mm the lens runs out to a knife edge at a radius of 6.91 mm, which bounds the clear
aperture of the front cell; at the model's 6.5 mm rim the edge is 0.30 mm thick.

### L2 — Biconcave Negative, front flint

nD = 1.575 (sodium D), no dispersion published. Glass: flint, unmatched. f = −27.9 mm.

The negative partner of the front achromat. Its rear face (r3 = +185.7 mm) is nearly flat. The cemented junction r2
has a small index step (0.058) and a weak diverging power of −0.0033 mm⁻¹; the front lens as a whole has a focal length
of +59.3 mm.

### L3 — Biconcave Negative, rear crown

nD = 1.517 (sodium D), no dispersion published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = −38.3 mm.

The strong negative member of the rear pair, 1.2 mm behind the stop. Its two concave faces (r4 = −53.5, r5 = +31.5 mm)
carry most of the negative contribution to the Petzval sum. In the second form of Fig. 103 this lens is turned round,
r4 = −31.5 and r5 = +53.5 mm, with every other value unchanged.

### L4 — Positive Meniscus, rear flint

nD = 1.575 (sodium D), no dispersion published. Glass: flint, unmatched. f = +55.5 mm.

A meniscus concave toward the object (r6 = −78.2, r7 = −22.8 mm), 1.6 mm behind L3. It is the positive partner of the
negative rear pair, which together has a focal length of −146.9 mm.

## Glass Selection

Both glasses are the ones of the portrait objective: a crown of nD = 1.517 and a flint of nD = 1.575. The hatching of
drawing No. 101 on Tafel II, descending for old crown and rising for flint, marks L1 and L3 as crown and L2 and L4 as
flint. Von Rohr cites Eder for the glass kinds and prints nD only; for these glasses he gives no dispersion figure.
His barred ν̄ for other glasses, defined by the column heads of his glass table on p. 339 as (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), is a
photographic blue figure rather than an Abbe number, and would not supply νd even if it were printed here. Petzval
took his optical constants from Voigtländer, determined by Fraunhofer's method without regard to the actinic rays
(p. 249), and the first objectives proved chromatically overcorrected (p. 251).

The front lens pairs a positive crown with a negative flint, the usual achromat. The rear pair inverts that order, a
negative crown with a positive flint, which is the arrangement a negative achromat needs: the less dispersive glass
carries the stronger, negative power. With no dispersion in the source this remains a reading of the glass roles,
not a computed colour correction. The model keeps nD only, with no Abbe number and no modern catalog glass, and is
therefore monochromatic.

## Aperture, Field and Focus

Von Rohr computes the lens for 1:8.1, close to the 1:8 at which the trial lens of 1840 was used without a stop. The
model sets the iris from 1:8.1: the stop radius is 5.458 mm, and the axial beam reaches 6.12 mm at r1, inside the
6.5 mm rims.

Von Rohr prints no field angle for this form. The model's 30° limit is chosen to match the ω = 30° he used for the
second form, Fig. 103, which has the same spacings and stop position. At 30° the chief ray passes with heights of
3.71, 2.88 and 2.55 mm on the front lens, and the oblique beam is cut by up to 44–49 % on one side at r1 and r2; the
chief ray itself is not blocked until about 45°.

A Coddington trace of this model along the chief ray, with the table's stop, reproduces von Rohr's verdict on the
oblique pencils. The sagittal image stays within 0.3 mm of the Gaussian plane out to 20°, while the meridional image
recedes by 1.3 mm at 10°, 4.0 mm at 15°, 10.7 mm at 20° and 29.5 mm at 25°.

The 6.5 mm rims are an estimate. Both drawings are to scale: the natural-size drawing on Tafel II and Fig. 101,
scaled by the horizontal lines drawn in front of the lens at the 1:8.1 entrance-pupil radius, show all four elements
square-edged at about 6.9 mm, where L1 reaches its knife edge. The drawn stop opening is about 6 mm against the
model's 5.458 mm. The file is focused at infinity only; von Rohr computes the lens for a distant object, and no
focusing movement is modelled.

## Design Heritage and Context

This landscape combination and the portrait objective were the two products of Petzval's set of three lenses. Von
Rohr stresses that the portrait objective was the first camera objective computed in advance rather than found by
trial (p. 254). He holds Petzval's set idea to be independent of Ch. Chevalier's in all probability, though Chevalier
published the principle first (pp. 248–249).

The patent named in this connection is context, not the source of the numbers. Von Rohr cites a patent bearing
Petzval's signature, communicated by Voigtländer on p. 103 of his 1859 pamphlet, for the landscape lens of 1856. A
patent attorney's search in Vienna, reported on 29 December 1898, could not find it; von Rohr later learned that it
had been granted to C. Dietzler on 28 December 1857 and extended to its sixth year. It carries no number in his text;
he knew it only as Voigtländer printed it, without the drawing it mentions, and hoped that someone would publish its
full wording. He takes the lens spacing of Fig. 101 from the same page of Voigtländer's pamphlet, yet marks every
distance as assumed.

For the 1856 lens Petzval provided a variable lens separation, not to change the focal length but to let the
photographer introduce any desired amount of spherical aberration, and he would have liked to call it the
photographic Dialyt. Von Rohr's trace of the second form gave smaller axial errors and much improved but still
unfavourable oblique pencils, with an image surface clearly convex toward the objective, contrary to Petzval's own
description; he concluded that he did not hold the final data. On the Orthoskop dispute he could reach no verdict
beyond non liquet.
