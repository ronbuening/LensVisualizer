# Steinheil Periskop f/40 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 109

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 288, Fig. 109
**Data after:** J. M. Eder, *Die photographischen Objektive, ihre Eigenschaften und Prüfung* (Halle: W. Knapp, 1891), p. 146 (von Rohr's "Quelle: J. M. Eder 1. 146")
**Designer:** Adolph Steinheil (Hugo Adolph Steinheil, 1832–1893), as the caption credits him; the English patent was a communication from his father, Carl August Steinheil
**Manufacturer:** Optisch-astronomische Werkstätte C. A. Steinheil, Munich (C. A. Steinheil Söhne from 1865); made under licence by Voigtländer from 1865
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1865

Fig. 109 is captioned "A. Steinheil: Periskop". Von Rohr describes the lens on p. 288 as the first photographic
objective of the Optisch-astronomische Werkstätte, put on the market in 1865: a symmetrical objective with a central
stop and no chromatic correction, made of simple crown-glass menisci. He reduces Eder's data to fD = 100 mm, gives
the relative aperture 1:40 and computes the lens for a half-field ω = 46°. Tafel III, among the wide-angle
objectives, redraws it at natural size as No. 109 with the astigmatism curve 109b; the plate has no spherical
aberration curve for this lens.

The design date follows from the text and the bibliography: the lens appeared in 1865, Voigtländer took a
manufacturing licence the same year, and the English patent (E.P. 2937 of 15 November 1865) was taken through
W. Bünger (bibliography p. 428). Von Rohr credits the computation of radii, thickness and stop distance as the
novelty of the lens; the type itself he traces back to G. S. Cundell (1844) and Th. Ross.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The radius magnitudes as symmetric pairs (r1 = r4 = 17.5, r2 = r3 = 20.8), the thickness
d1 = d2 = 1.3, the lens-to-stop distances b1 = b2 = 6.3, nD = 1.5233 for both lenses, the reduction to fD = 100 mm,
the aperture 1:40 and the half-field ω = 46°. The text adds the purpose of the computation (an image as flat as
possible and coincident principal points), the lens diameter chosen to allow a 90° field, the need to shorten the
camera by about f/40 after visual focusing, and strong astigmatism hidden by the small stop.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Nothing. The caption marks no value as assumed. By his notes on the
plates he traced each lens with the data of the named source and only then reduced data and results to f = 100.
Eder's table gives the same lens to two decimals: radii 17.53 and 20.76, thickness 1.26, lens separation 12.56
("or 8.29"), lens diameter 12.56, two indices (1.5233 and 1.5360) and an optical and a chemical focus (100.00 and
97.54). Von Rohr's table is that data rounded to one decimal, with the separation halved about the stop.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 109, Tafel III and the text: both menisci are convex outward and concave toward the
  central stop, so r1 and r2 are positive and r3 and r4 negative.
- The last gap, 90.948 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii of 6.28 mm on all four surfaces. The value is half of Eder's printed lens diameter of 12.56 mm, which
  belongs to his more precise data rather than to von Rohr's rounded table; both drawings measure the lenses at
  6.1–6.4 mm, so the figure supports it (see Aperture, Field and Rims). The stop radius, 1.160 mm, is derived from
  1:40.
- The f-stop list 40–45–56–64, the 42° field limit and the element focal lengths, computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The rounded table traces to an EFL of 98.697 mm, 1.30 % short of 100 mm. The residual
lies within the rounding of the radii: the lens power rests on the small difference between the curvatures of each
meniscus, so 17.54 instead of 17.5 for r1 alone gives 99.94 mm, and Eder's two-decimal data give 100.75 mm. The
model is f/40 at its own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 1.234 mm. The traced field is 42° against the printed
46°: the chief ray clears the 6.28 mm rims only up to 42.3°.

## Optical Architecture

The Periskop is a holosymmetric pair of identical positive menisci, both of one crown glass, standing 12.6 mm apart
with the stop midway. Each meniscus has a focal length of +185.7 mm, about 1.9 times that of the pair. The vertex
length is 15.2 mm and the total track 106.148 mm. The principal planes lie 0.15 mm on either side of the stop and
are crossed by 0.30 mm, close to the coincident principal points that von Rohr names as an aim of the computation.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 17.5 | air → 1.5233 | +0.0299 | outer collective face |
| r2 = 20.8 | 1.5233 → air | −0.0252 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The two faces of each lens nearly cancel, so each meniscus is weak and bent strongly outward around the stop. The
symmetry removes coma and distortion at unit magnification and keeps them small for distant objects: the chief ray
at 42° lands 89.136 mm from the axis against f·tan ω = 88.867 mm, a pincushion of 0.30 %. The lens has no
chromatic correction; von Rohr notes that its aperture was kept small for that reason and that the strong stopping
down also hid its astigmatism.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Positive Meniscus, convex toward the object

nD = 1.5233 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: crown, unmatched. f = +185.7 mm.

The front meniscus is 1.3 mm thick on the axis and thins to 1.11 mm at the 6.28 mm rim. Its outer face r1 carries
the power; the inner face r2, concave toward the stop, takes back about 84 % of it. The strong outward bending, with
the stop 6.3 mm behind the lens, is what flattens the image: the patent states that the lenses must be curved enough
for the image to fall in a plane normal to the axis.

### L2 — Positive Meniscus, convex toward the image

nD = 1.5233 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: crown, unmatched. f = +185.7 mm.

L2 repeats L1 reversed behind the stop. The two lenses are identical, which is what makes the lens holosymmetric and
lets each half correct the other's coma and distortion.

## Glass Selection

Both lenses are of one crown glass, nD = 1.5233. Von Rohr's table names no glass type; his text calls the lenses
simple crown-glass menisci, and the hatching of both lenses on Tafel III is the one his plate legend assigns to old
low-index crown. The English patent describes them only as two lenses of the same kind of glass and, in the
complete specification, as made of mirror glass.

Von Rohr prints no dispersion. Eder prints two indices, nv = 1.5233 and no = 1.5360, beside an optical focus
fo = 100.00 and a chemical focus fv = 97.54. Traced through Eder's data, 1.5233 gives the longer, optical focus and
1.5360 a focus 2.4 % shorter, the f/40 shortening of the camera that von Rohr describes; the subscripts of the two
indices therefore appear interchanged, and von Rohr takes 1.5233 as nD. The second index belongs to unspecified
actinic light, not to a Fraunhofer line, so it gives no Abbe number. The model keeps nD only, with no νd and no
modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic; the chemical focus shift is not modelled.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The aperture is 1:40. The model's stop radius is 1.160 mm; both drawings show the stop opening at 1.18 mm and the
incident axial beam 1.25–1.33 mm from the axis, the 1.25 mm f/40 pupil at f = 100. C. A. Steinheil's own figures
for a Periskop made in mid-1865 (f = 379 mm, lens diameter 42.9 mm, stop diameter 9.03 mm, p. 288) scale to a stop
radius of 1.19 mm and a lens radius of 5.66 mm at f = 100; that specimen is a different lens and is not used here.

Von Rohr computes the oblique pencils to ω = 46° and reports C. A. Steinheil's statement that the lens diameter was
chosen to allow a 90° field. With the 6.28 mm rims the chief ray of the model meets r1 and r4 at 6.22 mm at 42° and
passes up to 42.3°; at 46° it reaches 6.93 mm. Fig. 109 and Tafel III, both drawn at natural size, show the lenses
6.1–6.4 mm from the axis, so raising the rims to 6.93 mm would contradict the drawings. The rims stay at 6.28 mm and
the trace field is capped at 42°. Because the lens is symmetric, the upper half of an oblique beam is clipped by the
front lens and the lower half by the rear lens, and both reach the chief ray together: the meridional beam passes
whole up to about 36.6°, then 97 % at 37°, 63 % at 39°, 45 % at 40°, 27 % at 41°, 7 % at 42° and nothing from 42.4°.
Eder's own data, with the same rims, would pass the chief ray to about 42.6°.

The spherical aberration is undercorrected: −0.486 mm at the rim of the f/40 pupil, −0.351 mm at 0.85, −0.238 mm at
0.7 and −0.121 mm at 0.5 of the pupil, well inside the ±1.9 mm diffraction depth of focus at f/40.

## Oblique Pencils

Curve 109b on Tafel III gives, from the paraxial focus, the sagittal (solid) and meridional (dashed) image positions
at five computed field angles. A Coddington trace along the exact chief ray of the model gives:

| ω (read on the plate) | Curve 109b, sagittal / meridional (mm) | Model (mm) | Eder's data, reduced to f = 100 (mm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10.0° | −0.74 / −0.27 | −0.71 / −0.26 | −0.73 / −0.29 |
| 18.8° | −2.52 / −0.63 | −2.42 / −0.52 | −2.48 / −0.62 |
| 28.0° | −5.31 / +0.36 | −5.06 / +0.61 | −5.19 / +0.38 |
| 37.2° | −8.52 / +6.38 | −8.18 / +6.73 | −8.40 / +6.33 |
| 46.3° | −11.75 / off scale | −11.28 / +29.4 | −11.60 / +28.6 |

Eder's two-decimal data reproduce the curve within about 0.15 mm, which confirms that von Rohr traced them with the
12.56 mm separation (the alternative 8.29 mm would put the sagittal image 13.6 mm short at 42°). The rounded table
departs by up to 0.5 mm. In the model the meridional image crosses the focal plane near 25°, while the sagittal
image falls ever further short; the mean of the two stays within about 2.2 mm of the plane up to 38°. That is the
flatness "in a figurative sense" with strong astigmatism that von Rohr describes; the astigmatic difference is
14.6 mm at 37°. The Petzval sum is 0.00623 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 160 mm or 1.6 f.

## Design Heritage and Context

According to von Rohr (pp. 286–288), C. A. Steinheil had long planned photographic lenses for his Munich workshop,
and the Periskop was its first. The Aplanat of 1866, computed with the trigonometric formulas for oblique rays that
L. Seidel supplied (pp. 290, 294), followed and kept the Periskop's holosymmetric form. In 1865 the workshop passed
to his sons as C. A. Steinheil Söhne. The Periskop drew much attention, rivalled the globe lenses in sharpness, and
led to a dispute with E. Busch over his Pantoskop (p. 289). Von Rohr sees the type itself in G. S. Cundell's lens of
1844 and in earlier work by Th. Ross. Eder adds that the Periskop had gone out of use and drew attention again
around 1890 for instantaneous wide-angle work with detective cameras, where one fixed setting absorbs its focus
difference, and that manufacture had resumed in America and at Steinheil's in Munich.

The English patent 2937 of 1865, a communication from C. A. Steinheil, prints a table of radius ratios for image
angles of 45°, 70° and 90° (r1 : r2 = 1 : −1.1407, −1.1529, −1.1828) and two worked examples in lines. The 90°
example (focus 78, radii 13.95 and 16.50, thickness 1.00, separation 6.60, lens diameter 10, stop 2) scales at
f = 100 to radii 17.88 and 21.15, thickness 1.28, separation 8.46, lens radius 6.41 and an f/39 stop. Its radius
ratio, 1.183, is close to Eder's 1.184, but its separation is much smaller than the 12.56 of Eder and von Rohr. The
patent is context, not the source of these numbers.
