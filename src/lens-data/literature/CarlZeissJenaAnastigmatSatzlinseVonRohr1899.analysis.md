# Carl Zeiss Jena Anastigmat-Satzlinse f/14.4 (von Rohr 1899, Fig. 137)

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 374, Fig. 137
**Data after:** P. Rudolph (caption "Quelle: P. Rudolph. 1."; read here as entry 6, British patent 4692 of 1893, an inference explained below)
**Designer:** Paul Rudolph
**Manufacturer:** Carl Zeiss Jena
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** end of 1891

Von Rohr captions Fig. 137 "P. Rudolph: Anastigmat-Satzlinse", reduces the table to fD = 100 mm and states that it
was computed through for 1:14.4 and ω = 34°. The figure also refers to Plate IV of the book, which was not consulted
for this model.

The caption's source line reads "Quelle: P. Rudolph. 1." In von Rohr's bibliography (p. 424), Rudolph entry 1 is the
German patent 56109 of 3 April 1890 on the Anastigmat doublet, cited only on pp. 360–362; it predates this design by
more than a year. Entry 6 is the British patent 4692 of 22 April 1893, "auf Ser. VI", cited on pp. 373/374 and 377.
The running text on p. 373 introduces these single lenses with the citation "(6.)", and the table's footnote, "misprint
in the B. J. corrected", points to the reprint in the *British Journal of Photography* (1893, No. 1725) that the
bibliography lists under entry 6. The caption's "1" is therefore read here as a misprint for 6. This is an inference
from the book's own cross-references; the patent itself was not retrieved.

Von Rohr's account (pp. 373–379) gives the history. Rudolph computed two cemented single lenses that the British patent
office later published; the earlier of them, described second in the patent, is the type of Fig. 137. Rudolph found it
at the end of 1891 but at first gave priority to the new f/4.5, f/6.3 and f/9 doublets, which kept the Jena works fully
occupied. When news of Goerz's Doppel-Anastigmat application reached him, he filed the single lenses abroad; the German
application was refused, but a right of prior use remained, and the 1891 construction was made as Series VI at the
relative aperture 1:14.5. Von Rohr concludes that the astigmatically corrected single lens of this type was invented
independently by Rudolph (end of 1891) and E. von Höegh (end of 1892), and that Rudolph's construction data were the
first to be printed. The Series VI single lens and the Series VIa sets built from two of them were issued in 1893 and
replaced by a new series in spring 1895.

The relationship between this computed example and a production Series VI lens is lineage, as von Rohr describes it,
not a measured specimen: the table is computed for 1:14.4, while the catalog rating he quotes is 1:14.5.

## Reconstruction status

**Printed by von Rohr (pp. 373–374).** The four radii as magnitudes (14.2, 5.5, 20.7, 12.6), the thicknesses
d1 = 1.7, d2 = 0.5 and d3 = 1.4, the stop distance b1 = 1.0 in front of the first surface, the three indices
nD 1.52246, 1.56724 and 1.61120, the reduced focal length fD = 100 mm, the aperture 1:14.4 and the field angle ω = 34°.
Page 373 names the glasses and gives his barred ν̄ for each. The footnote marks the L2 index as corrected from a misprint
in the British Journal; the model uses von Rohr's corrected value. The text adds two performance statements: a zonal
residual of about −1.1 mm at about 1:12.5, the aperture at which the margin would be corrected, and an astigmatic
difference of only about 4.8 mm at 34°, where the field is flattened "in the transferred sense".

The focal-length residual is a rounding effect of the one-decimal radii: a change of 0.05 mm in r4 alone moves the
focal length by 2.1 mm, so r4 = 12.603 would give exactly 100. The spherical-aberration and astigmatism figures are
computed for this model (an exact meridional trace on axis, and a Coddington trace along the real chief ray, measured
along the axis from the paraxial image plane). The spherical figures differ from von Rohr's by amounts the rounding
allows: a change of 0.05 mm in r2 alone moves the zonal residual by about 0.3 mm and the aperture of corrected margin
between about f/12.4 and f/14.1. The astigmatic difference of 5.2 mm is close to his 4.8 mm, and the sagittal and
tangential image surfaces at 34° lie on opposite sides of the image plane with their mean 0.34 mm beyond it, which is
the flattening von Rohr describes.

## Optical Architecture

The lens is a single cemented group of three elements behind a front stop: a positive meniscus, a thin biconcave
lens and a biconvex lens, the group as a whole concave toward the stop and convex toward the image. With the computed
thick-lens focal lengths of +16.1, −7.6 and +13.0 mm, the three members are individually six to thirteen times
stronger than the whole lens; nearly all of their power cancels, and the cemented junctions carry the correction. The
vertex length is 3.6 mm, the stop sits 1.0 mm in front of the first surface, and the back focus is 109.4 mm.

The two junctions have index steps of nearly equal size but opposite pairing. At r2 the low-index crown L1 meets the
higher-index flint L2, the classic order of an achromat in the old glasses, where the positive lens has the lower
index. At r3 the flint L2 meets the heaviest baryta crown L3, the order made possible by the Jena baryta glasses, where
the positive lens has the higher index. Read this way, one old and one new achromat share the middle lens: the
"opposed gradation of the refractive indices" that von Rohr names as the principle of Rudolph's anastigmat doublet,
here inside one cemented lens. Von Rohr files the type under "2aα" in his scheme of single lenses derived from the
anastigmat doublet (p. 373).

The paraxial power is concentrated in the two outer faces. The concave front face contributes −0.037 mm⁻¹ and the
convex rear face +0.049 mm⁻¹; the strongly curved junction r2 adds only −0.008 mm⁻¹ because its index step is 0.045,
and r3 adds +0.002 mm⁻¹. The Petzval sum computed from the printed data is 0.0034 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about
297 mm or 3 f; that sum is about half that of a thin single crown lens of the same focal length. The concave front
face is the main reason: its negative Petzval contribution offsets most of the rear face's.

Von Rohr's verdict on p. 374 is that this was the first landscape lens of a new type since Thomas Grubb's that claims
spherical correction in the strict sense, with the sine condition also fulfilled, while every other single lens of
the time remained more or less undercorrected. The one earlier attempt he recalls is M. Mittenzwei's effort of 1883
to bring out a spherically corrected single lens (p. 342).

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Positive Meniscus, concave to the stop

nD = 1.52246 (sodium D); von Rohr's ν̄ = 46.4 (not an Abbe number). Glass: Unmatched (ordinary silicate crown).
f = +16.1 mm (computed).

The front member and the lowest-index glass. Its front face (r = −14.2) is the strongest negative surface in the
lens and the main source of its low Petzval sum; its rear face is the deeply curved junction r2 (r = −5.5), whose
sag at the 4.0 mm rim is about 1.7 mm, the full axial thickness of L1. The edge thickness at the rim is 0.55 mm.

### L2 — Biconcave Negative

nD = 1.56724 (sodium D); von Rohr's ν̄ = 41.5. Glass: Unmatched (baryta light flint). f = −7.6 mm (computed).

The thin middle lens (0.5 mm on axis, about 2.6 mm at the rim) that both achromats share. Its front junction with L1
is strongly curved and its rear junction with L3 is weak; the index rises by 0.045 at r2 and 0.044 at r3, so inside
the cemented group neither junction has much focusing power, and their work is the correction of aberrations and
color. Von Rohr's footnote marks its index as corrected from the British Journal's misprint.

### L3 — Biconvex Positive

nD = 1.61120 (sodium D); von Rohr's ν̄ = 44.8. Glass: Unmatched (heaviest baryta crown). f = +13.0 mm (computed).

The rear collective lens and the highest-index glass. Its rear face (r = −12.6) is the strongest positive surface in
the lens and fixes the focal length most sensitively. The edge thickness at the 4.0 mm rim is 0.36 mm, the thinnest
edge in the lens.

## Glass Identification

| Element | Von Rohr's name | nD | ν̄ (von Rohr) | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L1 | gewöhnliches Silikat-Crown (ordinary silicate crown) | 1.52246 | 46.4 | positive, low index |
| L2 | Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | 1.56724 | 41.5 | negative, middle index |
| L3 | Schwerstes Baryt-Crown (heaviest baryta crown) | 1.61120 | 44.8 | positive, high index |

Von Rohr gives these names and figures on p. 373 as N, M and H. His barred ν̄ is defined by the column heads of his
glass table on p. 339 as (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), a dispersion figure for the photographically active blue, not the Abbe
number (nD − 1)/(nF − nC). The values cannot be converted to νd without the glasses' F and C indices, which he does not
give, so the model stores nD only and traces monochromatically at the sodium D line. No modern catalog glass is
substituted. The ordering of the ν̄ values still shows the chromatic logic: L3 has a higher index than L2 and a lower
dispersion, the new-achromat pairing that the Jena baryta crowns made possible.

## Aperture, Field and Focus

The stop is a plain diaphragm 1.0 mm in front of the lens, so it is also the entrance pupil. At f/14.4 its radius is
3.477 mm, and the axial beam reaches 3.82 mm at the rear face, inside the 4.0 mm rim. At the printed ω = 34° the chief
ray reaches the image plane at 63.5 mm; the oblique beam keeps its full width at the stop but loses about 41 % on one
side at the two rear faces, ordinary mechanical vignetting of a lens with a front stop. The paraxial rim limit is
about 49.6°, but the traced field is held at von Rohr's 34°. Smaller stops of f/16 and f/22 are provided. Focus is at
infinity only; von Rohr gives the lens for a distant object.

## Design Heritage and Context

Fig. 137 belongs to the short Series VI and VIa episode of the Zeiss anastigmats. Von Rohr shows the set lens it formed
as Fig. 139 (p. 378): two Fig. 137 lenses in symmetric opposition, the Satz-Anastigmat Series VIa, computed for 1:6.8
and ω = 29°, for which he gives no spacing data. On p. 379 he lists the issued combinations as holosymmetric at 1:6.9
and hemisymmetric at 1:7.7 and 1:8.5. The idea of single lenses that serve alone or in pairs came from the older
sets of aplanat halves, in which only the spherical aberration was corrected; Rudolph's single lenses changed it by
correcting the first four Seidel errors, more or less closely, in each component. From spring 1895 the series was
replaced by the four-lens cemented Anastigmatlinse of Series VII (Fig. 140) and its paired form, Series VIIa
(Fig. 141).

On p. 374 von Rohr also describes the second type in the British patent, the later one, with the outward form of
Fig. 136: two achromats of normal glass pairs, of which the one nearer the stop has a negative focal length about twice
the positive one of the other. Its correction, especially of astigmatism, was distinctly worse, and it was not
introduced.

The British patent 4692 of 1893 is the publication von Rohr's bibliography links to this lens (context, not the source
of these numbers). Von Rohr says the patent printed Rudolph's detailed construction data (p. 377); whether its table
matches the reduced table of Fig. 137 has not been checked.
