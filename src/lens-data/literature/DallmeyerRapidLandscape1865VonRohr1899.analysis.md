# Dallmeyer Rapid Landscape f/11 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 77

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 178, Fig. 77
**Data after:** D. van Monckhoven, *Photographische Optik*, German edition by A. Martin (Vienna: Oscar Kramer, 1866), p. 126, the translation of his *Optique photographique* (von Rohr's "Quelle: D. van Monckhoven 1. 126")
**Designer:** John Henry Dallmeyer (1830–1883), credited in the caption as "J. H. Dallmeyer"
**Manufacturer:** J. H. Dallmeyer, London
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1865 (brought out early 1865; English patent 2539 of 14 October 1864)

Fig. 77 is captioned "J. H. Dallmeyer: Rapid landscape lens", and Tafel IV, among the landscape lenses with a front
stop, redraws it at natural size as No. 77 with the spherical-aberration curve 77a and the astigmatism curve 77b. The
text on p. 178 names the lens in two ways: early in 1865 Dallmeyer brought out his "dreifache Landschaftslinse"
(triple landscape lens), "later known under the name rapid landscape lens". The design date and the later trade name
are therefore von Rohr's own pairing. Dallmeyer's paper on the lens, as von Rohr's bibliography lists it (B. J. 1865),
was titled "On a new form of landscape lens, including a large angle of view".

Von Rohr reduces the source data to fD = 100 mm and computes the lens for 1:11.0 and a half-field ω = 34°. The text
adds a spherical aberration of about −3.1 mm at 1:11.04 and, at 34°, an astigmatic difference of about 6.6 mm with a
negative field curvature of about 5 mm. He places the lens alongside Th. Ross's wide-angle doublet of late 1864 and,
following Th. Grubb, describes it as essentially Grubb's aplanatic lens with its front crown lens divided into two
menisci, together equivalent to it, that enclose the flint.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The radius magnitudes r1 = 60.4, r2 = 17.3, r3 = 48.1 and r4 = 25.6; the thicknesses
d1 = 3.0, d2 = 1.0 and d3 = 1.5; the stop distance b1 = 10.0 in front of the lens; nD = 1.521, 1.581 and 1.514 for
L1, L2 and L3; the reduction to fD = 100 mm; the aperture 1:11.0; the half-field ω = 34°. The text gives the
spherical aberration at 1:11.04 and the astigmatic difference and field curvature at 34°; Tafel IV plots both
aberrations at the heights and angles he traced.

**Measured by von Rohr from a drawing.** The caption states that the distance (b1) and the thicknesses are taken from
the drawing; the table sets these four numbers in italics. It does not say whose drawing; the one in van Monckhoven's
book is the likely one, an inference. The radii and indices are not marked. By his notes on the plates, von Rohr
traced each lens with the data of the named source and only then reduced data and results to f = 100.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 77 and Tafel IV: one cemented meniscus with all four faces concave toward the front
  stop, so every radius is negative.
- The last gap, 102.898 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii of 10 mm on all four surfaces, an estimate kept because nothing clips (see Aperture, Field and Rims).
  The stop radius, 4.518 mm, is derived from 1:11.
- The f-stop list 11–16–22–32–45, the field limit at the printed 34° and the element focal lengths, computed thick
  in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The rounded table traces to an EFL of 99.387 mm, 0.61 % short of 100 mm. The residual
lies within the rounding of the radii and indices: the triplet's power rests almost entirely on r4, and |r4| = 25.65
instead of 25.6 alone gives 99.79 mm. The model is f/11 at its own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 4.518 mm. Its
longitudinal spherical aberration is −3.076 mm at that rim and −3.091 mm at 4.529 mm, the 1:11.04 height at
f = 100, against the printed −3.1 mm. The field is traced to the printed 34°.

## Optical Architecture

The lens is a single cemented triplet, crown–flint–crown, bent as a meniscus concave toward a stop 10 mm in front of
it. The vertex length is 5.5 mm and the total track from stop to image 118.398 mm. The rear principal plane lies
3.51 mm behind the last vertex, outside the glass on the convex side, as is usual for a meniscus of this bending.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = −60.4 | air → 1.521 | −0.0086 | front face, weakly diverging |
| r2 = −17.3 | 1.521 → 1.581 | −0.0035 | deep crown–flint junction |
| r3 = −48.1 | 1.581 → 1.514 | +0.0014 | shallow flint–crown junction |
| r4 = −25.6 | 1.514 → air | +0.0201 | rear face, carries the power |

All the collective power sits on the last face; the front face takes some of it back, and the two cemented
junctions, with index steps of only 0.06–0.07, are where the crowns and the flint meet for colour correction. Von
Rohr sees the lens's main merit in the correction of the oblique pencils, and its shorter stop distance, against the
French landscape lens, as what allows a much larger field. The price is distortion: on pp. 191–192 he explains,
after Dallmeyer's own lecture of 1859/60, that a simple lens with a front stop gives barrel distortion, and calls
this landscape lens "naturally also burdened with distortion". In the model the chief ray at 34° lands 62.83 mm from the
axis against f·tan ω = 67.04 mm, a barrel distortion of 6.3 %; at 20° it is 2.0 %.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Positive Meniscus, concave toward the stop

nD = 1.521 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = +45.5 mm (thick, in air).

The front half of the split crown is 3.0 mm thick on the axis and thins to 0.65 mm at the 10 mm rim. Its rear face,
r2 = −17.3, is by far the deepest curve in the lens; in air it would make L1 strongly positive, but cemented to the
flint it is the principal achromatizing junction. With the printed radii L1 runs out to a knife edge at 11.10 mm,
which is where both drawings end the lens.

### L2 — Negative Meniscus, concave toward the stop

nD = 1.581 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint, unmatched. f = −47.1 mm (thick, in air).

The flint is the thin middle member, 1.0 mm on the axis and 3.13 mm at the 10 mm rim, because its front face is
much deeper than its rear face (r3 = −48.1). It supplies the negative power that balances the colour of the two
crowns, and its strong curvature on the stop side places most of that correction at the r2 junction.

### L3 — Positive Meniscus, concave toward the stop

nD = 1.514 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = +104.1 mm (thick, in air).

The rear half of the split crown is 1.5 mm thick on the axis and 0.52 mm at the rim. Its outer face r4 is the only
strongly collective surface of the lens. Its index is slightly lower than that of L1 (1.514 against 1.521).

## Glass Selection

The table gives only "Glasarten nD" with no glass names. The text calls the outer members crown ("die vordere
Crownlinse in zwei ... Menisken zerlegt") and the middle member the flint component, and both drawings hatch L1 and
L3 in the descending lines that the plate legend assigns to old, low-index crown and L2 in the rising lines it
assigns to flint. Von Rohr prints no dispersion for this lens; his own dispersion figure ν̄, where his tables give
one, is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), a photographic blue measure rather than the Abbe number, and it does not appear on this
page. The model keeps nD only, with no νd and no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic: its colour
correction cannot be evaluated.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The aperture is 1:11; the stop is the entrance pupil, 4.518 mm in radius at the model's EFL. Both drawings show the
incident axial beam, which also marks the stop opening, at 4.4–4.5 mm from the axis, the 1:11 pupil at f = 100
being 4.55 mm. The f/11 axial beam reaches 4.67 mm at r4.

Von Rohr computes the oblique pencils to ω = 34°. With the 10 mm rims the exact chief ray at 34° meets the four
surfaces at 6.51, 7.29, 8.14 and 8.49 mm, so it passes with a margin; it would first strike r4 at about 39.7°. The
meridional beam passes whole up to about 23°, then 96 % of the stop diameter at 25°, 88 % at 28°, 82 % at 30°, 76 %
at 32° and 70 % at 34°, the loss falling on the upper rim rays at r3 and r4. The paraxial rim limit, from r4, is
35.6°. The field limit is the printed 34°.

The 10 mm rims are an estimate, kept because nothing clips. Fig. 77 and Tafel IV, both drawn at about natural size
for f = 100, show the lens edged square at 10.7–11.1 mm from the axis, the height at which L1 reaches its knife edge.
Rims of 10.9 mm would raise the 34° meridional transmission from 70 % to 82 %.

## Aberrations Against Tafel IV

Curve 77a gives the longitudinal aberration of the back focus (solid) and of the focal length (dashed) at the three
incidence heights von Rohr traced; curve 77b gives the sagittal (solid) and meridional (dashed) focus positions,
measured from the paraxial focus, at three field angles. The model's exact trace gives:

| Height | 77a back focus / focal length (mm) | Model (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.5 mm | −0.34 / −0.28 | −0.34 / −0.26 |
| 3.0 mm | −1.41 / −1.12 | −1.36 / −1.05 |
| 4.5 mm | −3.09 / −2.35 | −3.05 / −2.36 |

| ω (read on the plate) | 77b sagittal / meridional (mm) | Model (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 19.7° | −3.04 / −1.36 | −3.06 / −1.35 |
| 27.6° | −5.66 / −1.94 | −5.77 / −1.92 |
| 34.1° | −8.34 / −1.72 | −8.42 / −1.71 |

The rounded table reproduces both curves within about 0.1 mm. At 34° the model's astigmatic difference is 6.68 mm
and the mean of the two foci lies 5.05 mm inside the focal plane, von Rohr's "about 6.6 mm" and "about 5 mm". The
meridional focus stays within 2 mm of the plane over the whole field while the sagittal focus falls steadily short:
von Rohr notes that Dallmeyer gave up a flat mean field, less important in a landscape lens, for good correction of
astigmatism. The Petzval sum is 0.00673 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 149 mm or 1.5 f.

Von Rohr also states that the spherical correction is unchanged from the French landscape lens (Fig. 54, about
−1.9 mm at 1:14). Scaled by the square of the aperture, that value becomes about −3.06 mm at 1:11.04, close to the
−3.1 mm printed here; the scaling is a calculation for this analysis, not von Rohr's.

## Design Heritage and Context

Von Rohr's chapter on English and American optics treats the lens as a contemporary of Th. Ross's doublet. Against
its predecessor, the French landscape lens with its stop 18.1 mm in front and a 27½° half-field (Fig. 54, p. 94),
the shorter stop distance lets the lens cover a much larger angle. He credits its type to Th. Grubb's aplanatic lens
and cites Grubb's paper of 1865, "On a triple cemented compound, constructed on the principle of the aplanatic
(double) view lens", for the view that the triple form gained nothing over the aplanatic lens; von Rohr thinks Grubb
probably right but had no data on the aplanatic lens to make the comparison. On p. 192 he connects the lens with the
distortion dispute between the two opticians. Praising his distortion-free triplet, Dallmeyer had put the distortion
of the simple landscape lens at 4° for a 20° obliquity, where Grubb showed it to be 38′; the lesson seems to have
borne fruit, for within three years Dallmeyer brought out a landscape lens, naturally also with distortion, closely
related to Grubb's, with the crown split in two and cemented in front of and behind the flint.

Dallmeyer, a young merchant from Prussia, found work in Andrew Ross's London workshop, probably in the early 1850s,
and married Ross's daughter (p. 189); his triple achromatic lens and his rectilinear lenses are treated elsewhere in
the chapter. The bibliography lists his English patent 2539 of 14 October 1864 "on the triple landscape lens"
(p. 408, cited for p. 192). Its specification could not be obtained, so whether it prints numerical data is
unknown; it is context, not the source of these numbers.
