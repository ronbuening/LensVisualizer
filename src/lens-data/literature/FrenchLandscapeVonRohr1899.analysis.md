# French Landscape f/12.1 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 54

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 94, Fig. 54
**Data after:** J. M. Eder, *Die photographischen Objektive, ihre Eigenschaften und Prüfung* (Halle: W. Knapp, 1891), p. 57 (von Rohr's "Quelle: J. M. Eder 1. 57")
**Designer:** Not established; the form is the achromatized single lens that the Chevalier workshop in Paris supplied for Daguerre's first cameras, and von Rohr writes that Charles Chevalier's own share in it can no longer be determined
**Manufacturer:** É. Français, Paris, who made the lens that von Rohr reduces (Eder's "Français in Paris")
**Source dated:** 1899

Fig. 54 is captioned "Französische Landschaftslinse (nach É. Français)". On pp. 93–94 von Rohr describes the
first photographic single lens of the Chevalier workshop, used in Daguerre's cameras from 1839: a biconcave flint
cemented in front of a biconvex crown, the flint turning its weaker face toward a front stop. He names this form
"the French landscape lens" and calls it unfavourable for spherical correction. Because the details of the original
construction are only imperfectly known, he takes as its representative a single lens made by É. Français, whose
glasses had been chosen from the old silicate series by their specific gravity. Its external dimensions agree well
with the reduced figures for the Chevalier lens, and he treats the results of his computation as typical of both.

The prescription is therefore Français's lens, not a reconstruction of the 1839 Chevalier lens. Neither von Rohr
nor Eder dates Français's lens; Eder calls it a good newer form of the French landscape lens. Tafel IV, among the
landscape lenses with front stop, redraws it at natural size as No. 54, with the spherical aberration curve 54a and
the astigmatism curve 54b. Von Rohr names no patent for the lens.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The radius magnitudes r1 = 80.3, r2 = 77.2 and r3 = 28.0, the thicknesses d1 = 1.3 and
d2 = 3.1, the stop distance b1 = 18.1 in front of r1, nD = 1.613 for L1 and 1.516 for L2, the reduction to
fD = 100 mm, the aperture 1:12.1 and the half-field ω = 27½°. The text adds a longitudinal spherical aberration of
about −1.9 mm at 1:14, an astigmatic difference of more than 10 mm at a chief-ray inclination of 25°, and a small
field caused by the comparatively large stop distance.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Three footnotes qualify the table. The stop distance is "somewhat
reduced here with regard to the field"; both thicknesses are "taken from the figure"; both indices are "roughly
estimated from the stated specific gravity". By his notes on the plates he traced each lens with the data of the
named source and only then reduced data and results to f = 100.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 54, the Tafel IV drawing and the text: the front flint is biconcave and the rear crown
  biconvex, so r1 and r3 are negative and r2 positive.
- The last gap, 102.648 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii of 10.25 mm on all three faces, set just above the chief-ray height at the printed field angle and
  inside the 10.7–10.8 mm at which both drawings show the lens (see Aperture, Field and Rims). The stop radius,
  4.141 mm, is derived from 1:12.1.
- The f-stop list 12.1–14–16–22–32 and the element focal lengths, computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The table traces to an EFL of 100.209 mm, 0.21 % long. The residual lies within the
rounding of r3: 27.95 instead of 28.0 gives 99.87 mm, and Eder's radii divided by 155/80.3 give 100.04 mm. The model
is f/12.1 at its own EFL. The trace field is the printed 27.5°; the chief ray passes to about 27.6°.

## Optical Architecture

The lens is a single cemented doublet, a meniscus concave toward the object, with the stop 18.1 mm in front of it.
The vertex length is 4.4 mm and the total track 125.148 mm. The front face r1 and the cemented junction r2 are both
weak and slightly negative; nearly all the power sits on the steep rear face r3.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = −80.3 | air → 1.613 | −0.0076 | weak front face, concave toward the stop |
| r2 = +77.2 | 1.613 → 1.516 | −0.0013 | cemented junction, concave toward the image |
| r3 = −28.0 | 1.516 → air | +0.0184 | collective rear face, convex toward the image |

Both principal planes lie behind the lens, 0.77 mm and 2.44 mm beyond r3, as in any meniscus convex toward the
image. The stop is the entrance pupil; the exit pupil lies 27.9 mm in front of r3. The front stop gives the lens
its barrel distortion: the chief ray lands 1.6 % short of f·tan ω at 20°, 2.4 % at 25° and 2.9 % at 27.5°.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Biconcave Negative (flint, in front)

nD = 1.613 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old silicate flint, unmatched. f = −64.0 mm.

The flint stands in front, the arrangement von Rohr gives as characteristic of the French form, and turns its
weaker face (r1, 80.3 mm) toward the stop. It is 1.3 mm thick on the axis and 2.64 mm at the 10.25 mm rim. Its
negative power makes it the achromatizing partner; the junction r2 with the crown has an index step of only 0.097.

### L2 — Biconvex Positive (crown, behind)

nD = 1.516 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old silicate crown, unmatched. f = +40.2 mm.

The crown carries the collective power, almost all of it on the 28.0 mm rear face. With the 3.1 mm axial thickness
taken from the drawing it thins to 0.47 mm at the 10.25 mm rim and would reach a knife edge at about 11.1 mm. In a
third-order sum the rear face produces nearly all of the undercorrected spherical aberration; the weak front face
and the junction offset only about 4 % of it, which is consistent with von Rohr's verdict that the flint-in-front
form is unfavourable for spherical correction.

## Glass Selection

Von Rohr's glass column gives only nD, 1.613 for the flint and 1.516 for the crown, chosen "from the series of the
old silicate glasses" by the specific gravity that his source states. Eder gives the densities as 3.56 for the
flint and 2.52 for the crown (read from the text layer of the digitized Eder; the page image was not consulted).
The hatching of No. 54 on Tafel IV follows the plate legend: rising lines (flint) for L1 and descending lines (old,
low-index crown) for L2.

Von Rohr prints no dispersion figure for this lens. His barred ν̄, which he defines with his glass table on
p. 339 as (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), would not be an Abbe number in any case. The model keeps nD only, with no νd and no
modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic. Von Rohr adds only that the achromatization of these early
lenses was made, as for other instruments of the time, for the optically effective part of the spectrum.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The aperture is 1:12.1, an entrance-pupil radius of 4.141 mm. Both drawings show the incident axial beam 4.1 mm
from the axis. For comparison, the Chevalier lens as Daguerre used it (lens diameter 81 mm, f = 380 mm, a 27 mm
stop 68 mm in front of the lens, 1:14) scales at f = 100 to a lens radius of 10.66 mm and a stop 17.9 mm in front,
close to the 18.1 mm of the table. Eder's Français lens (diameter 40 mm, stop 45 mm from the glass) scales by von
Rohr's factor of 1.93 to a lens radius of 10.36 mm and a stop distance of 23.3 mm; the 18.1 mm of the table is the
distance his footnote reports as reduced.

The staged clear radius of 10.0 mm blocked the chief ray at the printed 27.5°, which meets r2 at 10.02 mm and r3 at
10.22 mm. Fig. 54 and Tafel IV, scaled from their own vertex spacings and stop distance, draw the doublet with one
square rim line at 10.7–10.8 mm. The rims were therefore raised to 10.25 mm, just above the chief-ray height, on all
three faces so that both elements keep one height as drawn. The chief ray then passes to about 27.6°.

Vignetting is real and kept. The meridional beam passes whole to about 17°, then 96 % at 18°, 87 % at 20°, 77 % at
22°, 63 % at 25° and 50 % at 27.5°, where the upper half of the beam is lost at the rear face. The axial beam clears
every rim. The paraxial rim limit is 25.6° (r3).

## Oblique Pencils and Spherical Aberration

The spherical aberration is undercorrected: −2.545 mm at the rim of the f/12.1 pupil, −1.838 mm at 0.85, −1.246 mm
at 0.7 and −0.636 mm at 0.5, and −1.90 mm at f/14, the "about −1.9 mm" of the text. Curve 54a on Tafel IV reaches
about −2.56 mm at its top ordinate of about 4.1 mm.

Curve 54b gives, from the focal point, the plate shift needed for sagittal (solid) and meridional (dashed) focus.
A Coddington trace along the exact chief ray of the model gives, in mm from the paraxial image plane:

| ω | Curve 54b, sagittal / meridional | Model, sagittal / meridional |
|---|---|---|
| 10° | — | −0.44 / +0.83 |
| 20° | −1.65 / +3.87 | −1.69 / +4.22 |
| 25° | — | −2.55 / +7.98 |
| 27.5° | −3.18 / +10.57 | −3.02 / +10.88 |

The plate values were read at 600 dpi on a warped scan and agree with the model within about 0.35 mm. At 25° the
two foci lie 10.5 mm apart, the astigmatic difference of more than 10 mm that the text reports. The meridional image
runs away from the lens much faster than the sagittal image falls short; von Rohr calls the state of the oblique
pencils unfavourable for that reason and the field small because of the comparatively large stop distance. The
Petzval sum is 0.00691 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 145 mm or 1.45 f.

## Design Heritage and Context

According to von Rohr (pp. 93–96), the Chevalier workshop in Paris supplied lenses to both N. Niépce and
L. Daguerre. Daguerre himself may have prompted the achromatization of W. H. Wollaston's meniscus, and the first
Daguerre cameras already carried a single lens achromatized by the method of V. and Ch. Chevalier.
Wollaston's uncorrected meniscus stands beside this lens on Tafel IV as No. 52. In 1840 Charles Chevalier combined
two lenses into his objective "à verres combinés"; its rear lens alone was essentially the old landscape lens and
could be used by itself in its correct position, concave side toward the object and the stop. Von Rohr had no
numbers for that objective (p. 97).

Eder calls Français's lens a good newer form of the type but gives no date. He describes it, drawn at natural size
for 13 × 18 cm plates, with a lens diameter of 40 mm, a stop 45 mm from the glass, a focus of 180 mm and radii of
155, 149 and 54 mm (from the digitized text, not the page image). Those radii are von Rohr's divided by 1.930, which
with von Rohr's indices gives a focal length of 193 mm: he reduced the lens by its computed focal length, not by the
stated 180 mm. The firm's Rectilinear objective, a separate design, is the subject of an 1883 article by Eder that
von Rohr cites on p. 129.
