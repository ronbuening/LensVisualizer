# Carl Zeiss Jena Planar f/3.3 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 146

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 391, Fig. 146
**Designer:** Paul Rudolph
**Manufacturer:** Carl Zeiss, Jena
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1896

Fig. 146 is captioned "P. Rudolph: Planar." and carries the source line "Quelle: Angaben des Verfassers": the table is
von Rohr's own data, not a reduction of a named earlier publication. The same objective is drawn again, without the
table, as No. 146 among the portrait objectives of Tafel I, with its spherical-aberration curves (146a) and
astigmatism curves (146b). Von Rohr discusses the design on pp. 389–391 of the historical part, in the section on
German and Austrian optics.

His account runs as follows. In 1896 Rudolph showed that two glasses of equal refractive index but different
dispersion, cemented together, make a lens whose refraction is that of a single homogeneous lens while its colour
dispersion can be chosen almost freely (the hyperchromatic negative lens, p. 389). Half a year later the idea was put
to use in the Planar (p. 390): an essentially symmetric objective whose halves take the form of a Gauss telescope
objective, modified so that each half gives up the sine condition, with that error removed by the symmetric pairing
of the two halves. The negative lenses were achromatized with the hyperchromatic cemented lens, and a slightly
different glass in the negative lens proved useful for further compensation of the spherical zones. Von Rohr calls
the example a "Portraitplanar" type and states that Rudolph developed the Planar type in the Series Ia of Carl Zeiss
toward fast systems with an anastigmatically flattened field of moderate size (p. 391). He prints no production
rating, so the 1:3.3 below is the aperture of his computation, not a catalogue figure.

The design date is the Planar's own: von Rohr places the construction in 1896, and his bibliography (p. 425) lists it
as Rudolph's patent D.R.P. 92 313 of 14 November 1896, granted to the firm of Carl Zeiss in Jena. The date of the
particular computation he prints is not stated.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** Reduction to fD = 100 mm; computation for 1:3.3 and ω = 25°; all ten radius magnitudes
(r1 = 32.0, r2 = r9 = 144.1, r3 = 37.6, r4 = r7 = 45.5, r5 = r6 = 20.5, r8 = 32.6, r10 = 33.9); the thicknesses
d1 = d6 = 5.0, d2 = d5 = 5.3, d3 = d4 = 2.7; the air spaces b1 = b4 = 0.27 and b2 = b3 = 5.9 (cemented pair to stop
on each side); and three nD values, 1.56785 (L1, L6), 1.56768 (L2, L5) and 1.57087 (L3, L4). The table pairs most
values between the halves but prints r1 ≠ r10 and r3 ≠ r8, so the objective is not a mirror image. Tafel I adds the
computed zonal curves.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** No value in the table is marked as assumed. His notes to the plates
explain the general procedure: each lens was computed from the data of its source and only then were the data and
results reduced to f = 100 mm, and the extreme incidence heights and field angles quoted in the text are rough
estimates that the plates give more exactly. The highest computed zone in curve 146a lies at an incidence height of
about 15.3 mm (1:3.26), and the astigmatism curves 146b end at about 25°.

**Not part of the print.** The e-rara scan of p. 391 carries a later reader's ink and pencil notes: hand colouring of
the elements, surface labels, a build-length note, a diameter sketch and modern glass-code guesses with νd values in
the margin. None of it is used. The table itself is clean and unambiguous, and the uncoloured Tafel I drawing agrees
with Fig. 146.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 146 and Tafel I: both outer menisci convex outward, each inner pair a biconvex crown
  cemented to a biconcave flint whose deep face is concave toward the stop, the rear half mirroring the front.
- The last gap, 80.532 mm, the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii (see Aperture, Field and Rims), the iris radius derived from 1:3.3, the f-stop list 3.3–16, the field
  limit at the printed 25°, and element focal lengths computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The printed table traces to an EFL of 99.545 mm, 0.46 % short of 100 mm. That is
rounding: 0.05 mm on r5 or r6 alone moves the EFL by about 0.38 mm. Making either pair symmetric gives 85 mm
(r3 = 32.6) or 120 mm (r8 = 37.6), so the printed asymmetry is real. At the model's EFL the f/3.3 entrance pupil has
a radius of 15.083 mm. The longitudinal spherical aberration is +0.008, −0.237, −0.272 and −0.188 mm at 1, 0.85,
0.7 and 0.5 of that pupil. Curve 146a, read at von Rohr's computed zones and compared with the model scaled to
f = 100:

| Incidence height (f = 100) | 146a, back focus | Model | 146a, focal length | Model |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 8.8 mm | −0.19 | −0.23 | −0.26 | −0.28 |

The model has the same form, an undercorrected zone near −0.2 mm with the marginal ray crossing zero just inside

## Optical Architecture

Six elements in four air-separated groups about a central stop: a positive meniscus, a cemented pair of net negative
power, the stop, a second cemented negative pair and a second positive meniscus. Each half has the form of a Gauss
objective: a collective meniscus beside a negative member whose outer faces (r3 = 37.6 and r5 = 20.5 in front) form a
meniscus, here built as a hyperchromatic cemented pair. The power distribution is + − | − +.

| Group | Elements | Focal length (mm) |
|---|---|---:|
| G1 | L1 | +71.3 |
| G2 | L2 + L3 | −93.7 |
| G3 | L4 + L5 | −124.5 |
| G4 | L6 | +76.8 |

The front half (r1–r5) has a focal length of +166.9 mm and the rear half (r6–r10) +137.0 mm: the stronger r8
(32.6 against 37.6) makes the rear half about a fifth stronger, partly offset by the weaker r10. The deep concave
faces r5 and r6 (surface power −0.0279 mm⁻¹ each) face each other across an 11.8 mm air space and supply the
negative Petzval contribution. The Petzval sum is 0.00220 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 454 mm or 4.5 f, which
with the symmetric correction of astigmatism gives the flat field the text describes.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Positive Meniscus, outer collective

nD = 1.56785 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: new high-index crown (figure hatching), unmatched. f = +71.3 mm
(L1), +76.8 mm (L6).

The outer menisci carry the collective power of each half at their strong outer faces, r1 = 32.0 and r10 = 33.9,
with nearly flat inner faces (r2 = r9 = 144.1). L1 is convex toward the object and L6 toward the image. The 0.27 mm
air space behind L1 and before L6 is a thin meniscus of air that widens toward the rim.

### L2 / L5 — Biconvex Positive, crown of the cemented pair

nD = 1.56768 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: new high-index crown (figure hatching), unmatched. f = +37.1 mm
(L2), +34.3 mm (L5).

The positive lens of each inner pair. Its outer face (r3 = 37.6 in front, r8 = 32.6 behind) is the second strong
collective surface of each half and the main place where the two halves differ. Both are drawn running to a knife
edge at the rim, which the radii reach at about 14.5 mm (L2) and 13.9 mm (L5).

### L3 / L4 — Biconcave Negative, flint of the cemented pair

nD = 1.57087 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint (figure hatching), unmatched. f = −24.4 mm.

The negative lens of each pair turns its deep face, r5 = r6 = 20.5, toward the stop. These two surfaces are the
strongest in the system. They balance the collective faces for spherical aberration, Petzval sum and astigmatism;
the zonal residual of that balance is the one curve 146a shows.

### The hyperchromatic junctions r4 and r7

Across r4 = r7 = 45.5 the index changes by only 0.00319, so each junction has a surface power of −0.00007 mm⁻¹,
less than 0.5 % of r1. At the D line the cemented pair refracts almost like one lens of a single glass; the junction
exists for its chromatic effect, the dispersion difference between crown and flint. The small index step is
presumably what von Rohr means by the "slightly different glass in the negative lens" that helped compensate the
spherical zones.

## Glass Selection

| Glass class (figure hatching) | nD | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| New high-index crown | 1.56785 | L1, L6 | outer collective menisci |
| New high-index crown | 1.56768 | L2, L5 | positive lens of each cemented pair |
| Flint | 1.57087 | L3, L4 | negative lens of each cemented pair |

Page 391 names no glasses and gives no dispersion figure: only nD. The classes come from the hatching of Fig. 146 and
Tafel I, which the notes to the plates define (axis-parallel hatching for new, high-index crown; hatching rising to
the right for flint). Where von Rohr does print dispersion elsewhere in the book, his ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD),
defined by the column heads of his glass table on p. 339; it measures dispersion toward the photographic blue rather
than over the F–C interval and is not an Abbe number. The model therefore keeps nD only, with no Abbe number and no
modern catalog glass as a proxy (the margin's glass-code guesses included), and is monochromatic.

The three indices lie within 0.0032 of one another: the cemented junctions carry the colour correction and the outer
radii the monochromatic correction, as the hyperchromatic principle intends.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The model's iris follows from the printed 1:3.3: a stop radius of 11.47 mm, whose axial beam reaches 15.26 mm at r1,
14.33 mm at r3 and 14.28 mm at r4. The two horizontal lines left of the Tafel I drawing, presumably the incident
axial beam, lie at a radius of about 14.9 mm, close to 1:3.3 at f = 100. The printed ω = 25° (an image circle of
about 94 mm at f = 100) is the field limit: the chief ray passes every rim there, with heights up to 9.5 mm at r1 and
r10, while the outer rims cut the oblique pencil on one side, ordinary vignetting that is kept. The paraxial rim
limit is 38.3°, set by r1.

| Surface | Model clear radius (mm) | f/3.3 axial ray (mm) | Tafel I (mm) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| r1, r2 | 16.0 | 15.26, 14.89 | 16.05 |
| r3, r4 | 14.4 | 14.33, 14.28 | 14.26 |
| r5 | 13.8 | 11.69 | 12.13, land to 14.26 |
| stop | 11.47 | — | 11.0 |
| r6 | 13.8 | 11.22 | 11.97, land to 13.62 |
| r7, r8 | 13.8 | 12.56, 12.72 | 13.62 |
| r9, r10 | 16.0 | 12.80, 12.96 | 16.09 |

The 16 and 13.8 mm values are earlier estimates, not figure measurements; only r3 and r4 were raised, from 13.8 to
14.4 mm, because at 13.8 they clipped the f/3.3 axial beam. Tafel I, scaled from its 38.34 mm vertex length, matches
the outer lenses and the L2/L3 rim within 0.15 mm and draws L4/L5 0.2 mm smaller. It ends the concave faces r5 and r6
at about 12 mm with a flat land to the element rim; the model keeps them at 13.8 mm, passing the same axial beam.

## Design Heritage and Context

The patent named in von Rohr's bibliography, D.R.P. 92 313 ("Astigmatisch, sphärisch und chromatisch corrigirtes
Objectiv", Carl Zeiss in Jena, patented from 14 November 1896, published 5 July 1897), is context here, not the
source of the numbers. It opens from the Gauss telescope objective and claims a lens system with anastigmatic field
flattening of two air-separated lenses, one or both cemented from glasses of nearly equal index but different
dispersion, and a double objective built of two such systems. Its Beispiel 2 is a symmetric double objective at
1:4 whose values, scaled to f = 100, differ throughout from von Rohr's table: inner radius 19.54 against 20.5,
junction 43.70 against 45.5, flint thickness 4.11 against 2.7, outer-meniscus thickness 3.08 against 5.0, stop
distance 5.14 against 5.9, and two glasses (nD 1.57631 and 1.57244) against three. The bibliography also lists an
English counterpart, E.P. 27 635 of 4 December 1896.

Von Rohr judged the Planar a theoretical step as decisive as the anastigmat doublets, but expected its practical
effect to be smaller because the anastigmats had already raised the quality of the better lenses on the market
(p. 391). Zeiss later reused the name Planar for its double-Gauss objectives.
