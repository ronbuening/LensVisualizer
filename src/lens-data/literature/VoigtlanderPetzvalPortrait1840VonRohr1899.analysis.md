# Voigtländer Petzval Portrait f/3.4 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 100

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 250, Fig. 100
**Data after:** Fr. Voigtländer (radii), J. Petzval (lens separation), J. M. Eder (glass), B. S. F. 1858 (stop distance)
**Designer:** Joseph Petzval
**Manufacturer:** Voigtländer (Fr. Voigtländer's workshop, Vienna)
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1840

Fig. 100 is captioned "J. Petzval: Portraitobjektiv" and is drawn again, without the table, as No. 100 among the
portrait objectives of Tafel I, with its spherical-aberration curves (100a) and astigmatism curves (100b). The caption
names a separate source for each kind of datum. The radii come from Fr. Voigtländer's 1859 pamphlet against Petzval,
which reprints their correspondence (bibliography Voigtländer 3, p. 98); the lens separation from Petzval's report on
dioptric investigations in the Vienna Academy's Sitzungsberichte of 1857 (Petzval 6, p. 54); the glass from
J. M. Eder's *Die photographischen Objektive* of 1891 (Eder 1, p. 115); and the stop distance from a letter of Petzval
to Paul Pretsch printed in the Bulletin de la Société française de photographie for 1858 (vol. 4, p. 237).

Von Rohr tells the history on pp. 248–255. Andreas von Ettingshausen, who had learned the daguerreotype from Daguerre
in Paris and found Chevalier's single objective too slow, asked his Vienna colleague Joseph Petzval, apparently in
1839, to compute a new and faster objective. Petzval obtained optical constants from the Vienna optician
Fr. Voigtländer and computed a set of three lenses (Fig. 99): front lens 1 combined with lens 3 gave the portrait
objective, combined with lens 2 the landscape objective, which Voigtländer identified with his Orthoskop. The types
were finished by mid-1840; Petzval gave the manufacture to Voigtländer without any contract over his rights, trial
pictures were taken in autumn 1840, and the portrait objective was announced before the end of that year and issued
with Voigtländer's conical camera (Fig. 102). The design date is therefore 1840, and the firm von Rohr credits with
making it is Voigtländer. Von Rohr names no patent for the design.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** Reduction to fD = 100 mm; computation for 1:3.4 and ω = 12°; the seven radius magnitudes
r1 = 52.9, r2 = 41.4, r3 = 436.2, r4 = 104.8, r5 = 36.8, r6 = 45.5 and r7 = 149.5; the cell-to-stop distances
b1 = b2 = 23.3, which place the stop at the middle of the 46.6 mm space between the cells; and two indices,
nD = 1.517 for L1 and L4 and nD = 1.575 for L2 and L3. Tafel I adds the computed zonal curves.

**Assumed by von Rohr.** The four lens thicknesses d1 = 5.8, d2 = 1.5, d3 = 2.2, d4 = 3.6 and the rear air space
b3 = 0.7 carry the footnote "Willkürlich angenommen" (arbitrarily assumed). His notes to the plates add that each lens
was computed from its source data before reduction to f = 100, and that the horizontal lines of the plates mark the
incidence heights and field angles actually computed.

**Not part of the print.** A later reader's pencil notes on the e-rara scan of p. 250 ("1840", "1:3,7 f=149") are
not used.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 100 and Tafel I: L1 biconvex, cemented to the biconcave flint L2 whose nearly flat rear
  face r3 is concave toward the image; L3 a meniscus concave toward the image; L4 biconvex; L3 and L4 enclose a thin
  air meniscus that narrows toward the rim. With r3 convex instead, the EFL falls to 83.9 mm, so the sign is also
  fixed by the printed focal length.
- The last gap, 58.576 mm, the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints none.
- Clear radii, the iris radius derived from 1:3.4, the f-stop list 3.4–16, the field limit at the printed 12°, and
  element focal lengths computed thick in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The table traces to an EFL of 99.927 mm, 0.07 % short of 100 mm; 0.05 mm on r5 alone
moves it by 0.13 mm, so the residual is rounding. At f/3.4 the entrance pupil has a radius of 14.695 mm. Curve 100a,
read at its three computed heights (equal-area zones up to 14.56 mm) and compared with the model scaled to f = 100:

The text on p. 253 gives the same picture: a longitudinal aberration of at most about −0.21 mm on 100 mm even at

length is h/sin u′ (the sine-condition form); it lags curve 100a by 0.01–0.02 mm, inside the table's rounding.

For the oblique pencils von Rohr reports a nearly flat sagittal field to a little over 12° and a meridional image
that recedes from the lens, the astigmatic difference reaching about 3.4 mm at the largest computed obliquity.

## Optical Architecture

Four elements in three air-separated groups, two positive cells with the stop between them: a cemented achromat in
front (f = +139.5 mm), then 46.6 mm of air with the stop at its middle, then a rear cell of a negative flint meniscus
and a biconvex crown separated by 0.7 mm (f = +209.1 mm). The front cell carries most of the power and the rear cell
shortens the focal length to 100 mm while bringing the image close behind it (back focus 58.6 mm).

Von Rohr calls it the first camera objective computed in advance by theory (p. 254); Petzval treated the rear lenses
as an eyepiece-like complement to the front lens (p. 249). Dividing the power between two separated positive cells
keeps the bending at each cell moderate, which is how four lenses reach 1:3.4 (an interpretation, not von Rohr's
words). The price is field curvature: the Petzval sum is 0.00835 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 120 mm or 1.2 f. The
correction holds the sagittal field nearly flat by balancing astigmatism against it, and the meridional field curves
away.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 + L2 — Front cemented achromat

L1: nD = 1.517 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown (figure hatching), unmatched. f = +45.9 mm.
L2: nD = 1.575 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint (figure hatching), unmatched. f = −65.7 mm.

The convex front face r1 (surface power +0.0098 mm⁻¹), met by the axial beam at its full height, contributes most of
the system's convergence. The cemented junction r2 = −41.4 sits between glasses 0.058 apart in index, so it refracts
weakly at the D line and exists for the chromatic correction. The flint's rear face r3 = 436.2 is nearly plane,
concave toward the image; the drawings show it almost straight.

### L3 — Negative Meniscus, rear flint

nD = 1.575 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint (figure hatching), unmatched. f = −99.8 mm.

L3 turns its strong concave face r5 = 36.8 toward the image; at −0.0156 mm⁻¹ it is the most powerful surface in the
system and supplies the negative contributions to spherical aberration and astigmatism that balance the two cells.
Von Rohr notes that it makes the rays of the axial beam run almost parallel in the air space behind it (p. 253): in the
model the marginal ray falls only from 8.97 to 8.96 mm across the 0.7 mm gap.

### L4 — Biconvex Positive, rear crown

nD = 1.517 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown (figure hatching), unmatched. f = +67.9 mm.

L4's front face r6 = 45.5 nearly follows L3's r5, so the air space between them is a thin meniscus that closes toward
the rim (0.1 mm at a height of 14.5 mm). Because the axial beam is nearly parallel there, changing this space hardly
moves the focus but does change the oblique pencils, which is the lever the Voigtländer workshop used (see Design
Heritage).

## Glass Selection

| Glass class (figure hatching) | nD | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old (low-index) crown | 1.517 | L1, L4 | collective lenses of each cell |
| Flint | 1.575 | L2, L3 | dispersive lenses of each cell |

The table prints only nD under "Glasarten nD", with Eder as the source of the glass. The classes come from the
hatching of Fig. 100 and Tafel I, which von Rohr's notes to the plates define (hatching falling to the right for old,
low-index crown; rising to the right for flint). Where he does print dispersion elsewhere in the book, his ν̄ is
(nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), defined by the column heads of his glass table on p. 339: a figure for the photographic blue,
not an Abbe number. No such figure is given here, so the model keeps nD only, with no νd and no modern catalog glass
as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

The text adds two points on colour that the model cannot show: the first objectives proved chromatically
overcorrected (p. 251), and the spherical figures would be more favourable in blue and violet light (p. 253).

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The model's iris follows from 1:3.4 by the engine's paraxial stop rule: a stop radius of 11.574 mm, which admits a
real axial beam reaching 14.44 mm on r1. The exact f/3.4 ray, entering at 14.695 mm, reaches 14.70, 14.59 and 14.24 mm
on r1–r3 and 11.79 mm at the stop. At the printed ω = 12° (an image circle of about 43 mm at f = 100) the chief ray
passes every rim, with heights of 6.4–7.6 mm; the rims cut 33–43 % of the oblique pencil's outer half in front and
12–15 % behind, ordinary vignetting that is kept. The paraxial rim limit is 22.4°, set by r7.

| Surface | Model clear radius (mm) | f/3.4 axial ray (mm) | Fig. 100 (mm) | Tafel I (mm) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| r1, r2, r3 | 15.5 | 14.70, 14.59, 14.24 | 14.4 | 14.2 |
| r4, r5, r6, r7 | 14.5 | 9.26, 8.97, 8.96, 8.77 | 14.3 | 14.1 |

The 15.5 and 14.5 mm values are earlier estimates, not figure measurements, and they are kept. Both drawings, scaled
from the 60.4 mm vertex length, draw the front cell out to the incident-beam lines (14.3–14.4 mm), which stand for
the largest computed incidence height (14.56 mm on curve 100a), and the rear cell about as large. The front rim stands
about 1.1 mm (8 %) above the drawing and 0.8 mm above the exact f/3.4 ray; drawn at 14.4 mm it would clip that ray on
r1 and r2. The difference lies within the noise band of the figure procedure and is not changed.

The 1840 lens itself is a separate measure. Page 252 gives its focal length as 149 mm (5½ inches) with lens diameters
of 40.6 mm in front and 42.9 mm behind, hence 1:3.7; scaled to f = 100 those are radii of 13.6 and 14.4 mm. Von Rohr
remarks that his computed 1:3.4 does not quite match the 1:3.7 of the old figures and suspects uncertain data or an
imprecise focal-length determination at the time (p. 253). Read together, the 40.6 mm front lens over a computed focal
length of about 138 mm would give his 1:3.4; that reading is an inference. Petzval's own figures for portrait
objectives with equal front and rear lenses (p. 252) were the full aperture over a 10° total field, falling to about
1:8 by 31° and to nothing at 50°; the model's meridional pupil is 0.94 of the axial beam at 5° from the axis, 0.49 at
15.5° and zero at 25°.

## Design Heritage and Context

Von Rohr reports that the Voigtländer workshop found at the first trials that the oblique pencils improved when the
rear lenses were set further apart, and that the objectives were therefore issued with a ring separating the two rear
lenses, contrary to the computation. Calculations made for him confirmed it: the separation hardly affects the axial
beam, but it shifts both astigmatic image shells toward the lens, the meridional one much more, so that one can choose
between flattening the field for a given angle and correcting the astigmatism, negative field curvature then being
unavoidable in this type. The model keeps the table's 0.7 mm, the computed design, not the issued lens.

While the book was being printed, Eder published data of the first portrait objective (Phot. Correspondenz 1899).
Von Rohr summarises them in a footnote (pp. 253–254): R. Steinheil's measured radii agree with the table for the
first three lenses, those of the fourth are 5.2 % and 1.7 % larger, the large separation between the cells is much
smaller than the value adopted here, the rear lenses already show the experimental separation, and four different
glasses were used. A computation with the changed distances but his glasses gave a somewhat better spherical
correction; he did not examine the astigmatism.

Pages 254–255 follow the objective's success: export from 1841, a priority dispute with Chevalier, the larger
"Dreizöller" of 1842 (80.1 mm aperture at 298 mm, again 1:3.7), and friction between Petzval and Voigtländer.
