# Busch Pantoskop f/30.4 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 108

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 281, Fig. 108
**Data after:** J. M. Eder, *Die photographischen Objektive, ihre Eigenschaften und Prüfung* (Halle: W. Knapp, 1891), p. 144 (von Rohr's "Quelle: J. M. Eder 1. 144")
**Designer:** Emil Busch (1820–1888), Rathenow
**Manufacturer:** Emil Busch's optical works at Rathenow, the spectacle and lens factory he took over in 1845 (pp. 280–281)
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1865

Fig. 108 is captioned "E. Busch: Pantoskop". Von Rohr introduces it on p. 280 of the historical part of his book,
in the section on German and Austrian optics, as Busch's modification of the globe lens, and prints the table on
p. 281. He reduces the data to fD = 100 mm and computes the lens for 1:30.4 and a half-field ω = 49°. Tafel III
redraws it at natural size among the wide-angle objectives as No. 108, with curves 108a (spherical aberration) and
108b (astigmatism).

The design date comes from Eder: von Rohr places the new construction in 1865 on Eder's authority and adds that the
circumstances of its first introduction are not known (p. 281). On p. 289 he describes it as introduced at about the
same time as Steinheil's Periskop of 1865, or a little later.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** The three radius magnitudes as symmetric pairs (r1 = r6 = 7.45, r2 = r5 = 10.39,
r3 = r4 = 7.63), the glass thicknesses d1 = d4 = 1.74 and d2 = d3 = 0.19, the cell-to-stop distances
b1 = b2 = 6.2, the two nD values (1.5331 for L1 and L4, 1.6079 for L2 and L3), the reduction to fD = 100 mm, the
computation aperture 1:30.4 and the half-field ω = 49°. A footnote marks the crown index as his correction of an
obvious misprint in Eder; 1.5331 is therefore the book's own value, not a correction made for this model. The text
gives one numerical result, a spherical aberration of −6.1 mm at 1:30.4.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Nothing. The caption marks no value as assumed. By his notes on the
plates, every lens was traced with the data of the named source before data and results were reduced to f = 100,
so the table is a rounded reduction of Eder's numbers.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs, read from Fig. 108, from Tafel III and from the symmetric pairing of the table (r1 = r6 and so
  on, a stop distance on each side): each cell is a thick crown meniscus outside, cemented to a thin flint
  meniscus, both convex toward the object in front and concave toward the stop; the rear cell mirrors the front.
- The last gap, 91.070 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints no back focus.
- Clear radii of 6.4 mm on all six surfaces, a heuristic value set by the chief ray at 49° and bounded by the
  drawings (see Aperture, Field and Rims). The stop radius, 1.441 mm, is derived from 1:30.4.
- The f-stop list 30.4–40–56–64, the 49° field limit (the printed ω) and the element focal lengths, computed thick
  in air.

**Printed against modelled.** The rounded table traces to an EFL of 100.245 mm, 0.245 % long. That lies inside the
rounding of the radii: 0.005 mm on r1 moves the EFL between 99.38 and 101.13 mm, and r1 = 7.4486 alone would give
100.00. The f-number is f/30.4 at the model's own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 1.649 mm. The longitudinal
spherical aberration is −6.149 mm at the rim of that pupil and −6.120 mm at the 1.645 mm height that 1:30.4 gives at
exactly f = 100, against the printed −6.1 mm; curve 108a also ends at −6.1 mm. The chief ray at 49° lands
115.28 mm from the axis against f·tan ω = 115.32 mm, a barrel distortion of only 0.04 %.

## Optical Architecture

The Pantoskop is a holosymmetric wide-angle doublet: two identical cemented cells face each other across a central
stop, each a crown meniscus outside and a flint meniscus inside. The menisci are extremely deep. Measured from the
front vertex, the centres of curvature of r1, r2 and r3 lie at 7.45, 12.13 and 9.56 mm, around the stop at
8.13 mm, so the outer surfaces of the two cells lie close to one sphere about the stop. This is the globe-lens
principle: the chief rays of oblique pencils meet each surface not far from its normal (the 49° chief ray strikes r1
at about 10° incidence), which allows the very wide field. The vertex length is only 16.26 mm for f = 100.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 7.45 | air → 1.5331 | +0.0715 | outer collective face |
| r2 = 10.39 | 1.5331 → 1.6079 | +0.0072 | cemented junction, weak |
| r3 = 7.63 | 1.6079 → air | −0.0797 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The three surface powers nearly cancel; each cell owes its weak positive power, +175.3 mm or about 1.75 times the
focal length of the pair, to the 1.93 mm separation of its deep outer and inner faces.

The symmetry about the stop removes coma, distortion and lateral colour at unit magnification and keeps them small
for distant objects. The Petzval sum is 0.0000894 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 11 m or 112 f: the deep menisci
flatten the Petzval surface almost completely, and the curvature that remains in the field is astigmatism. That
matches von Rohr's reading of his trace: everything had been done to correct the oblique pencils, with a
considerable gain, but the astigmatism stays undercorrected with a negative mean field curvature, and a small change
of spacing would bring the lens much closer to an anastigmatic flat field (p. 281). The price is the spherical
aberration, which von Rohr calls extraordinarily large for so small an aperture.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L4 — Positive Meniscus, outer crown

nD = 1.5331 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: old low-index crown, unmatched. f = +41.0 mm.

The outer meniscus is convex toward the object in front (r1 = 7.45) and less deeply curved at its cemented face
(r2 = 10.39). It is 1.74 mm thick on the axis and thins toward the rim, reaching a knife edge at a radius of
6.70 mm with the printed radii. It carries the collective power of the cell. L4 mirrors it in the rear cell,
convex toward the image.

### L2 / L3 — Negative Meniscus, inner flint

nD = 1.6079 (sodium D), no νd published. Glass: flint, unmatched. f = −48.5 mm.

The inner meniscus is only 0.19 mm thick on the axis and concave toward the stop (r3 = 7.63). Because r3 is more
deeply curved than the cemented face, it thickens to 1.46 mm at a radius of 6.4 mm. Its rear face is the strongest
negative surface of the cell, and the flint is the dispersive partner of L1 in the achromatic pair; across the
cemented junction the index rises by only 0.075, so r2 has little power. L3 mirrors it in the rear cell.

## Glass Selection

| Von Rohr's data | nD | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crown (drawn as old low-index crown) | 1.5331 | L1, L4 | positive outer menisci |
| Flint (by index) | 1.6079 | L2, L3 | negative inner menisci |

Von Rohr prints the two indices under "Glasarten nD" without glass names and without his barred dispersion figure
ν̄, which elsewhere in the book is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), a photographic blue figure rather than an Abbe number. Both
drawings hatch the thick outer lenses in the direction the plate legend assigns to old low-index crown. The inner
lenses are too thin near the axis for their hatching to be read; the flint label rests on the index and on the
achromatic pairing. Von Rohr reports that Busch was said to have carried out actinic achromatism, the correction of
colour for the photographically active light, as early as the 1850s (p. 281). With no dispersion published, the
model keeps nD only, with no Abbe number and no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

Von Rohr computes the lens for 1:30.4, a working aperture fixed by the spherical aberration. The model sets the iris
from that aperture: the entrance-pupil radius is 1.649 mm at the model's EFL and the physical stop radius 1.441 mm.
The f/30.4 axial beam reaches only 1.65 mm at r1 and 1.49 mm at r6. Fig. 108 draws the stop opening at a radius of
about 1.6 mm and Tafel III at about 1.5 mm.

The printed half-field is ω = 49°, a 98° field. The exact chief ray at 49° crosses r1 and r6 at 6.38 mm, r2 and r5
at 6.16 mm and r3 and r4 at 5.51 mm, so 6.0 mm rims would block it from 44.5°. The rims are set to 6.4 mm on all six
surfaces, the cells being drawn with one flat rim line across their three surfaces. Fig. 108, scaled from the
printed vertex length, draws the cells to about 6.55 mm; Tafel III, at natural size, draws them to about 6.7 mm,
which is where the crowns run out to their knife edge. Both drawings therefore support 6.4 mm.

The oblique beams are vignetted only near the edge. A meridional trace passes the full stop diameter up to 42°,
92 % at 43°, 79 % at 44°, 66 % at 45°, 37 % at 47°, 21 % at 48° and 5 % at 49°. With 6.0 mm rims the same trace
loses beam from 37° and passes nothing beyond 44.5°. The paraxial rim-limited field of the model is only 34.9°,
far short of what the exact trace passes, so the printed 49° is declared as the trace limit.

## Spherical Aberration and Astigmatism

The spherical aberration is undercorrected and grows almost exactly with the square of the aperture: −1.56, −3.05,
−4.47 and −6.15 mm at 0.5, 0.7, 0.85 and 1.0 of the f/30.4 pupil. There is no zonal correction; the deep menisci
leave the third-order term alone to set the working aperture.

A Coddington trace along the exact chief ray puts both astigmatic images in front of the paraxial image plane, the
meridional about three times as far as the sagittal, as a flat Petzval surface requires:

| ω | Meridional (mm) | Sagittal (mm) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 20° | −2.07 | −0.72 |
| 30° | −4.65 | −1.61 |
| 40° | −8.27 | −2.82 |
| 44.4° | −10.22 | −3.45 |
| 49° | −12.49 | −4.17 |

Curve 108b has the same form but smaller values: its last computed point, at about 44.4° on the plate's angle scale,
reads about −7.8 mm meridional and −2.0 mm sagittal. The difference lies within the rounding of the printed stop
distance. The astigmatism is very sensitive to b, as von Rohr's remark on spacing implies: 0.05 mm on b moves the
meridional image at 44.4° between −6.8 and −13.4 mm while the EFL changes by only 0.03 mm. The plate's computed
points end near 44.4°, below the 49° of the caption; von Rohr notes that the plates give the extreme values of ω
more exactly than the text.

## Design Heritage and Context

Von Rohr derives the Pantoskop from the globe lens of C. C. Harrison and Schnitzer (Fig. 74, No. 74 on Tafel III),
which Gasc & Charconnet had copied freely in France as the lentiforme de l'oeil. He describes that lens as a small
symmetric objective with a central stop whose lack of any spherical correction was hidden by strong stopping down
(p. 280). The globe lens attracted attention in Germany as well, and Busch at Rathenow modified it; the result was
named Pantoskop, from the Greek for "all" and "to see". A factory for spectacle glasses, frames and lenses had
existed at Rathenow by 1800; Busch took it over from his uncle Eduard Duncker in 1845 and remained its director
after its conversion to a joint-stock company in 1872 (pp. 280–281).

The Pantoskop appeared at about the time of A. Steinheil's Periskop of 1865 (Fig. 109), another symmetric
wide-angle doublet with a central stop, or a little later. Von Rohr gathers from a lecture by
O. Voget (Photographische Correspondenz, 1866) that the Periskop's only serious contest was with the Pantoskop,
which had somewhat greater sharpness and a larger angle of view together with chromatic correction; the polemic was
heated enough that C. A. Steinheil published a correcting reply (pp. 288–289). Von Rohr names no patent for the
Pantoskop, and his bibliography lists none for Busch.
