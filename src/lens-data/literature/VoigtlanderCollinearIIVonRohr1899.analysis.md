# Voigtländer Collinear Type II f/6.9 — von Rohr 1899, Fig. 144

## Source and Design Identification

**Source:** Moritz von Rohr, *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (Berlin: Julius Springer, 1899), p. 385, Fig. 144
**Data after:** D. Kaempfer (von Rohr's "Quelle: D. Kaempfer 2", which his bibliography, p. 416, lists as D.R.P. 90 482 of 22 May 1895)
**Designer:** David Kaempfer
**Manufacturer:** Voigtländer & Sohn, Braunschweig (holder of the patent; von Rohr knew of no introduction of the lens)
**Source dated:** 1899
**Design dated:** 1895

Fig. 144 is captioned "D. Kaempfer: Collinear, Typus II". Von Rohr discusses it on pp. 385–386, in the section on
German and Austrian optics. David Kaempfer was the scientific officer of Voigtländer & Sohn in Braunschweig; he had
learned optical computation in Jena under Paul Rudolph (p. 354). Working in parallel with Rudolf Steinheil's
Orthostigmat Type II, he set out to secure a different type of spherically, chromatically and astigmatically corrected
single lens by a patent of his firm, which he obtained as D.R.P. 90 482 in May 1895. The new objective was called the
Collinear, Typus II, or the Dreimenisken-Objektiv (three-meniscus objective), because each half is three cemented
menisci. Von Rohr reduces the patent example to a focal length of 100 mm and computes it for 1:6.9 and a half-field
ω = 30½°.

The name needs one caution. The Collinear that Voigtländer actually sold, in Series II at 1:6.3, Series III at 1:7.7
and Series IV at 1:12.5, was the Collinear, Typus I: the type of Steinheil's Orthostigmat patent 88 505, which Kaempfer
had found independently and which the two firms agreed to make jointly (p. 384). This file is the other design, Typus
II; the "II" is the type number under which the design was named, not a sales series.

## Reconstruction Status

**Printed by von Rohr.** All eight radius magnitudes as four symmetric pairs (r1 = r8 = 32.6, r2 = r7 = 11.0,
r3 = r6 = 14.5, r4 = r5 = 41.1), the three glass thicknesses of each cell (1.3, 1.8, 3.0), the cell-to-stop distances
b1 = b2 = 3.3, the three nD values, the reduction to fD = 100 mm, the computation aperture 1:6.9 and the half-field
ω = 30½°. Page 386 adds the glass names and von Rohr's dispersion figures ν̄, and the results of his ray trace:
spherical correction reached for a relative aperture of about 1:7, spherical zones of about −0.6 mm, a close
agreement in the run of the intercept and focal-length deviations, and a field curvature that remains "not
inconsiderable" even with astigmatism corrected for fairly large ω.

**Assumed, measured or estimated by von Rohr.** Nothing. The table marks no thickness or index as assumed; it is his
reduction of the patent example to f = 100, rounded to 0.1 mm.

**Added by this model.**

- Radius signs. Fig. 144 draws each cell as three menisci, all convex toward the outside and concave toward the
  central stop, so r1 to r4 are positive and the rear cell mirrors them. The patent text agrees: a positive middle
  meniscus enclosed by a positive and a negative meniscus, both of higher index.
- The last gap, 91.890 mm, is the computed paraxial back focus; von Rohr prints no back focus.
- Clear radii of 8.0 mm on every surface, a heuristic value. The stop radius (6.625 mm) is derived from 1:6.9.
- The f-stop list 6.9–8–11–16, the 30.5° field limit (the printed ω) and the element focal lengths, computed thick in
  air.

**Printed against modelled.** The printed table traces to an EFL of 99.885 mm, 0.11 % short of 100 mm, which is
ordinary 0.1 mm rounding. The model is f/6.9 at its own EFL, an entrance-pupil radius of 7.238 mm. Its longitudinal
spherical aberration at the f/6.9 pupil is +0.351 mm at the rim, −0.371 mm at 0.85, −0.519 mm at 0.7 and −0.376 mm
at 0.5 of the pupil. The marginal ray crosses zero near 1:7.3, and the largest zone, −0.52 mm, lies near 1:9.8.
Von Rohr reports correction at about 1:7 and zones of about −0.6 mm. The difference is within the rounding of the
table: 0.05 mm on r2 = r7 alone moves the zero to about 1:7.0 and deepens the zone to about −0.6 mm. The chief
ray passes at the printed 30½°.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a symmetrical double: two identical cemented triplets face each other across a central stop, 6.6 mm
apart. Each triplet is of von Rohr's type +H ∓N −M with ν̄H > ν̄N > ν̄M, which places it under case 1aβ of his scheme
of anastigmatic single lenses (p. 385). Reading from the stop outward, a positive meniscus of the heaviest baryta
crown H is followed by a weak positive meniscus of a high-dispersion crown N and an outer negative meniscus of
baryta light flint M. The patent's first claim describes the cell as one half of the double objective of patent 88 505 with the
middle positive meniscus kept, the biconvex lens replaced by a positive meniscus and the biconcave lens by a negative
one, to obtain a more complete correction of spherical aberration.

The two cemented junctions carry opposite index steps, the arrangement the anastigmatic principle needs:

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 32.6 | air → 1.5682 | +0.0174 | outer collective face |
| r2 = 11.0 | 1.5682 → 1.5262 | −0.0038 | dispersive junction, index falls |
| r3 = 14.5 | 1.5262 → 1.6112 | +0.0059 | collective junction, index rises |
| r4 = 41.1 | 1.6112 → air | −0.0149 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

Each cell has a focal length of +182.9 mm, 1.83 times that of the pair, and is 6.1 mm thick; the total length from
first to last vertex is 18.8 mm. The Petzval sum of the whole objective is 0.00535 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about
187 mm or 1.9 f, roughly twice the Petzval curvature of von Höegh's Doppel-Anastigmat (Fig. 138). The high
Petzval sum is consistent with von Rohr's verdict that this type is decidedly unfavourable in field curvature, with a
residual that stays considerable even when astigmatism is corrected for a fairly large field angle.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Negative Meniscus, outer

nD = 1.5682 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 38.2. Glass: baryta light flint, unmatched. f = −29.9 mm.

The outer lens is the cell's only negative member and its most dispersive glass, so it carries the chromatic
correction against the two positive menisci. Its convex outer face (r1 = 32.6) is the strongest collective surface
of the cell, and its deep concave rear face (r2 = 11.0) meets L2 in the dispersive junction. Because the lens is
concave toward the stop it is much thicker at the edge than on the axis: 3.75 mm at the 8 mm rim against 1.3 mm at
the centre. L6 mirrors it in the rear cell.

### L2 / L5 — Positive Meniscus, middle

nD = 1.5262 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 39.8. Glass: high-dispersion crown, unmatched. f = +73.6 mm.

The middle lens has the lowest index of the three, which is the patent's defining feature: it is enclosed by two
lenses of higher refraction. Its low index gives the two cemented junctions their opposite steps, falling at r2 and
rising at r3. With radii of 11.0 and 14.5 mm and a centre thickness of 1.8 mm it thins quickly toward the rim and
reaches a knife edge at a radius of about 9.37 mm, which bounds the clear radius of the inner surfaces of the cell.
L5 mirrors it.

### L3 / L4 — Positive Meniscus, inner

nD = 1.6112 (sodium D), no νd published; ν̄ = 44.8. Glass: heaviest baryta crown, unmatched. f = +35.2 mm.

The high-index meniscus next to the stop carries most of the cell's collective power. Its concave inner face
(r4 = 41.1) is the main negative contribution to the Petzval sum. The patent explains the meniscus form: the new Jena
high-index crown was bubbly and poorly coloured, and a thin positive meniscus in place of a biconvex lens was expected
to give a cleaner, more colourless element. L4 mirrors it in the rear cell.

## Glass Selection

| Von Rohr's name | Symbol | nD | ν̄ | Elements | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schwerstes Baryt-Crown (heaviest baryta crown) | H | 1.6112 | 44.8 | L3, L4 | inner high-index collective |
| Crown mit hoher Dispersion (high-dispersion crown) | N | 1.5262 | 39.8 | L2, L5 | low-index middle meniscus |
| Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | M | 1.5682 | 38.2 | L1, L6 | outer negative meniscus |

Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), the dispersion figure defined by the column heads of his glass table on
p. 339. It measures dispersion toward the photographically active blue (G′ line) rather than across the F–C
interval, so it is not an Abbe number and cannot be converted to νd. The patent prints an F-line index beside each
nD, but no C-line index, so it does not give νd either; in the Google Patents text layer the first of those values
(1.57037 for L1) implies an nF − nD of only 0.0022, implausibly small for a flint, and is probably an OCR error. The
model therefore keeps nD only, with no Abbe number and no modern catalog glass as a proxy, and is monochromatic.

The ν̄ ordering ν̄H > ν̄N > ν̄M puts the least dispersive glass in the strongest positive lens and the most dispersive
in the negative lens. The middle crown N is named for its high dispersion: its ν̄ is close to that of the flint, so
the junction r2 between them has a large index step (0.042) but only a small difference in ν̄.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

Von Rohr computes the objective for 1:6.9 and finds spherical correction near 1:7. The model sets the iris from that
aperture: the entrance-pupil radius is 7.238 mm at the model's EFL and the physical stop radius 6.625 mm. The f/6.9
axial beam reaches 7.24 mm at r1 and 6.73 mm at r8, inside the 8.0 mm rims.

The printed half-field is ω = 30½°, a 61° field. The 8.0 mm rims pass the chief ray there, with chief heights of
4.54, 3.86, 3.26 and 2.19 mm at r1–r4 (mirrored behind the stop), and the paraxial rim limit is 45°. The oblique beams
are vignetted: a meridional trace of this model passes about 78 % of the stop diameter at 20°, 68 % at 25° and 55 %
at 30½°. The field is limited to the printed 30½° without changing any rim.

The 8.0 mm rims are an estimate, not a measurement. Fig. 144 is drawn to scale: its vertex spacings, scaled from the
18.8 mm total length, agree with the table to within about 0.2 mm, and the two horizontal lines drawn in front of the
lens sit 7.3 mm from the axis, the entrance-pupil radius at 1:6.9. On that scale the cells are drawn to a clear radius
of about 9.2 mm, where L2 nearly runs out to its knife edge, and the stop opening to about 7.0 mm. Rims of 9.2 mm
would raise the meridional transmission at 30½° to about 75 %.

## Design Heritage and Context

The patent is context here, not the source of the numbers in the file. According to the Google Patents text of
DE 90482 C ("Patentirt im Deutschen Reiche vom 22. Mai 1895 ab"), it prints one numerical example in its text: a
double objective of 150 mm focal length and 28 mm opening, with radii r1 = 49.4, r2 = 16.7 and r4 = 62.2 mm, glass
thicknesses of 2, 2.7 and 4.6 mm, the distance between the halves, and nD and nF for the three glasses. The text
layer drops r3 and the distance. The legible radii and thicknesses divided by about 1.515 give von Rohr's 32.6, 11.0,
41.1, 1.3, 1.8 and 3.0, and the nD values are his. The 28 mm opening becomes 18.5 mm at von Rohr's scale, close to
the 18.4 mm diameter Fig. 144 draws, though the text does not say whether it means the lens diameter or the working
aperture. The patent recommends the single cell chiefly as a landscape lens and claims the symmetrical pair as well.

The Collinear, Typus I had an earlier origin: Kaempfer's first trial lenses of that type went back to July 1892, and
the two firms agreed on joint manufacturing rights under Steinheil's patent 88 505 (p. 384). Typus II was
Voigtländer's attempt at a type of its own. Von Rohr reports that the workshop itself judged it, as its additional
patent 91 883 shows, suitable for correcting the spherical aberrations but less so for the astigmatic ones. Kaempfer
then tried to cross the halves of his two Collinear types so as to keep only their virtues; von Rohr found no
official name for that product. No introduction of the three-meniscus objective was known to him, and Voigtländer &
Sohn, Aktiengesellschaft, let both patents lapse; they were deleted on 26 September 1898 (p. 386).
