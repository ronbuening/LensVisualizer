# Steinheil Orthostigmat Type II 493mm f/6.2 — DE 88505 C

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** DE 88505 C (Kaiserliches Patentamt, Patentschrift Nr. 88505, Klasse 57: Photographie)<br>
**Effective date:** 28 November 1893 ("Patentirt im Deutschen Reiche vom 28. November 1893 ab")<br>
**Issued:** 4 September 1896; second edition 12 September 1899, the print transcribed here<br>
**Inventor:** Rudolf Steinheil (named as patentee, "Dr. Rudolf Steinheil in München"; the document names no firm)<br>
**Title:** *Objectiv für photographische Zwecke*<br>
**Example details:** the first of two unnumbered numerical examples, page 1 (f = 493 mm, 1:6.2); the second
(f = 476 mm, 1:4, other glasses) follows on page 2. The drawing sheet shows two unnumbered sections; the left one
matches this example.

The patent opens with the argument for the design. New glass types make it possible to build an objective from two
positive, equal or similar halves, each corrected on its own chromatically, spherically and astigmatically, so that
the whole objective carries all three corrections. Earlier symmetrical objectives could be corrected only for colour
and spherical aberration; adding astigmatic correction had led to unsymmetrical constructions. The astigmatic
correction, the patent says, can be made only at an interface that turns its concave side to the more strongly
refracting medium, the spherical correction only at one that turns its concave side to the weaker medium. A half
built from three cemented lenses can carry both: the first interface corrects astigmatism, the second spherical
aberration. The claim defines the half as a positive lens enclosed by a biconvex and a biconcave lens, both more
strongly refracting than the enclosed lens.

Von Rohr's *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (1899) prints this example reduced to f = 100 as
Fig. 143, "R. Steinheil: Orthostigmat. Typus II.", and calls it the generally known form of the universal lens. The
model transcribes the patent at its own scale; von Rohr's reduced table is a secondary check (see Design Heritage).
No production lens or fitting is tied to this exact example: the commercial series von Rohr lists were rated 1:6.8,
1:8 and 1:10, not 1:6.2, so the file carries no mount or format.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a symmetrical double lens: two identical cemented triplets face each other across a 50 mm air
space. Each half is 49 mm thick on the axis and the vertex length is 148 mm. The patent gives no stop position; the
model centres the stop in the air space, where von Rohr puts it and where the symmetry of the claim implies it.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Patent's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = +173.0 | air → 1.61003 | +0.00353 | outer collective face |
| r2 = −139.66 | 1.61003 → 1.51874 | +0.00065 | first interface, concave toward the denser L1: astigmatic correction |
| r3 = −77.805 | 1.51874 → 1.56370 | −0.00058 | second interface, concave toward the lighter L2: spherical correction |
| r4 = +202.47 | 1.56370 → air | −0.00278 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The rear half mirrors these four surfaces. The example traces to an EFL of 494.149 mm, 0.23 % above the stated
493 mm, with a paraxial back focus of 424.962 mm. Each half has a focal length of +838.8 mm, 1.70 times that of the
pair. The Petzval sum is 8.67 × 10⁻⁴ mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of 1153 mm or 2.33 f.

The patent prints its radii with signs that describe the face rather than a coordinate direction: + for the outer
faces R0 and R14, − for the faces R6 and R8 toward the centre, ± for the cemented faces. Read with the claim and the
drawing, they give the signs above.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Biconvex Positive, outer lens

nD = 1.61003, nF = 1.61759; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's heaviest baryta crown.
f = +129.8 mm.

The densest glass of the system carries most of the half's positive power at its outer face. Its rear face is the
first interface, where the index falls into L2 across a surface concave toward L1: the patent's astigmatic
correction. At the 52 mm rim the lens keeps an edge of 1.96 mm, the thin sliver the patent drawing shows. L6 is its
mirror image.

### L2 / L5 — Positive Meniscus, enclosed lens

nD = 1.51874, nF = 1.52561; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's crown with high
dispersion. f = +297.6 mm.

The lowest index of the three forms the thick enclosed lens the claim names, 25 mm on the axis. Both faces are
convex toward the stop. Its front interface with L1 lowers the index and collects; its rear interface r3, the most
strongly curved surface of the system, raises the index into L3 across a face concave toward L2 and disperses: the
patent's spherical correction. L5 mirrors it in the rear half.

### L3 / L4 — Biconcave Negative, inner lens

nD = 1.56370, nF = 1.57160; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's baryta light flint.
f = −99.2 mm.

The thin lens, 4 mm on the axis and 30.7 mm at the rim, closes each half toward the stop. Its stop-side face is the
main negative contribution to the Petzval sum. L4 is its mirror image.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent prints two index lines for each glass, nD and nF, and names no glass type.

| Glass | nD | nF | nF − nD | Elements | Von Rohr's name (p. 384) | ν̄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| first | 1.61003 | 1.61759 | 0.00756 | L1, L6 | Schwerstes Baryt-Crown (heaviest baryta crown) | 44.6 |
| second | 1.51874 | 1.52561 | 0.00687 | L2, L5 | Crown mit hoher Dispersion (crown with high dispersion) | 41.6 |
| third | 1.56370 | 1.57160 | 0.00790 | L3, L4 | Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | 39.2 |

Without nC no Abbe number can be formed, and the data file keeps nD and nF as printed with no catalog glass as a
proxy. The engine needs nC (or an Abbe number) for its chromatic tiers, so the model traces at nD alone and shows
no colour error; the chromatic correction the patent claims is not reproduced. Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD),
a dispersion figure toward the photographically active G′ line, given here only as context. The relative D–F
dispersion of the printed values, (nF − nD)/(nD − 1), runs 0.0124, 0.0132 and 0.0140 for the three glasses: the same
order as von Rohr's ν̄, with the dense outer crown the least dispersive and the light flint the most.

The index steps do the main work the patent describes. The dense crown against the light crown gives the collective
interface with a falling index; the light crown against the flint gives the dispersive interface with a rising
index. Von Rohr places this choice in his group 1aα, with the dispersion order ν̄M < ν̄N < ν̄H.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity prescription. The model is infinity-only: the last gap is the computed paraxial
back focus, and no focusing motion or close-focus spacing is modelled.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The patent rates the example at 1:6.2. The model opens the iris to that f-number at the traced EFL: an entrance-pupil
radius of 39.85 mm and a stop radius of 33.82 mm. The f/6.2 axial beam reaches 39.85 mm at r1 and 34.72 mm at r8,
inside the rims. Traced on axis, the longitudinal spherical aberration at f/6.2 is −0.44 mm at the rim of the
pupil, −2.15 mm at 0.85, −2.29 mm at 0.7 and −1.53 mm at 0.5; reduced to f = 100 the worst zone is −0.46 mm, which
agrees with von Rohr's statement that the zones stay within about −0.5 mm.

The patent prints no field angle. The model traces to ω = 28°, the half-field von Rohr computed for this example.
The chief ray passes there with heights of 36.4 mm at r1 and r8, and the rims cut 44 %, 46 % and 22 % of one half of
the meridional beam at r1, r2 and r3: ordinary rim vignetting for a symmetrical objective of this length. The
paraxial rim limit is 36.9°.

The clear radii, 52 mm on every surface, are measured from the patent drawing. Its left section is this example:
the drawn radii fit 181.5 mm for R0 (printed 173.0) and 207.6 mm for R6 (printed 202.47), where the second example
prints 229 and 490.196. The section is close to scale along the axis (1190 px for the 148 mm vertex length, the two
halves 389 and 401 px for 49 mm, the air space 400 px for 50 mm), and both halves end square at a semi-width of
419 px, 52.1 mm on the vertex-length scale and 51.0–52.8 mm on the scale of either half. That is 10.5 mm on von
Rohr's f = 100 scale, against the 10.0–10.2 mm his Fig. 143 draws.

## Design Heritage and Context

Von Rohr tells the history on pp. 382–385 of his book. Steinheil's first Orthostigmat, applied for on 25 March 1893,
collided with the Zeiss Series VIa and Goerz's Doppel-Anastigmat; the application was withdrawn and the firm made
that type in France. In November of the same year Rudolf Steinheil applied for the different type of this patent,
built from two cemented triplets and introduced as the Orthostigmat Type II. Von Rohr took its data from the abstract
of the English patent printed in The British Journal of Photography in 1896 and reports that the specification holds
two forms: the faster one (his Fig. 142, the patent's 476 mm example at 1:4) and the slower universal form modelled
here (Fig. 143). On the basis of patent 88 505 two series were sold in Germany at 1:6.8 and 1:10, and from the summer
of 1899 a 1:8 form as well.

Von Rohr reduces this example to f = 100 and prints r = 35.0, 28.3, 15.8 and 41.0, d = 4.1, 5.1 and 0.8, and a
cell-to-stop distance of 5.06 on each side, with the patent's three nD values, computed for 1:6.0 and ω = 28°. A
single reduction factor 100/f with f between 493.6 and 493.8 mm reproduces every value of his table after
rounding; the traced 494.15 mm misses r3 and d1 by less than 0.005 mm of the rounding boundary, and the stated
493 mm misses three values. His Tafel II curve 143a, computed with the unrounded data, gives the longitudinal
aberration at incidence heights of 8.30, 6.76 and 4.81 mm as about +0.05, −0.42 and −0.40 mm; this transcription
gives +0.04, −0.45 and −0.40 mm at the same reduced heights. His curve 143b shows the meridional field more curved
than the sagittal (−6.8 against −4.5 mm at 28°), which he reads as a separation of the halves that is slightly too
small.

The half can be read as two achromats sharing the middle lens: a new achromat outside (dense crown against a lighter
glass across a collective interface) and an old achromat toward the stop (light crown against a denser flint across
a dispersive interface). In the patent dispute E. von Höegh argued that the type follows from the Doppel-Anastigmat
cell by interchanging its lenses; von Rohr grants the derivation but notes that the patent office recognised an
inventive step for von Höegh, Steinheil and Kaempfer alike.

D. Kaempfer of Voigtländer & Sohn had found the type independently, with trial lenses dating back to July 1892. The
two firms agreed on a joint right to make the objective protected by patent 88 505, which Voigtländer sold as the
Collinear Type I in Series II at 1:6.3, III at 1:7.7 and IV at 1:12.5. The symmetrical layout keeps coma,
distortion and lateral colour small, so the design work lies in the spherical, chromatic and astigmatic correction
of the single half, which the patent states is corrected on its own.
