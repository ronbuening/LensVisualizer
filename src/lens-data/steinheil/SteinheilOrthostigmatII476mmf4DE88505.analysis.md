# Steinheil Orthostigmat Type II 476mm f/4 — DE 88505 C

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** DE 88505 C (Kaiserliches Patentamt, Patentschrift Nr. 88505, Klasse 57: Photographie)<br>
**Effective date:** 28 November 1893 ("Patentirt im Deutschen Reiche vom 28. November 1893 ab")<br>
**Issued:** 4 September 1896; second edition 12 September 1899, the print transcribed here<br>
**Inventor:** Rudolf Steinheil (named as patentee, "Dr. Rudolf Steinheil in München"; the document names no firm)<br>
**Title:** *Objectiv für photographische Zwecke*<br>
**Example details:** the second of two unnumbered numerical examples, page 2 (f = 476 mm, 1:4, X0 = 59.5 mm); the
first (f = 493 mm, 1:6.2, other glasses) is on page 1. The drawing sheet shows two unnumbered sections; the right one
matches this example.

The patent opens with the argument for the design. New glass types make it possible to build an objective from two
positive, equal or similar halves, each corrected on its own chromatically, spherically and astigmatically, so that
the whole objective carries all three corrections; earlier symmetrical objectives could be corrected only for colour
and spherical aberration, and adding astigmatic correction had led to unsymmetrical constructions. The astigmatic
correction can be made only at an interface that turns its concave side to the more strongly refracting medium, the
spherical correction only at one that turns its concave side to the weaker medium. A half of three cemented lenses
carries both: the first interface corrects astigmatism, the second spherical aberration. The claim defines the half
as a positive lens enclosed by a biconvex and a biconcave lens, both more strongly refracting than the enclosed lens.

The second example follows the first with "während bei einer Brennweite von 476 mm und einem Oeffnungsverhältniss
1:4 die Elemente ... die folgenden sind" and adds a line the first lacks, X0 = 59.5: the incidence height of the
computed marginal ray, which is exactly 476/8. The prescription traces to 478.38 mm, so X0 is 1:4.02 at the traced
focal length; the model opens to that value and keeps 476 mm and 1:4 as the patent's ratings.

Von Rohr's *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (1899) prints this example reduced to f = 100 as
Fig. 142, "R. Steinheil: Orthostigmat. Typus II.", computed for 1:4.02 and ω = 29°, and says the faster form has the
type of a portrait objective (p. 383). The model transcribes the patent at its own scale; von Rohr's reduced table is
a secondary check (see Design Heritage). No production lens or fitting is tied to this example: von Rohr knows of
commercial series only for the slower universal form, so the file carries no mount or format.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a symmetrical double lens: two identical cemented triplets face each other across a 50 mm air
space. Each half is 49 mm thick on the axis and the vertex length is 148 mm, the same frame as the first example. The
patent gives no stop position; the model centres the stop in the air space, where von Rohr puts it and where the
symmetry of the claim implies it.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Patent's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = +229 | air → 1.62356 | +0.00272 | outer collective face |
| r2 = −562.95 | 1.62356 → 1.50786 | +0.00021 | first interface, concave toward the denser L1: astigmatic correction |
| r3 = −142.03 | 1.50786 → 1.60304 | −0.00067 | second interface, concave toward the lighter L2: spherical correction |
| r4 = +490.196 | 1.60304 → air | −0.00123 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The rear half mirrors these four surfaces. The example traces to an EFL of 478.384 mm, 0.50 % above the stated
476 mm, with a paraxial back focus of 412.923 mm. Each half has a focal length of +844.5 mm, 1.77 times that of the
pair. The Petzval sum is 1.433 × 10⁻³ mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of 698 mm or 1.46 f, against 2.33 f in the slower first
example.

The patent prints its radii with signs that describe the face rather than a coordinate direction: + for the outer
faces R0 and R14, − for the faces R6 and R8 toward the centre, ± for the cemented faces. Read with the claim and the
drawing, they give the signs above. The inner radius 490.196 mm is the reciprocal of 0.00204, which suggests the
curvature was set directly.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Biconvex Positive, outer lens

nD = 1.62356, nF = 1.63498; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's baryta flint.
f = +263.6 mm.

The densest glass of the system carries most of the half's positive power at its outer face. Its rear face, almost
flat at 562.95 mm, is the first interface, where the index falls by 0.116 into L2 across a surface concave toward
L1: the patent's astigmatic correction. A flint as the outer collective is the notable choice of this form; the claim
asks only that the outer lenses refract more strongly than the enclosed one, and the first example meets it with a
dense baryta crown. At the 65 mm rim the lens keeps an edge of 6.8 mm. L6 is its mirror image.

### L2 / L5 — Positive Meniscus, enclosed lens

nD = 1.50786, nF = 1.51351; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's borosilicate crown.
f = +366.7 mm.

The low-index lens the claim calls the enclosed positive lens, 25 mm on the axis and convex toward the stop. Its rear
interface r3, the most strongly curved surface of the system, raises the index by 0.095 into L3 across a face concave
toward L2 and disperses: the patent's spherical correction. It is also the only low-dispersion glass of the cell.
L5 mirrors it in the rear half.

### L3 / L4 — Biconcave Negative, inner lens

nD = 1.60304, nF = 1.61428; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's ordinary light flint.
f = −182.2 mm.

The thin lens, 4 mm on the axis and 24.1 mm at the rim, closes each half toward the stop. Its stop-side face is the
strongest negative surface of the cell and with r3 supplies the negative contribution to the Petzval sum. L4 is its
mirror image.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent prints two index lines for each glass, nD and nF, and names no glass type.

| Glass | nD | nF | nF − nD | Elements | Von Rohr's name (p. 383) | ν̄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| first | 1.62356 | 1.63498 | 0.01142 | L1, L6 | Baryt-Flint (baryta flint) | 29.6 |
| second | 1.50786 | 1.51351 | 0.00565 | L2, L5 | Borosilikat-Crown (borosilicate crown) | 49.6 |
| third | 1.60304 | 1.61428 | 0.01124 | L3, L4 | Gewöhnliches Leichtflint (ordinary light flint) | 29.0 |

Without nC no Abbe number can be formed, and the data file keeps nD and nF as printed with no catalog glass as a
proxy. The engine needs nC (or an Abbe number) for its chromatic tiers, so the model traces at nD alone and shows no
colour error; the chromatic correction the patent claims is not reproduced. Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), a
dispersion figure toward the photographically active G′ line, given here only as context. The relative D–F
dispersion of the printed values, (nF − nD)/(nD − 1), is 0.0183, 0.0111 and 0.0186: the two flints almost equal and
the crown far below them, the same pattern as von Rohr's ν̄.

That pattern is what separates this form from the universal one. The first example's three glasses rise in
dispersion in step from the outer lens inward; here the crown sits between two flints of nearly equal dispersion,
ν̄M < ν̄N > ν̄H in von Rohr's notation, which he notes does not fit his classification of the type. Cut into two
achromats, he adds, the cell groups its glass pairs under the second possibility of Rudolph's anastigmatic principle.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity prescription. The model is infinity-only: the last gap is the computed paraxial
back focus, and no focusing motion or close-focus spacing is modelled.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The model opens the iris to 1:4.02 at the traced EFL: an entrance-pupil radius of 59.50 mm, the patent's X0, and a
stop radius of 52.52 mm in the engine's paraxial sizing; the real marginal ray crosses the stop plane at 53.59 mm.
The axial beam reaches 59.50 mm at r1 and 52.68 mm at r8, inside the rims. Traced on axis, the longitudinal
spherical aberration is +1.82 mm at the rim of the pupil, −0.05 mm at 0.85, −0.66 mm at 0.7 and −0.62 mm at 0.5; it
crosses zero at an incidence height of 50.9 mm, 1:4.7, in line with von Rohr's statement that the spherical
correction holds for about 1:4.5.

The patent prints no field angle. The model traces to ω = 29°, the half-field von Rohr computed for this example.
The chief ray passes there at 35.5 mm on r1 and r8, and the rims cut 38 %, 35 % and 26 % of one half of the
meridional beam at r1–r3 and 19–34 % at r6–r8: ordinary rim vignetting. The paraxial rim limit is 44.8°.

The clear radii, 65 mm on every surface, are measured from the patent drawing. Its right section is this example:
its deep junction is drawn near the 142 mm of r3 where the first example prints 77.8 mm. The section is close to scale
along the axis (1189 px for the 148 mm vertex length, the two halves 393 and 401 px for 49 mm, the air space 395 px
for 50 mm), and r1 and r3 come within about 20 % of the printed curves; the weak junction r2 and the inner faces r4
and r5, however, are drawn about twice as deep as their radii allow, so the section gives the rims but not the
curves. Both halves end square at a semi-width of 525 and 521 px, 65.4 and 64.9 mm on the vertex-length scale; the
rotated reproduction of the same sheet on the scan's page 3 gives 63.4 mm. 65 mm is 13.6 mm on von Rohr's f = 100
scale, against the 13.3–13.6 mm his Fig. 142 and Tafel I draw.

## Design Heritage and Context

Von Rohr tells the history on pp. 382–384 of his book. Steinheil's first Orthostigmat, conceived by A. Steinheil and
computed by his son, was applied for on 25 March 1893 and collided with the Zeiss Series VIa and Goerz's
Doppel-Anastigmat; the application was withdrawn and C. A. Steinheil Söhne made that type in France. In November of
the same year Rudolf Steinheil applied for the different type of this patent, built from two cemented triplets and
introduced as the Orthostigmat Type II. Von Rohr took its data from the abstract of the English patent printed in The
British Journal of Photography in 1896 and reports that the specification holds two forms: the slower universal lens
(his Fig. 143, the patent's 493 mm example) and the faster one modelled here (Fig. 142). He knows of commercial use
only for the slower form: two series at 1:6.8 and 1:10 sold in Germany under patent 88 505, and a 1:8 form offered
from the summer of 1899 (p. 384).

Von Rohr reduces this example to fD = 100 and prints r = 47.9, 117.6, 29.7 and 102.4, d = 4.2, 5.2 and 0.8, and a
cell-to-stop distance of 5.2 on each side, with the patent's three nD values. A single reduction factor 100/f with f
between 478.50 and 478.58 mm reproduces every value of his table after rounding; the traced 478.38 mm gives 117.68
and 102.47 for r2 and r4, 0.08 and 0.07 mm from his figures, and every other value. His curve 142a, computed at
incidence heights of 12.43, 10.13 and 7.13 mm on the reduced scale, shows the back focus at about +0.36, −0.14 and
−0.15 mm and the focal length at +0.15, −0.25 and −0.21 mm; this transcription gives +0.38, −0.06 and −0.15 mm and
+0.14, −0.19 and −0.20 mm. The outer and inner points agree within 0.02 mm; the middle zone is 0.06–0.08 mm less
undercorrected than his plotted point, which rounding cannot explain, since these are the unrounded values. He
reads the oblique curves 142b as showing that the design chiefly removed the astigmatic difference while leaving a
small negative field curvature, a clear gain over the older portrait systems off axis, and regrets having computed
rays of too great an inclination.

D. Kaempfer of Voigtländer & Sohn had found the type independently. The two firms agreed on a joint right to make the
objective protected by patent 88 505, which Voigtländer sold as the Collinear Type I (p. 384). Von Rohr describes no
production version of this fast portrait form. The symmetrical layout keeps coma, distortion and lateral colour
small, so the design work lies in the spherical, chromatic and astigmatic correction of the single half, which the
patent states is corrected on its own.
