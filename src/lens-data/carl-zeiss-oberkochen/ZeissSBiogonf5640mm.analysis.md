# Carl Zeiss S-Biogon 40mm f/5.6

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 3,997,247
**Application Number:** 559,585
**Filed:** 18 March 1975
**Granted:** 14 December 1976
**Priority:** German application 2412898, 18 March 1974
**Inventors:** Erhard Glatzel, Heinz Zajadatz
**Assignee:** Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung, Oberkochen, Germany (printed "Carl Zeiss-Stiftung")
**Title:** Unsymmetrical Wide Angle Lens
**Claims / examples:** 18 claims; 14 numerical examples
**Embodiment analyzed:** Example 11 (FIG. 2; the same table is reproduced as claim 15)

US 3,997,247 presents itself as an improvement on the same inventors' earlier US 3,833,290 (col. 1, ll. 4–10). It keeps the parent's five-component layout — two front menisci A and B, two condensing components C and D separated by a central vertex space CS that may hold a diaphragm, and a rear negative component E convex to the image (col. 1, l. 28 – col. 2, l. 30). The new teaching is a power distribution rule for the front pair and for the two air lenses that follow it (col. 2, l. 53 – col. 3, l. 13). Example 11 is a moderate-aperture member of the family: f/5.6, 2ω₀ = 63°, s′∞ = 0.35027 F (col. 11, ll. 1–25).

The production lens taken as the counterpart is the Carl Zeiss S-Biogon 5,6/40, a fixed-aperture lens for microfilm reduction and enlargement. The identification rests on third-party sources; no Zeiss data sheet, catalog page, or statement linking a patent example to the product has been located. The convergent evidence:

1. **Designers and date.** Cavina attributes a 40 mm f/5.6 S-Biogon to Glatzel working with Zajadatz in 1974, for the Zeiss Mikrobox microfiche recorder operating between 1:8 and 1:30. The inventors and the German priority year match.
2. **Aperture and field.** A forum post by a Zeiss staff member states that the S-Biogon 5,6/40 is optimized for a magnification of 1:30, at which its image circle of 52 mm covers a 32 × 45 mm field, the exposed field on 35 mm microfilm. In the modeled prescription at 1:30, a 52 mm image circle corresponds to an image-side chief-ray angle of 32.7°, close to the patent's 31.5° half field (2ω₀ = 63°), and the modeled semi-diameters pass the full stop-filled beam at that circle (largest ray height 0.975 of the semi-diameter, at surface 3). The post is a staff statement, not a Zeiss publication.
3. **Construction type.** Cavina describes the 40 mm f/5.6 as an orthodox Biogon whose main departure from the classical 90° model is a thin, sickle-shaped first element. Example 11's first element L1 is a thin meniscus with almost equal radii (f = +1475.6 mm).
4. **Mechanics.** Dealer descriptions agree on a fixed f/5.6 aperture with no iris, no focusing mount, and an M50 × 0.75 thread. This matches a fixed-focus, fixed-stop optical design such as Example 11, but does not discriminate between examples.

The correlation is limited. Examples 5, 6, and 7 (FIG. 2) and Example 10 (FIG. 4) are also f/5.6 designs with 62–66° fields, and none of the third-party sources reproduces a prescription. Example 11 is the selected embodiment, not a manufacturer-confirmed one. One dealer text attributes the lens to Charles G. Wynne for X-ray screening; that attribution is unsupported by any other source and is not adopted.

## Optical Architecture

Example 11 is an all-spherical eight-element, five-group Biogon-type wide-angle objective. Read front to rear, the component powers follow the sequence weak positive (A), negative (B), strong positive (C), positive (D), negative (E). The diaphragm space lies between C and D. Components A, B, and E all turn their concave surfaces toward that central space, as the patent requires (col. 2, ll. 7–14).

| Component | Elements | f (mm) | Power / Φ | Petzval term (× F) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | L1 | +1475.6 | +0.027 | −0.019 |
| B | L2 | −60.9 | −0.657 | −0.437 |
| C | L3a + L3b (cemented) | +30.6 | +1.309 | +0.856 |
| D | L4a + L4b + L4c (cemented) | +109.5 | +0.365 | +0.121 |
| E | L5 | −59.0 | −0.678 | −0.507 |

Focal lengths are standalone values in air at the 40 mm scale; Φ is the power of the whole lens. The two outer negative components B and E carry almost equal power, and almost all positive power sits in C. The construction is nevertheless unsymmetrical, as the patent title states: two menisci stand in front of the stop and one behind it, the front condensing component is a doublet while the rear one is a triplet, and D carries less than a third of C's power.

The patent table is normalized to F = 1.00000 (col. 5, ll. 16–19). The data file scales every radius, thickness, and the back focus by s = 40 mm/F. The computed focal length is 39.9996 mm. The authored back focus is the patent's s′∞ × 40 = 14.0108 mm; the paraxial focus of the scaled table is 14.0106 mm, a difference within the rounding of the printed table. The vertex length is 109.800 mm and the first vertex lies 123.811 mm ahead of the infinity image plane.

The lens is neither a telephoto nor a retrofocus design: the overall length is 3.095 times the focal length, and the back focus is 0.350 of it. The front principal plane lies 50.57 mm behind the first vertex and the rear principal plane 25.99 mm ahead of the last vertex.

The patent does not fix the stop position inside CS. The data file places the stop 2.06 mm behind L3b, about 0.68 of the 3.025 mm space, from the iris marks in FIG. 2 — a figure the patent says is not to scale (col. 4, ll. 31–43). The stop semi-diameter (3.757 mm) is calibrated so the paraxial f-number equals the patent's f/5.6; it is not a published diaphragm size. With that stop, the entrance pupil lies 51.46 mm behind the first vertex and the exit pupil 25.12 mm ahead of the last vertex, with a pupil magnification of 0.978. The near-unit pupil magnification is typical of the Biogon arrangement: the real chief ray leaves the lens at 32.0° for a 31.5° object field angle.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### Component A — L1 (Positive Meniscus, convex to object)

nd = 1.64769, νd = 33.86. Glass: SF2 (Schott) — dense flint. f = +1475.6 mm.

L1 is a 5.14 mm shell with radii of 30.57 and 29.49 mm, so its power is only +0.027Φ. The front pair is dispersing only in combination: Φ_AB = −0.581Φ, almost all of it from B, and Φ_A/Φ_B = −0.041. This places the patent's power distribution number Ψ_BA at +0.921, close to the value of +1 that would correspond to a powerless A (col. 2, ll. 53–65). The patent allows A to carry either sign; in Example 11 it is a nearly afocal meniscus that bends the oblique bundles before B rather than adding power.

The air space between L1 and L2 forms the patent's air lens α. Its surface-power sum is θα = −0.675Φ, a strongly diverging air lens.

### Component B — L2 (Negative Meniscus, convex to object)

nd = 1.51454, νd = 54.68. Glass: KF3 class (Sumita; historical supplier unconfirmed). f = −60.9 mm.

L2 is a thin meniscus (1.79 mm) that provides the front negative power. Its rear surface (R 23.77 mm, concave to the stop) is followed by the long air space β (30.92 mm) to component C. The surface-power sum of that air lens is θβ = +0.121Φ, so β is weakly converging in the patent's surface-power sense. B supplies the second-largest negative Petzval term (−0.437 per F) and, in the third-order breakdown below, the largest barrel-distortion term of any component.

### Component C — L3a + L3b (cemented doublet, front condensing component)

- **L3a — Negative Meniscus, convex to object.** nd = 1.71736, νd = 29.52. Glass: SF1 (Schott) — dense flint. f = −44.0 mm.
- **L3b — Biconvex Positive.** nd = 1.71300, νd = 53.85. Glass: N-LAK8 (Schott); LaK8-type lanthanum crown. f = +13.6 mm.

C carries +1.309Φ and is the main positive component. L3a is a thick (17.86 mm) dense-flint meniscus whose front surface (R 29.07 mm) is the strongest converging air–glass surface ahead of the stop. L3b, a thick biconvex lanthanum crown, provides the doublet's positive power.

The junction (surface 6, R 11.25 mm, convex to the object) is the doublet's most distinctive feature. The two glasses differ in nd by only −0.00436, so the surface has almost no power in d light (−0.016Φ). Their dispersions differ greatly, however, so the first-order F−C power of the same surface is −0.039Φ, about 2.5 times its d-line power. The junction therefore changes the paths of rays of different colors far more than it changes the d-line ray paths. The patent asks for the lower index on the rear element of C (col. 2, ll. 17–21; claim 3); Example 11 satisfies that preference by the small margin of 1.71300 against 1.71736. The patent does not state why.

C is the largest positive contributor to the Petzval sum (+0.856 per F) and the largest third-order spherical-aberration contributor (−168 µm transverse at f/5.6).

### Component D — L4a + L4b + L4c (cemented triplet, rear condensing component)

- **L4a — Biconcave Negative.** nd = 1.54883, νd = 45.43. Glass: LLF7 class (Sumita; historical supplier unconfirmed). f = −14.7 mm.
- **L4b — Biconvex Positive.** nd = 1.61772, νd = 49.78. Glass: N-SSK8 (Schott); SSK8-type. f = +9.4 mm.
- **L4c — Negative Meniscus, convex to image.** nd = 1.71300, νd = 53.85. Glass: N-LAK8 (Schott); LaK8-type lanthanum crown. f = −24.8 mm.

L4b has the shortest standalone focal length in the lens, but the triplet as a whole carries only +0.365Φ. The two flanking negative elements cancel most of L4b's power.

The two junctions behave in the opposite way to surface 6. Surface 9 (L4a to L4b, R 10.85 mm, convex to the stop) has a d-line power of +0.254Φ and surface 10 (L4b to L4c, R −9.26 mm) one of −0.412Φ. Because the dispersions of the glasses on either side are close, their first-order F−C powers are only +0.001Φ and −0.004Φ. The cemented surfaces of D therefore shape the d-line ray paths with almost no chromatic side effect. Surface 9 is the cemented surface convex toward CS that the patent requires in D, with the lower index on its front side (col. 2, ll. 22–30; claim 4).

D's most visible third-order role is coma balance with C: the tangential coma contributions of C and D (+623 and −620 µm at full field and full aperture) cancel almost exactly about the stop. That figure depends on the modeled stop position.

### Component E — L5 (Negative Meniscus, convex to image)

nd = 1.46450, νd = 65.70. Glass: FK3 (Schott, inquiry glass). f = −59.0 mm.

L5 is the rear negative meniscus required by the patent (col. 2, ll. 1–6). It is separated from D by the air space γ, whose surface-power sum is θγ = −0.267Φ; the patent names γ but sets no condition on it. With −0.678Φ, E matches B in power and supplies the largest negative Petzval term (−0.507 per F). For a given power, the Petzval term of a surface varies as 1/(n·n′), so the low-index fluor crown places more field-flattening Petzval correction in this element than a higher-index glass would.

The Petzval terms of all components sum to +0.01455 per F, a Petzval radius of −68.7 F (−2749 mm at 40 mm).

## Glass Identification

The patent publishes only nd and νd at the helium d-line (col. 6, ll. 1–15); no line indices or partial dispersions are given. The labels below are the data file's labels. They rest on coordinate matches against vendor catalogs and two legacy lists; a matching coordinate does not prove the supplier or melt.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | 1.64769 / 33.86 | SF2 (Schott) | current Schott SF2, coordinate-exact | nearly afocal front shell |
| L2 | 1.51454 / 54.68 | 515547 — KF3-type crown-flint | legacy Schott-type KF3 list entry, close | front negative meniscus |
| L3a | 1.71736 / 29.52 | SF1 (Schott) | current Schott SF1, coordinate-exact | flint of front doublet |
| L3b, L4c | 1.71300 / 53.85 | N-LAK8 (Schott); LaK8-type | current Schott N-LAK8, coordinate-exact | positive crown of C; rear member of D |
| L4a | 1.54883 / 45.43 | 549454 — LLF7-type extra-light flint | legacy Schott-type LLF7 list entry, coordinate-exact | front member of D |
| L4b | 1.61772 / 49.78 | N-SSK8 (Schott); SSK8-type | current Schott N-SSK8, coordinate-exact | main positive of D |
| L5 | 1.46450 / 65.70 | FK3 (Schott, inquiry glass) | Schott FK3 (vendor AGF via mirror), close | rear negative meniscus |

All seven coordinates fit one set of classical Schott glass types (SF2, KF3, SF1, LaK8, LLF7, SSK8, FK3), consistent with the patent's statement that ordinary glasses are used throughout (col. 3, ll. 20–24). The index range is 1.4645–1.71736 and the Abbe range 29.52–65.70.

The chromatic arrangement uses the cemented surfaces in two different ways. In C, a near-index-matched junction between a dense flint and a lanthanum crown acts mainly on color. In D, dispersion-matched junctions act mainly on d-line ray paths. A first-order calculation from nd and νd alone gives a paraxial F−C focus difference of −0.069 mm, with the F focus nearer the lens. Second-order terms, which depend on where nd falls between nC and nF, move this between −0.054 and −0.084 mm. That spread is comparable to the d-line quarter-wave depth of focus at f/5.6 (±0.037 mm). Lateral color is −0.009 mm at full field. The patent does not state the spectral band for which Example 11 was balanced, and the data file carries no line indices, so neither secondary spectrum nor spherochromatism is evaluated and no apochromatic claim is made.

## Focus Mechanism

The production lens has no focusing mechanism and no iris; it is held at a fixed distance by the apparatus in which it is mounted. The patent publishes only the infinity state.

The data file adds a calculated close state at a reduction of 1:30, the working ratio reported by third-party sources. It is modeled as a translation of the whole lens: only the image-side gap changes. It is not a patent state or a manufacturer-published state.

## Conditional Expressions

The patent defines two power distribution numbers. Ψ_BA = (Φ_A + Φ_B)/(Φ_B − Φ_A) for the front components (col. 2, ll. 53–65). Ψβα = (θβ − θα)/(θβ + θα) for the two air lenses, where each θ is the sum of the powers (n′ − n)/R of the two surfaces enclosing the air space (col. 3, ll. 1–13; col. 12). The claim limits are the extreme values of Tables I and II widened by 5% (col. 13, ll. 25–41).

The specification's own wording of feature 4 gives the Φ_AB range as 1.19 to 0.24 times Φ (col. 1, ll. 56–62); claim 2 uses the tolerance-widened limits. Example 11 satisfies both.

## Aberration Correction Strategy

The patent explains its two features in aberration terms. Feature (a), the front-pair power distribution, is said to reduce the zonal residual of spherical aberration. Feature (b), the air-lens proportion, is said to reduce the asymmetry of coma in strongly inclined bundles. Both are part of a front design that passes the incoming rays nearly free of aberration (col. 3, ll. 32–68). The patent also says that a weak front dispersing power avoids the strong negative front groups of earlier designs, whose Φ_AB lay between −1.21Φ and −2.40Φ (col. 3, ll. 39–55).

The table gives the third-order contributions of each component, computed from the modeled prescription. Values are transverse at the image, at f/5.6 and, where a field is involved, at 31.5°. Spherical aberration and axial color do not depend on the stop position; coma, distortion, and lateral color depend on the modeled stop.

| Component | Spherical (µm) | Tangential coma (µm) | Distortion (%) | Axial color F−C (µm) | Lateral color F−C (µm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | −2 | +41 | +2.78 | −6.3 | +83 |
| B | +40 | −303 | −13.65 | +36.5 | −216 |
| C | −168 | +623 | +12.24 | −63.9 | +209 |
| D | +87 | −620 | −8.67 | +17.4 | −132 |
| E | +32 | +245 | +7.12 | +10.1 | +47 |
| Total | −11 | −13 | −0.18 | −6.2 | −9 |

Three patterns stand out:

- **Spherical aberration.** The front pair contributes much less than either condensing component, consistent with the patent's description of the front of the lens.
- **Coma.** C and D cancel each other almost exactly.
- **Distortion.** Distortion is the residue of large opposing terms: the negative meniscus B and the positive triplet D produce barrel distortion, while C, E, and A produce pincushion. The exact chief-ray trace gives −0.072% at 0.6 of the image height and −0.184% at 31.5°.

Higher-order spherical aberration is strongly balanced against third order. Third order alone would place the f/5.6 rim focus 0.121 mm short of the paraxial focus. The real rim ray falls only 0.005 mm short, and the largest residual, −0.035 mm, occurs in the zone at 0.74 of the aperture radius. This is the zonal residual that feature (a) is said to control. On axis, the d-line wavefront error at best focus (about 0.03 mm ahead of the paraxial focus) is 0.029 λ peak-to-valley and 0.006 λ RMS. That is well inside Rayleigh's quarter-wave criterion and supports the patent's claim of near-Rayleigh-limited imaging (col. 3, ll. 25–31) for the axial monochromatic image only; off-axis and polychromatic wavefronts are not evaluated.

The field is flat to within a few hundredths of a millimeter. Measured from the paraxial image plane with real rays through the modeled stop:

| Field angle | 10° | 20° | 25° | 28° | 31.5° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chief-ray image height (mm) | 7.05 | 14.55 | 18.63 | 21.24 | 24.47 |
| Tangential focus (µm) | −16 | −18 | +7 | +20 | 0 |
| Sagittal focus (µm) | −12 | −39 | −51 | −57 | −61 |

The 24 × 36 mm diagonal (21.63 mm image height) is reached at a full field of 56.9°, inside the patent's 63°.

The modeled pupil also shows the property Bertele's Biogon patent identified as favorable for illumination toward the field edge. At 31.5°, the cross-section of the beam that fills the modeled stop is 0.955 of its on-axis area; a fixed flat pupil would shrink to 0.853. Combined with the image mapping, this gives a geometric relative illumination of about 0.60 at the field edge, against 0.53 for cos⁴. The estimate is geometric: it ignores transmission losses and any vignetting by the production barrel; the modeled semi-diameters themselves pass the full beam. The descriptions of the production lens differ on this point: the Zeiss staff member describes the field illumination as exceptionally even, with no center filter required, whereas Cavina reports a fall-off of about one stop at the edges in the Mikrobox application and the routine use of a concentric neutral-density filter. The geometric estimate, about −0.75 EV at 31.5°, does not resolve that difference, because it depends on the modeled stop and omits barrel vignetting.

### Departure from the parent patent

Claim 1 of US 3,833,290 requires a strongly dispersing air lens β between B and C (θβ between −1.200Φ and −0.490Φ), a combined α + β value between −1.400Φ and −0.605Φ, and a sum of the first and last surface powers between 1.290Φ and 1.970Φ. Example 11 has θβ = +0.121Φ and θα + θβ = −0.554Φ, both outside the parent's ranges, while its outer-surface sum of 1.389Φ lies inside. The 1975 patent replaces the parent's strongly dispersing β with the Ψβα proportion, and its abstract names the air-lens power distribution as one of the points of difference. The parent was written for high-aperture (f/2.8–f/3.5) designs; Example 11 is the moderate-aperture case the newer patent describes as "medium aperture" (col. 3, l. 26).

## Design Heritage and Context

The five-component scheme of Example 11 follows Ludwig Bertele's Biogon patent US 2,721,499 (Swiss priority 12 July 1951; granted 25 October 1955). That patent describes two dispersing front menisci convex to the object, two converging multi-element components with the iris between them, and a final dispersing meniscus convex to the image. It already prefers, in the rear converging component, a cemented surface convex to the object with the lower index on its convex side — the arrangement that reappears as claim 4 of US 3,997,247. That patent addresses fields of more than 90°; Example 11 applies the scheme to a 63° field at f/5.6.

The intermediate step is US 3,833,290, "High power wide-angle lens" (filed 27 November 1972, granted 3 September 1974, assigned to Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung). Its printed inventors are Glatzel, Zajadatz, and Bertele, which accounts for the "third joint inventor" mentioned in the cross-reference of US 3,997,247. That patent concentrated on high-aperture variants with a strongly dispersing air lens between B and C. The 1975 patent moves the same family toward moderate aperture and very high resolution, with a nearly powerless first element.

Zeiss describes Glatzel as a leading mathematician and manager of its Oberkochen optical design department, who received the Apollo Achievement Award for special camera lenses designed for space photography.
