## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** GB 1,066,527 A
**Title:** Photographic Wide-Angle and Semi-Wide-Angle Objective of extremely High Light-Gathering Power
**Filed:** 29 May 1964 (priority: German application Z 10151, 31 May 1963)
**Published:** 26 April 1967
**Inventor:** Erhard Glatzel (named on the German family member DE 1,447,288; the GB specification names no inventor)
**Applicant:** Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung, trading as Carl Zeiss
**Example details:** Table I (printed page 2) and Fig. 1; printed at f = 1 and scaled ×35

The patent gives six examples. Table I is the f/1.8, ±31° nine-element version. Its identification with the Contarex Distagon 35mm f/2 rests on four points:

1. Construction: nine elements in eight groups, with two negative front menisci, a thick positive element, a thin positive meniscus, the iris, a biconcave negative element, a cemented meniscus pair and two positive singlets. The section drawing in Zeiss Ikon's (undated) Contarex booklet shows the same arrangement, and Marco Cavina gives nine elements in eight groups. Pacific Rim Camera's table lists ten elements in nine groups.
2. Designer and timing: Cavina attributes the lens to Glatzel and dates his German filing to 31 May 1963, the priority date of this patent; the lens was marketed from 1965.
3. Aperture and field: Cavina reports that the computed f/1.8 was rounded to f/2 for sale; the marketed 63° field is close to the patent's ±31°.
4. Glass: LIII is the LaK9 lanthanum crown that Cavina names for the production lens.

The other examples fit less well: Tables II and III are f/1.9 and f/2 versions with different glass arrangements, Tables IV and V have eight elements, and Table VI is an f/1.4 design for ±28°. Cavina also notes that production used a thicker LIII, with the two following elements shifted, so the model is the patented design rather than the factory prescription.

Every length is multiplied by exactly 35, which gives a paraxial focal length of 35.001 mm; nothing is refitted to the marketed f/2. The claims (page 8) repeat Table I but print r2 = 0.06827f. The main table's 0.6827f is used: the Δn/r column of both tables (−0.88338/f) and the printed back focus agree with it, and 0.06827f would give a focal length of 0.37f.

## Optical Architecture

Nine elements in eight air-separated groups form an all-spherical retrofocus design. Table I and Fig. 1 name the elements LI to LIX from the front. The patent divides it into a front portion of negative menisci, concave toward the image, and a positive main portion at least 0.1f behind it (d4 = 0.3698f here). In the main portion a two-element positive group (LIII, LIV) precedes the element of highest negative power (LV), which is followed by three positive components: the cemented LVI–LVII meniscus, LVIII and LIX. The separated negative front portion yields the printed back focus of 1.0569f (paraxially 1.0570f); the patent asks for at least 0.8f so the lens suits single-lens reflex cameras. LVI and LVII share the only cemented interface.

Table I meets each design rule the specification states: LIII is 0.2475f thick (preferred range 0.2f–0.8f, for a flatter sagittal field); LIV has 0.22 times the power of LIII (limit 0.4); the front surface of LV supplies 37 % of that element's negative power (at least a quarter); and the air space between LV and the cemented component is 0.1128f (under 0.25f, for residual distortion).

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### LI: Negative Meniscus

nd = 1.60311, νd = 60.68 (SK14, Schott), f = −75.1 mm. The outer front meniscus, concave toward the image, begins the retrofocus expansion of the beam.

### LII: Negative Meniscus

nd = 1.62041, νd = 60.29 (SK16, Schott), f = −172.7 mm. A weaker second meniscus shares the front portion's negative power. The 12.9 mm air space behind it separates the front and main portions.

### LIII: Biconvex Positive

nd = 1.69100, νd = 54.80 (LaK9, Schott), f = +33.8 mm. The thick collector (8.66 mm) carries most of the positive power ahead of the iris; its thickness is the patent's lever on the sagittal field.

### LIV: Positive Meniscus

nd = 1.62041, νd = 60.29 (SK16, Schott), f = +151.0 mm. A weak meniscus, concave toward the image, which the patent uses mainly to correct lateral spherical aberration and coma. The iris follows in the 6.5 mm air space behind it.

### LV: Biconcave Negative

nd = 1.58144, νd = 40.80 (LF5, Schott), f = −30.5 mm. The thin biconcave element just behind the iris has the highest negative power in the lens. The patent limits the power of its front surface for the zonal part of the spherical aberration.

### LVI–LVII: Cemented Negative–Positive Pair

LVI: nd = 1.75520, νd = 27.53 (SF4, Schott), f = −38.2 mm. LVII: nd = 1.62041, νd = 60.29 (SK16, Schott), f = +31.9 mm. Together, f = +139.1 mm. The pair is a meniscus convex toward the image, the first of the three positive components that follow LV. The SF4 flint cemented to the SK16 crown at surface 12 corrects colour behind the iris, together with the LF5 flint of LV.

### LVIII: Biconvex Positive

nd = 1.62041, νd = 60.29 (SK16, Schott), f = +73.9 mm. Nearly plano-convex (r14 = 14.87f); the strongest of the two rear singlets.

### LIX: Biconvex Positive

nd = 1.62041, νd = 60.29 (SK16, Schott), f = +132.8 mm. The last positive element ahead of the 37.0 mm back focus. The patent lists no filter or cover plate.

## Glass Identification and Selection

Table I prints nd and νd at the d line. Every pair matches a Schott catalog glass exactly in nd and within 0.1 in νd: SK14 (LI), SK16 (LII, LIV, LVII, LVIII, LIX), LaK9 (LIII), LF5 (LV) and SF4 (LVI). The design predates Schott's lead-free glasses; the dispersion model uses N-SK14, N-SK16 and N-LAK9, which have the same nd. Five of the nine elements share the SK16 crown, and the only flints are LF5 in LV and SF4 in LVI. No anomalous-dispersion glass is used.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent prints only the infinity prescription, so focus travel is not modeled and the model stays at infinity. Zeiss Ikon's Contarex booklet gives a closest focus of 9 in (0.23 m) from the film plane; Cavina gives 0.22 m.

## Model Scope and Limitations

The patent prints no iris position; Fig. 1 draws the iris in the air space d8, about 55 % of the way back, and the model places it at the midpoint, 46.66 mm behind the first vertex (d8 spans 43.39–49.93 mm). Semi-diameters are not printed either; they are estimated from Fig. 1. LV and LIX follow the figure, and LV's front face also passes the real-ray f/1.8 axial beam. LII, LIII and the cemented pair are drawn 5–21 % smaller than the model carries them, and LI is drawn at 18.3 mm in front and 16.0 mm behind against the model's 20 and 18.7 mm; at the drawn rims of LII, LIII and the pair the model would lose up to 29 points of off-axis light at 20°.

The image plane is at the printed back focus, 105.70 mm behind the first vertex; the paraxial focus lies 0.003 mm further back, and the best axial focus at f/1.8 about 0.13 mm closer to the lens. The f-number follows the paraxial convention (entrance-pupil diameter = f/N). Because the strongly negative front portion bends the marginal rays, the real beam the iris admits at the f/1.8 setting corresponds to about f/1.92.

Vignetting at the edge of the patent's field is strong with these semi-diameters: at 31° about 46 % of the full-aperture beam passes at f/1.8 and 54 % at f/2, against 98 % and 100 % at 15.5°. These are sampled pupil fractions, not measured illumination. All MTF and chromatic figures come from the d-line prescription and catalog glass curves, not from production tolerances or coatings.
