# Carl Zeiss Biogon 35mm f/2.8 (post-war Contax) — JP S30-4837 B

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** JP S30-4837 B (特許出願公告 昭30-4837, examined application publication)
**Application Number:** 特願昭26-15481
**Filed:** 1951-11-22 (Japan)
**Priority:** 1950-11-25 (Germany)
**Published:** 1955-07-14
**Inventor:** Ludwig Bertele (Heerbrugg, Switzerland)
**Applicant:** Carl Zeiss (Brenz/Heidenheim, Germany), cataloged under Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung
**Title:** 対物レンズ (Objective lens)
**Embodiment analyzed:** Example 1 (the publication's single numerical example)

The publication describes a photographic or projection objective of four members separated by air: a simple collective first member, a cemented second member bent strongly toward the object, a cemented collective third member, and a cemented dispersive meniscus as the fourth member (p. 1 ¶2–¶3; claim, p. 2). The single example is tabulated for a focal length of 100 units, with an aperture ratio of 1:2.8 and an image angle of 62° (p. 1 ¶8; p. 2, line below the table). No stop position, clear apertures, back focus or close-focus state is published.

The example is correlated with the seven-element, four-group Biogon 1:2.8 f = 35 mm built at Oberkochen for the post-war Contax IIa and IIIa. No primary source states that this example is the production prescription; the identification rests on convergent evidence:

1. **Construction.** Four air-separated members containing seven elements (1 + 2 + 2 + 2) match the seven-element production lens listed by Zeiss Ikon and the four-group construction reported by third-party sources.
2. **Aperture and field.** The patent's 1:2.8 and 62° image angle correspond to the catalog's f/2.8 and 63° angle of view. At the implemented focal length of 34.75 mm the 24 × 36 mm diagonal subtends 63.8°.
3. **Inventor, applicant and timing.** Third-party histories attribute the post-war Biogon to Bertele, the applicant is Carl Zeiss, and the 1950-11-25 German priority coincides with the lens's reported 1950 introduction.
4. **Stated mechanical benefit.** The patent states that its construction lengthens the distance from the last lens surface to the image and reduces the free diameter of the last surface, easing mounting in the camera (p. 1 ¶4). A third-party account describes the post-war lens as having a shorter rear element than its pre-war predecessor.

**Source correction.** As printed, the table does not describe an f = 100 objective: it traces to an effective focal length of 288.67 units. The prescription file therefore uses r6 = −4205, read as a misplaced decimal point, which gives 99.291 units. Of 1,034 single edits to the printed table (each one digit substitution, sign change, adjacent transposition or decimal-point shift of a radius, spacing or index), only two bring the focal length within ±1 unit of 100. The nearer, r9 = −73.00, gives 100.04 units but leaves the third-order spherical, coma and astigmatism sums about twelve times larger than the r6 correction does (root-sum-square 1.19 against 0.095, each table scaled to f = 100 at f/2.8 and 31° half-field). It also leaves the printed r6 in place, whose sag would cut into the iris position drawn in the figure. The r6 correction gives design-like third-order sums and makes the front of L4 the nearly plane surface that the figure draws: −1471.75 mm after scaling, against −14.72 mm as printed. The remaining shortfall of 0.7 % from the stated f = 100 is retained, not tuned away; r6 = −2031 would give exactly 100 units but is not a single-glyph misprint.

The final radius is faint. It is read as printed, r11 = −133.9: its third digit matches the table's own 3s (open upper bowl, no ink at the left waist) rather than its 8s. The alternative reading −138.9 would raise the corrected focal length to 99.853 but has no support in the glyph itself and is not used.

**Scaling and inferred quantities.** Every radius and thickness is scaled by s = 0.35 (nominal f = 100 → 35 mm), giving a computed focal length of 34.75 mm; indices, Abbe numbers and the all-spherical form are unchanged. The aperture stop is placed 0.90 of the way across the r5–r6 air space, measured from the patent figure. Its semi-diameter, 4.3224 mm, is calibrated to reproduce f/2.8 and is not independent evidence. Clear semi-diameters are inferred from real ray bundles and the optical figure, not published values. The r7 cemented junction ends at 4.6 mm; the larger 6.55 mm rim belongs to L5 beyond the cemented contact. The stepped drawing does not support extending L4 to that outer height.

## Optical Architecture

The design is a four-member objective with the power sequence positive–negative–positive–negative. Computed member focal lengths are +48.30 mm (M1, L1), −70.63 mm (M2, L2+L3), +20.70 mm (M3, L4+L5) and −42.86 mm (M4, L6+L7). Normalized to the system power, the members carry 0.72, −0.49, 1.68 and −0.81. The thick cemented third member is the principal collective group.

The patent places the novelty in the fourth member (p. 1 ¶2–¶3). It describes the first three members as an objective of a known type, able to reach image angles of 45° to 50°. Widening the angle requires a thinner second member. The rise in Petzval sum that accompanies this must then be compensated by a fourth member curved toward the image. In earlier objectives of this kind that fourth member was not cemented. Here it is a positive lens followed by a negative lens, cemented on a surface strongly curved toward the image, with the positive lens of higher index. According to the patent, this shortens the objective behind the second air space from about 0.69 of the focal length in the known type (p. 1 ¶3).

The stop lies in the r5–r6 air space, 9.20 mm behind the first vertex. The concave rear face of M2 (r5) on one side and the almost flat front of M3 (r6) on the other bound it. The vertex length from r1 to r11 is 25.52 mm, and the paraxial back focal distance from r11 is 15.78 mm (0.45 of the focal length). The total length to the image is 41.30 mm, giving TL/EFL = 1.19. A telephoto requires TL/EFL below 1 and a retrofocus requires a back focal distance longer than the focal length, so the design is neither.

The paraxial entrance pupil, 6.21 mm in radius, lies 9.69 mm behind r1. The exit pupil, 5.00 mm in radius, lies 12.19 mm in front of r11, or 27.97 mm from the image plane. The pupil magnification is 0.80, so the stop is imaged at similar size on both sides.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Positive Meniscus, convex to object (Member I)

nd = 1.6177, νd = 55.1. Glass: K-SSK4 (Sumita) / SSK4 class (618551). f = +48.30 mm.

The patent's "simple collective lens" (p. 1 ¶2). It is weak relative to the system (normalized power 0.72) and is the only uncemented element. Its strongly convex front surface (r1, normalized power 1.24) is followed by a weakly concave rear (r2) separated from M2 by a thin air space (l1, 0.098 mm after scaling). In the third-order decomposition at full aperture and full field, r2 and r5 carry the two largest distortion terms of one sign, and the near-plane r6 behind the stop carries the largest term of the opposite sign.

### L2 + L3 — Cemented Member II (negative meniscus group, bent toward object)

**L2 — Biconvex Positive.** nd = 1.6645, νd = 35.9. Glass: J-BASF2 class (Hikari; supplier unconfirmed) (665359). f = +12.44 mm.

**L3 — Biconcave Negative.** nd = 1.7400, νd = 28.2. Glass: SF3 (Schott) (740282). f = −8.73 mm.

The claim requires the collective lens of this member to have a lower index than its dispersive partner (1.6645 < 1.7400). Both glasses are flint-type dispersions (νd < 50). The two standalone elements are strong, but they combine to a weak negative member (−70.63 mm) because the junction r4 is shallow and carries only a 0.0755 index step. The member's outer surfaces do the work. The convex front r3 (normalized power 2.24) and the deeply concave rear r5 (−3.23, the strongest surface in the system) form a thick negative meniscus facing the stop.

r5 supplies the largest negative Petzval contribution of any surface (−1.86 in units of 1/f). In the third-order decomposition it also carries the largest coma and astigmatism terms of any surface. The member's axial glass thickness, 12.89 patent units (4.51 mm scaled), equals 0.130 f and satisfies the 0.08–0.18 f range of 附記 4.

### L4 + L5 — Cemented Member III (collective, junction convex to object)

**L4 — Biconcave Negative.** nd = 1.4645, νd = 65.7. Glass: FK3 class (Schott; historical supplier unconfirmed). f = −25.34 mm.

**L5 — Biconvex Positive.** nd = 1.6204, νd = 60.3. Glass: N-SK16 (Schott) / SK16 class (620603). f = +12.77 mm.

L4 is almost plano-concave. Its front (r6) has a normalized power of only −0.011, so nearly all of its power comes from the cemented junction r7 (radius +11.8685 mm), which is convex toward the object. The index rises from 1.4645 to 1.6204 across r7, as the claim requires ("markedly lower index" in the dispersive lens). The surface therefore acts as a collective cemented surface.

L5 is the thickest element (10.05 mm). With L4 it forms the strongest member, M3 (+20.70 mm). M3 carries the largest positive member Petzval term (+1.01 in units of 1/f) and the largest member distortion term, opposite in sign to those of M1 and M2; the other members offset both.

The printed r6 = −42.05 would make L4 a strongly curved biconcave lens immediately behind the stop. The corrected value makes it the near-plane surface that the figure draws.

### L6 + L7 — Cemented Member IV (dispersive meniscus, bent toward image)

**L6 — Positive Meniscus, concave to object.** nd = 1.6204, νd = 60.3. Glass: N-SK16 (Schott) / SK16 class (620603). f = +43.45 mm.

**L7 — Negative Meniscus.** nd = 1.4645, νd = 65.7. Glass: FK3 class (Schott; historical supplier unconfirmed). f = −23.43 mm.

This is the member the patent claims. The collective lens leads and has the higher index (Δn = 0.1559 at the junction, 附記 1). The junction r10 (−8.729 mm) is convex toward the image and is more strongly curved than the concave outer face r9: |r10|/|r9| = 0.756, against the limit of 0.9 in 附記 2. The member is net negative (−42.86 mm).

Its Petzval contribution, −0.66 in units of 1/f, comes mostly from the concave front r9 (−1.15). This is consistent with the patent's statement that the fourth member compensates the Petzval sum. Its third-order spherical and astigmatism sums are opposite in sign to those of M3.

The patent attributes a marked influence on the total correction to the junction once Δn reaches 0.100 (p. 1 ¶5). That claim is qualitative, and a third-order decomposition cannot test it: the r10 terms are moderate at that order.

In the model, the rear surface of L7 has the largest clear semi-diameter (9.20 mm). This exceeds the radius of the r10 junction, which is consistent with the enlarged rear component drawn in the patent figure. Because the semi-diameters are ray-derived rather than published, the patent's statement about a reduced last-surface diameter cannot be tested against them.

The palette uses five glass coordinates for seven elements, and its structure follows the patent's index rules. The low-index crown (465657) appears twice, each time as the negative component of a cemented pair: L4 in M3 and L7 in M4. The project catalog provides a coordinate-compatible Schott FK3 dispersion curve for 465657; this is a spectral proxy, not evidence of historical procurement. The same index, 1.4645, appears for the negative lens of the third unit in Bertele's earlier four-unit Zeiss Ikon patent (US 2,084,309), which suggests continuity of glass choice. Identity of the melt cannot be established from the index alone.

## Focus Mechanism

Third-party sources describe the production lens as focusing on its own helicoid, which implies unit focus; Zeiss Ikon states that its lenses couple to the built-in rangefinder, that distance scales are calibrated in feet, and lists a closest distance of 3 ft. The patent publishes only the infinity state. The close-focus state in the prescription file is a constrained reconstruction. The whole optical unit is moved away from the image plane until an object 914.4 mm (3 ft) from the image plane is in paraxial focus. Treating the distance scale as referenced to the image plane is an assumption.

| State | Object distance | Last gap (r11 → image) | Magnification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infinity | ∞ | 15.7821 mm | 0 |
| Closest (3 ft) | 871.7 mm from r1; 914.4 mm from image plane | 17.2230 mm | −0.0415 |

The reconstructed extension is 1.44 mm, and the closest-focus magnification corresponds to about 1:24. Intermediate settings in the viewer interpolate the last gap linearly; they are not mechanical positions.

## Aberration Correction Strategy

**Petzval balance.** The Petzval sum of the implemented model is 0.007076 mm⁻¹ (0.246 in units of 1/f; Petzval radius −141.3 mm). By member, in units of 1/f, it divides as +0.42 (M1), −0.54 (M2), +1.01 (M3) and −0.66 (M4). The two negative members supply comparable flattening, M2 mainly through r5 and M4 mainly through r9.

The Petzval sum depends only on surface curvatures and indices, not on thicknesses. The patent's account, in which thinning member II raises the Petzval sum, is therefore best read as describing the change in surface curvatures that accompanies a thinner member at fixed correction, not a direct thickness effect.

**Field curvature and astigmatism.** Tangential and sagittal foci were computed along real chief rays (Coddington equations) at infinity focus, d-line, measured from the paraxial image plane. Sampled at tenths of the 135-format half-diagonal, the tangential focus stays within 0.065 mm of the image plane out to 0.8 field. The sagittal focus reaches its largest inward departure, −0.244 mm, at 0.6 field and returns toward the plane by 0.8 field.

Beyond that both curves turn sharply. At the corner (31.90°) the tangential focus lies at −0.458 mm and the sagittal at +0.355 mm, an astigmatic difference of 0.81 mm. The patent claims better edge sharpness than the known type in both the meridional and sagittal sections (p. 1 ¶4). It gives no numerical comparison, and none is attempted here.

**Spherical aberration.** The real-ray longitudinal spherical aberration at f/2.8 is −0.066 mm for the marginal ray and −0.138 mm at the 0.707 zone. The design is thus balanced with an undercorrected zone.

The patent discusses zonal spherical aberration in the paragraph that sets the second member's thickness (p. 1 ¶7). The printed text twice names the fourth member (第4部材) as the element that should be thin for a wide angle but thick for low zonal aberration. The paragraph's conclusion, however, sets the thickness of the second member, and ¶2 ties a wider angle to a thinner second member. Reading the passage as referring to the second member is an interpretation of an apparent wording inconsistency.

**Distortion.** Real-ray distortion is pincushion at every sampled field. It grows to +0.78 % at 0.7 field (23.5°) and falls back to +0.18 % at the corner. Extrapolated from small fields, the third-order term alone predicts about 2.62 % at the corner, so higher-order distortion offsets most of it toward the edge.

**Colour.** Only Abbe numbers are available, so chromatic results are first-order. The F–C axial focus difference is −0.130 mm (F focusing nearer the lens), and the paraxial F–C lateral colour at the corner is +0.017 mm. Secondary spectrum cannot be evaluated from the published data.

## Design Heritage and Context

Bertele's Zeiss Ikon patent US 2,084,309 (German priority 1934) already describes a four-unit objective with a collective front unit, a cemented meniscus second unit bent toward the object, and a collective cemented third unit. Its third unit pairs a low-index negative lens (nd 1.4645) with a positive lens, and its rear is a single concavo-convex meniscus. The pre-war Contax Biogon 35 mm f/2.8 is associated with that patent in secondary literature.

JP S30-4837 B describes the earlier four-member objectives of this kind as having an uncemented fourth member. Its contribution is to cement that member as a positive–negative pair with a strong, image-convex, index-raising junction, while keeping the first three members of the known type. Third-party histories place the resulting seven-element, four-group lens in production from 1950, marked Zeiss-Opton and later Carl Zeiss. The Biogon name was afterwards reused by Zeiss for an unrelated Bertele wide-angle type with large outer negative menisci (US 2,721,499).
