# Voigtländer Collinear Type II 150mm f/6.9 — DE 90482 C

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** DE 90482 C (Kaiserliches Patentamt, Patentschrift Nr. 90482, Klasse 57: Photographie)<br>
**Effective date:** 22 May 1895 ("Patentirt im Deutschen Reiche vom 22. Mai 1895 ab")<br>
**Issued:** 29 January 1897; the scan carries the stamp "Gelöscht" (deleted)<br>
**Applicant:** Voigtländer & Sohn, Braunschweig (the patentee; the document names no inventor)<br>
**Title:** *Photographisches Objektiv*<br>
**Example details:** one unnumbered numerical example on page 1 ("Bei einer Brennweite von 150 mm und einer Oeffnung
von 28 mm hat es folgende Elemente"); the drawing sheet (page 3) shows the double objective in one section.

The patent describes a photographic objective cemented from three menisci: a middle positive meniscus enclosed by a
second positive and a third negative meniscus, both of higher refraction than the enclosed lens. A single such cell is
meant chiefly as a landscape lens and, "according to the inventor", corrects spherical aberration, and with it
sharpness, far more completely than earlier cemented objectives. Two equal or symmetrical cells can be combined into a
double objective with the same advantage, and the drawing and the numerical example show that double objective.

The document names no inventor. Moritz von Rohr (1899) credits the design to David Kaempfer, the scientific officer of
Voigtländer & Sohn, and names it the Collinear, Typus II, or three-meniscus objective (Dreimenisken-Objektiv). The
name needs one caution. The Collinear that Voigtländer sold, in Series II at 1:6.3, III at 1:7.7 and IV at 1:12.5, was
the Collinear Type I, the type of Steinheil's patent 88 505. Von Rohr knew of no introduction of this Type II design.
The file is therefore the patent example, not a sold lens: it carries no mount or format. The "II" in the name is the
type number, not a sales series.

The aperture in the title is von Rohr's. The patent prints a focal length of 150 mm and an "Oeffnung" of 28 mm but no
relative aperture, and the evidence below shows that the 28 mm is the diameter of the lenses, not the working beam.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a symmetrical double lens: two identical cemented triplets face each other across an air space of
D = 10 mm between their inner vertices. Each half is 9.3 mm thick on the axis, and the vertex length is 28.6 mm. The
patent draws no stop. The model centres it in the air space, where von Rohr puts it (b1 = b2 = 3.3 at f = 100) and
where the symmetry implies it. The patent prints the radii without signs. Its drawing shows each half as three menisci,
all concave toward the centre, and labels the rear half L3, L2, L1 from the centre outward, so the file runs r1 to r4
positive and mirrors them behind the stop.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = 49.4 | air → 1.5682 | +0.01150 | outer collective face |
| r2 = 16.7 | 1.5682 → 1.5262 | −0.00251 | dispersive junction, index falls |
| r3 = 21.95 | 1.5262 → 1.6112 | +0.00387 | collective junction, index rises |
| r4 = 62.2 | 1.6112 → air | −0.00983 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The two cemented junctions carry opposite index steps across faces of the same bending, the arrangement the anastigmatic
single cell of the 1890s relies on. The example traces to an EFL of 151.362 mm, 0.91 % above the stated 150 mm, with a
paraxial back focus of 139.199 mm. Each half has a focal length of +276.9 mm, 1.83 times that of the pair. The Petzval
sum is 3.52 × 10⁻³ mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of 284 mm or 1.88 f, which agrees with von Rohr's verdict that the type is
decidedly unfavourable in field curvature.

The patent's first claim derives the cell from one half of the double objective of patent 88 505: the middle positive
meniscus is kept, the biconvex lens becomes a positive meniscus and the biconcave lens a negative meniscus. Counted from
the stop outward, the glasses run dense crown, light crown, flint here, where Steinheil's half runs the same order from
the outside in; von Rohr files the two cells as cases 1aα (Steinheil) and 1aβ (Kaempfer) of his scheme.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Negative Meniscus, outer

nD = 1.5682, nF = 1.57637; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's baryta light flint.
f = −45.4 mm.

The outer lens is the cell's only negative member and its most dispersive glass, so it carries the colour correction
against the two positive menisci. Its convex outer face is the strongest collective surface of the cell; its deep
concave rear face (r2 = 16.7) meets L2 in the dispersive junction. Concave toward the stop, the lens thickens toward
the rim: 2 mm on the axis, 7.6 mm at the 14 mm rim. L6 mirrors it.

### L2 / L5 — Positive Meniscus, enclosed lens

nD = 1.5262, nF = 1.53347; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's crown with high
dispersion. f = +112.7 mm.

The enclosed lens has the lowest index of the three, which is the patent's defining feature: both neighbours refract
more strongly. Its low index gives the junctions their opposite steps, falling at r2 and rising at r3. With radii of
16.7 and 21.95 mm and 2.7 mm on the axis it thins quickly and runs out to an edge at a height of 14.2 mm, 0.15 mm
thick at the 14 mm rim. The drawing shows it running out at the rim of the cell in the same way. L5 mirrors it.

### L3 / L4 — Positive Meniscus, inner

nD = 1.6112, nF = 1.61873; no nC, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nF only); von Rohr's heaviest baryta crown.
f = +53.2 mm.

The high-index meniscus next to the stop carries most of the cell's collective power, and its concave inner face is
the main negative contribution to the Petzval sum. The patent explains the meniscus form: the new Jena crown of very
high refraction was bubbly and poorly coloured, and a much thinner positive meniscus in place of a biconvex lens was
expected to be cleaner and more colourless. It is 4.6 mm on the axis and 1.15 mm at the rim. L4 mirrors it.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent prints two index lines for each glass, nD and nF, and names no glass type; its text mentions only the new
Jena glasses and a crown of very high refraction.

| Glass | nD | nF | nF − nD | Elements | Von Rohr's name (p. 386) | ν̄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| outer | 1.5682 | 1.57637 | 0.00817 | L1, L6 | Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | 38.2 |
| middle | 1.5262 | 1.53347 | 0.00727 | L2, L5 | Crown mit hoher Dispersion (crown with high dispersion) | 39.8 |
| inner | 1.6112 | 1.61873 | 0.00753 | L3, L4 | Schwerstes Baryt-Crown (heaviest baryta crown) | 44.8 |

Without nC no Abbe number can be formed. The data file keeps nD and nF as printed with `indexReference` "D" and no
catalog glass as a proxy. The engine needs nC or an Abbe number for its chromatic tiers, so the model traces at nD
alone and shows no colour error; the chromatic correction the patent's glass choice aims at is not reproduced.

Von Rohr's ν̄ is (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD), a dispersion figure toward the photographically active G′ line, given here only
as context. The relative D–F dispersion of the printed values, (nF − nD)/(nD − 1), runs 0.0144, 0.0138 and 0.0123 for
the outer, middle and inner glass: the same order as von Rohr's ν̄, with the dense crown the least dispersive and the
flint the most. Each printed nF − nD is 0.549–0.552 of the nG′ − nD that von Rohr's ν̄ implies, so the patent's values
and his are consistent. The middle crown is named for its high dispersion: it is nearly as dispersive as the flint, so
the junction r2 has a large index step (0.042) with little difference in dispersion.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity prescription. The model is infinity-only: the last gap is the computed paraxial
back focus, and no focusing motion or close-focus spacing is modelled.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The patent's 28 mm "Oeffnung" is read as the diameter of the lenses, for four reasons. L2 runs out to an edge at a
height of 14.2 mm, so no lens of these radii can be much wider than 28 mm. The drawing ends both halves square at a
semi-width of 14.7 mm on its axial scale, with L2 running out at the rim. Von Rohr's Fig. 144 draws the cells at the
reduced diameter (18.4 mm at f = 100, 28 mm here) and the entering beam narrower, at his computation aperture of 1:6.9.
And a 28 mm entrance pupil, 1:5.4, would leave the marginal ray overcorrected by +9.5 mm.

The model therefore sets the clear radius to 14.0 mm on every surface and opens the iris to von Rohr's 1:6.9 at the
traced EFL: an entrance-pupil radius of 10.97 mm and a stop radius of 10.03 mm. The f/6.9 axial beam reaches 10.97 mm
at r1 and 10.19 mm at r8. On axis the longitudinal spherical aberration is +0.31 mm at the rim of the f/6.9 pupil,
−0.69 mm at 0.85, −0.87 mm at 0.7 and −0.61 mm at 0.5. The marginal ray crosses zero near 1:7.1 and the deepest zone,
−0.87 mm, lies near 1:9.5, or −0.57 mm reduced to f = 100. Von Rohr reports spherical correction for about 1:7 and
zones of about −0.6 mm.

The patent prints no field angle. The model traces to ω = 30.5°, the half-field von Rohr computed. The chief ray passes
there with heights of 6.91, 5.86, 4.96 and 3.32 mm at r1–r4, mirrored behind the stop, and the paraxial rim limit is
49°. A meridional trace passes 97 % of the stop diameter at 20°, 88 % at 25° and 75 % at 30.5°, with the rear rim r8
doing most of the cutting.

## Design Heritage and Context

Von Rohr tells the history on pp. 384–386 of his book. Kaempfer had learned optical computation in Jena under Paul
Rudolph (p. 354). He found the Collinear Type I independently, with trial lenses going back to July 1892, and
Voigtländer and Steinheil agreed to make that type jointly under Steinheil's patent 88 505. Working in parallel with
Steinheil's Orthostigmat Type II, Kaempfer then secured a different type of corrected single cell for his own firm by
this patent. Von Rohr reports that the workshop itself, as its additional patent 91 883 shows, judged it suitable for
correcting the spherical aberrations but less so for the astigmatic ones, and that Kaempfer tried to cross the halves
of his two Collinear types so as to keep only their virtues. No introduction of the three-meniscus objective was known
to him, and Voigtländer & Sohn, Aktiengesellschaft, let both patents lapse; they were deleted on 26 September 1898.

Von Rohr reduces this example to f = 100 and prints r = 32.6, 11.0, 14.5 and 41.1, d = 1.3, 1.8 and 3.0, and a
cell-to-stop distance of 3.3 on each side, with the patent's three nD values, computed for 1:6.9 and ω = 30½°. A single
reduction factor 100/f with f between 151.30 and 151.52 mm reproduces every value of his table after rounding; the
traced 151.36 mm lies inside that window, and the stated 150 mm misses r1, r2, r3, r4 and d3. He reduced, in other
words, by the traced focal length rather than the printed one. His rounded table puts the spherical correction near
1:7.3; the patent's unrounded radii put it near 1:7.1, closer to the "about 1:7" of his text.
