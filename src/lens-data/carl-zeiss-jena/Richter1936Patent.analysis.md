# ZEISS / RICHTER 66mm f/6.3 — US 2,031,792 A, Figure 1 (the Topogon patent)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 2,031,792 A, *Anastigmatic Objective for Photography and Projection*  
**Application Number:** Serial No. 736,880  
**Filed:** July 25, 1934 (priority: Germany, July 26, 1933)  
**Granted:** February 25, 1936  
**Inventor:** Robert Richter  
**Assignee:** Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung (printed as "the firm of Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany")  
**Example details:** first of three constructional examples; section in Figure 1 (drawing sheet 1, which repeats the table); prescription table and stated focal length in the specification, p. 1–2

The patent describes objectives with a pair of meniscus members on each side of the diaphragm: in each pair one
member converges and one diverges, the diverging member sits next to the diaphragm, and every concave surface faces
the diaphragm. Its central idea is a large separation between the two concave surfaces nearest the diaphragm. The
claims require more than four fifths of the mean of their radii. The text adds that an angle of view above 100° becomes
possible when the separation exceeds the full mean. The first constructional example, shown in Figure 1, is fully
symmetrical, has a focal length of fD = 66.0 mm and is stated to give good central definition at f/6.3 with
anastigmatic correction over a 100° field. The second example (Figure 2, fD = 75.7 mm) makes only the outer pair
dissymmetrical to remove distortion for distant objects. The third (Figure 3, fD = 66.1 mm) adds two plane-parallel
plates for use at both 1:1 and infinite conjugates. This file models only the first example.

This patent is the patent of the Zeiss Topogon, the four-meniscus wide-angle lens Richter designed at Zeiss:

1. Zeiss's own company history lists 1933 as the year Robert Richter developed the Topogon, describing it as a fast,
   distortion-free 90° lens that changed photogrammetry
   ([ZEISS, history of camera and cine lenses](https://www.zeiss.com/corporate/en/about-zeiss/past/history/technological-milestones/camera-and-cine-lenses.html)).
2. H. H. Nasse's 2011 Zeiss article on lens names notes that Zeiss offered the Topogon from 1933, that it resembled a
   pair of Gauss telescope objectives with stronger curvatures, that it was long a standard in aerial photography,
   and that a 25 mm f/4 version was made for the Contax; it shows that version's lens section
   ([Nasse, *Lens Names: Planar*, p. 2](https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/app/uploads/2018/02/en_CLB_40_Nasse_Lens_Names_Planar.pdf)).
3. Bausch & Lomb's Metrogon aerial lenses carry this patent number, 2,031,792, on the front of the barrel. Reference
   accounts describe them as a licensed version of the very similar Topogon
   ([Metrogon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrogon); [Topogon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topogon)).
4. The construction matches: four single menisci in four groups, symmetrical about a central diaphragm, with thick
   positive outer menisci and thin negative inner menisci.

The limits are just as clear. No production prescription of any Topogon or Metrogon has been published, and the
patent does not say which example went into production. Zeiss describes the photogrammetric Topogon as
distortion-free, which fits the dissymmetrical Figure 2 at least as well as the symmetrical Figure 1. The 66 mm focal
length is simply the patent's working scale, not a catalogue focal length. The model is therefore the Topogon
patent's first worked example, a close relative of the production lenses rather than a confirmed copy of any one of
them.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a four-element, four-group symmetrical design. Two thick positive menisci (L1, L4) sit outside two
thin negative menisci (L2, L3), and all four curve around a central diaphragm. Its power sequence is
positive–negative | stop | negative–positive. Each outer meniscus almost touches its inner partner on the axis
(0.02 mm air space). The facing surfaces are curved differently (16.55 mm against 9.094 mm), so the thin air lens
between them opens toward the rim. The two inner menisci are separated by a 12.98 mm air space, 1.77 times the mean
radius (7.35 mm) of the two concave surfaces that face the diaphragm. This is the patent's wide-angle condition.

The patent's other conditions also hold for this example. The diverging lenses have an index above 1.65 (1.7172), and
their centre thickness, 0.5 mm, is 0.76 % of the focal length, below the 2 % the claims set. The strongly curved,
nearly concentric shells give a Petzval sum of almost exactly zero (about −0.000004 per mm). A field this flat is
essential if a four-element lens is to cover such a wide angle.

From first to last vertex the lens is 22.90 mm long. The computed focal length is 66.04 mm, 0.06 % from the printed
66.0 mm. The paraxial back focus is 51.94 mm, so the image plane is 74.84 mm behind the first vertex.

Because the lens is symmetrical, it is not corrected for distortion at infinity, and the patent says so. The model's
chief ray lands 0.9 % beyond the ideal rectilinear height at 30°, 1.6 % at 40° and 2.5 % at 50°: a mild pincushion.
At the 50° edge of the patent's field the image height is 80.7 mm, so the full field needs a circle about 161 mm
across. Covering that much image from one short focal length is what made the design valuable for aerial mapping.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 and L4 — Positive Meniscus (outer)

`nD = 1.6201, ν = 60.4`. Radii 11.25 / 16.55 mm, centre thickness 4.44 mm; standalone focal length +42.90 mm.

L1 and L4 are the two thick collective shells. Their outer radius is only 11.25 mm, about one sixth of the focal length.
They are deep, almost hemispherical shells, and at the 50° field the chief ray strikes them more than 10 mm from the
axis. They carry all the positive power of the objective. They are also the part of the design the patent's
second example makes dissymmetrical to cancel distortion.

### L2 and L3 — Negative Meniscus (inner)

`nD = 1.7172, ν = 29.5`. Radii 9.094 / 7.35 mm, centre thickness 0.5 mm; standalone focal length −60.71 mm.

L2 and L3 are thin, high-index flint shells that face the diaphragm. Their concave inner surfaces (7.35 mm radius)
are the two surfaces whose wide separation the patent claims. The patent notes that flint glasses are hard to make
in such forms. Keeping this pair symmetrical, and correcting distortion with the outer pair instead (Figure 2), is one
of the advantages it claims.

## Glass Identification

The patent prints only an nD index (sodium D line) and an Abbe number ν for each glass, and the model uses those
values directly. Both pairs match classic Schott types closely: 1.6201 / 60.4 is a dense barium crown of the SK16 kind
(modern N-SK16 is 1.62041 / 60.32 at the helium d line), and 1.7172 / 29.5 is a dense flint of the SF1 kind (SF1 is
1.71736 / 29.51). These are catalogue equivalents only. The data file names no glass, and the patent says nothing
about the supplier or melt. Partial dispersions are not printed, so the model's colour behaviour is an estimate
derived from nD and ν.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity prescription and no focusing data. The model is therefore infinity-only and
models no focusing movement. The patent's third example, with plane-parallel plates, is the form it gives for
distortion-free use at both 1:1 and infinity; that example is not modelled here.

## Aspherical Surfaces

None. All eight surfaces are spherical, as printed.

## Stop, Clear Apertures and Field Coverage

The patent prints no diaphragm position or diameter. Figure 1 draws the diaphragm in the middle of the central air
space, as the symmetry of the design requires, so the model places it 6.49 mm behind L2 and 6.49 mm ahead of L3. Its
f/6.3 opening has a radius of 3.97 mm. The drawn opening measures about 4.1 mm, so the figure is consistent with that
aperture.

Clear apertures are not printed either. Each rim was chosen as the smallest that still passes the chief ray at the
50° edge of the 100° field. The front and rear surfaces (S1, S8) are 10.45 mm, where that chief ray strikes them at
10.42 mm. The convex faces of the inner menisci (S3, S6) are 7.4 mm, against 7.39 mm for the chief ray. The other
four surfaces keep estimated values of 10 mm and 6.5 mm, which already clear it. The rim slope of S1 and S8 is 68°,
steeper than the usual drawing limit, and is allowed explicitly. Figure 1 draws the outer menisci almost to a knife
edge at about 11 mm and the inner menisci slightly larger than modelled, so these rims are, if anything, conservative.
Toward the edge of the field the rims of the deep shells cut more and more of the oblique beam. On top of that comes
the natural fall of illumination at such steep angles, so the corners of the 100° field are much darker than the
centre.

The patent's 100° angle of view is declared in the data file as the lens's full field. The ray diagram's own paraxial
estimate stops near 37° and would otherwise undercount the coverage the patent states.
