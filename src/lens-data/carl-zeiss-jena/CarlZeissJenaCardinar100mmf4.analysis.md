# Carl Zeiss Jena Cardinar 100mm f/4

## Source Reference and Design Identification

**Source:** Carl Zeiss Jena, *Optik-Daten* für Sach-Nr. 550513A (Opt.), Cardinar 4/100.  
**Manufacturer:** Carl Zeiss Jena, as identified on the original sheet.  
**Source dated:** 25 August 1958 (department mark "WOPho 25.8.58").  
**Calculation dated:** 14 June 1958 ("für Rechnung vom 14.6.58"); the sheet also records "ausgehend vom V 237".  
**Use:** Format 24 x 36, "Wechselobjektiv Werra".  
**Patent association:** DD 23 651 (Erich Fincke, Harry Zöllner) is a related 100 mm f/3.5 design. It supplies none of this model's numbers.

The Cardinar 4/100 is the short telephoto that Carl Zeiss Jena made for the Werra rangefinder cameras, which took it on their own bayonet. This model is the production prescription as printed on the factory optical data sheet, not a patent example. The sheet gives every radius, thickness, index, Abbe number, glass type and melt number, along with the stop position, stop diameters, free and mechanical diameters, focal length, back focus and pupil data.

The sheet closes on itself. Traced from its own radii, spacings and indices, the model returns the printed focal length, back focus and front focal distance to within 0.006 mm, so the numbers below can be read as a working design record rather than a reconstruction.

The sheet was kept up after issue. Ink changes widen or tighten several thickness tolerances, "Objektiv" is struck out with "Cardinar" stamped over it, the last characters of the part number are overwritten in black, and later dated notes read 14.7.64 and 7.12.65. No radius, thickness, index or Abbe number is altered, so the typed state is the one modeled. The dates suggest that the sheet stayed in use for most of the lens's production life; that is an inference from the notes.

## Optical Architecture

Five elements in four groups, all spherical, with the stop in the long air space between the third and fourth groups. In order from the object: two positive menisci convex to the front, a cemented doublet of negative power, and a thin positive meniscus.

| Unit | Elements | In-air focal length |
|---|---|---|
| Front pair | L1 + L2 | +43.7 mm |
| Doublet C1 | L3 + L4 | −30.0 mm |
| Front group | L1 to L4 | +177.2 mm |
| Rear element | L5 | +87.5 mm |

The arrangement is positive, negative, stop, positive. The strong positive front pair followed by the negative doublet moves the rear principal plane forward, to 1.37 mm ahead of the first vertex. The overall length from S1 to the image is 93.76 mm for f' 95.13 mm, a telephoto ratio of 0.986, so the lens is only just a true telephoto. Its value lies less in a short barrel than in a small rear element: the Werra's central shutter sits behind the lens, and the beam must pass through it.

The history published by zeissikonveb.de describes the lens as a Sonnar type. That fits the thick second element and the doublet with a deep concave rear face.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Positive Meniscus, convex to object

nd = 1.5890, νd = 61.1. Glass: SK5 (Jena). f = +107.7 mm.

L1 meets the full beam and takes the smaller share of the front pair's power. Its free diameter of 41.0 mm is the largest on the sheet and much wider than the 23.77 mm axial beam, because the off-axis beams cross this face well away from the axis. It is one of the faces that sets the vignetting.

### L2 — Positive Meniscus, convex to object

nd = 1.5889, νd = 58.5. Glass: SK13 (Jena), Unmatched in the catalog. f = +68.2 mm.

L2 is the strongest positive element and, at 13.25 mm, by far the thickest. Its front face S3 carries most of its power. The sheet relieves the rear rim with a printed facet, "Fac 26.8 ∢40°", and the 1:1 section shows a cone from Ø35 down to a short Ø26.8 band at the rear. A 0.20 mm air space separates L2 from L3.

### L3 and L4 — Cemented Doublet C1

**L3, Biconvex Positive.** nd = 1.5102, νd = 63.4. Glass: BK1 (Jena). f = +91.1 mm.

**L4, Biconcave Negative.** nd = 1.6991, νd = 30.0. Glass: SF15 (Jena). f = −21.8 mm.

The doublet pairs a low-index crown with a dense flint, and its power is −30.0 mm. Its rear face S7, R = 20.098 mm, is the strongest surface in the lens at −0.0348 mm⁻¹, nearly twice S3's +0.0178 mm⁻¹. That face provides the telephoto action. It is also where the sheet's second facet sits, "Fac 18 ∢45°": the section shows a 45° cone from the Ø27 edge down to the rim of the concave face.

### L5 — Positive Meniscus, convex to object

nd = 1.8072, νd = 25.3. Glass: SF6 (Jena). f = +87.5 mm.

L5 is only 2.90 mm thick and Ø17 mm, and it restores convergence behind the stop. A pencil note beside this element on the sheet reads "fd 87.49", the same in-air focal length the model gives. Using a dense flint as a positive element is unusual. Its high index keeps the surfaces weak and the Petzval contribution small, and its dispersion probably works against the lateral colour of the front group; both readings are inferences from the prescription, since the sheet gives no reasons.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The sheet names each glass and melt and prints the melt's index and Abbe number. Those values are kept as printed even where a modern catalog differs. A catalog row supplies only the shape of the dispersion curve; the index at the d-line stays the sheet's.

| Element | Sheet glass | Melt | nd / νd | Catalog row | Δnd | Δνd |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | SK5 | G 4703 | 1.5890 / 61.1 | SK5 (Sumita) | +0.00013 | +0.10 |
| L2 | SK13 | 59976 | 1.5889 / 58.5 | none | — | — |
| L3 | BK1 | G 68327 | 1.5102 / 63.4 | BK1 (Sumita) | −0.00011 | 0.00 |
| L4 | SF15 | G 69242 | 1.6991 / 30.0 | SF15 (Sumita) | −0.00015 | 0.00 |
| L5 | SF6 | 67433 | 1.8072 / 25.3 | SF6 (Schott) | −0.00202 | +0.13 |

No public catalog lists SK13 under that name, so L2 traces its printed pair by Abbe number alone. The nearest row, Hoya's obsolete BACD13 (1.59181 / 58.31), is 0.0029 away in index and no equivalence to Jena SK13 has been documented.

SF15 and SF6 of this period are lead flints, so the legacy rows are used rather than their lead-free successors. The choice is small here: lead-free rows move the g-line focus by 0.007 mm.

The sheet marks the lens "Farbortkorrig.", corrected for the position of colour. The model puts the paraxial C-line focus 0.10 mm behind the d-line focus, the F-line 0.09 mm ahead of it and the g-line 0.03 mm ahead of it. A pen note dated 7.12.65 gives the A′ focus difference from d as +0.28 mm; with L2 given BACD13's dispersion shape for this one comparison, the model gives +0.29 mm. That is good evidence that the catalog dispersions are representative, even though the sheet prints no spectral indices.

## Focus Mechanism

The sheet gives no focusing travel, close distance or mount drawing, so this model is static at infinity. The museum record for the lens gives a close focus of 1.5 m. The helicoid sits in the lens on the Werra, and an owner account describes the whole lens moving, but no maker document confirms the focusing method, so no focus travel is modeled.

## Stop, Pupils and Why the Lens Stops at f/4

The sheet prints "Blendenlage 9.2 + 8.2": the stop lies 9.2 mm behind S7 and 8.2 mm ahead of S8, slightly nearer the rear element. Printed stop diameters run from 11.24 mm at f/4 through 7.95, 5.62, 3.97, 2.81 and 1.99 to 1.40 mm at f/32, each a √2 step.

The printed pupil data are tagged "f. 8°", and they are real-ray values for that field. A real chief ray at 8° through the stop centre gives the entrance pupil 80.23 mm behind S1 (printed +80.2) and the exit pupil 10.65 mm ahead of S9 (printed −10.7). The paraxial pupils lie at 79.41 and 10.51 mm. This agreement also fixes the reading of "9.2 + 8.2" as measured from S7.

The file derives its stop radius from the f-number, as for any ordinary prime: 5.574 mm, against the sheet's 5.62 mm. The paraxial value is smaller because the real marginal ray reaches the stop higher, at 5.604 mm for the printed Ø23.77 mm entrance pupil.

The sheet boxes 1:4 as the full aperture, and a catalog change sheet replaces "Objektiv 3,5/100 mm" with "Cardinar 4/100 mm". The history at zeissikonveb.de reports that the lens was calculated for f/3.5 and limited mechanically to f/4, because f/3.5 did not fit the Werra's light-value coupling. The printed numbers are consistent with a limit that is not set by the glass. The printed free Ø 13.5 mm at S7 passes the axial beam only to about f/3.65, but the polished face continues to the Ø18 facet, which would clear f/3.5. That reading is an inference.

## Field, Rims and Vignetting

The sheet prints a field of 2σ = 25° for the 24 x 36 format. The real chief ray at 12.5° lands 21.34 mm from the axis, and the format corner at 21.63 mm is reached at 12.7°. Distortion is mild pincushion, computed at +0.7 % at 8° and +1.2 % at 12.5°; the sheet prints no distortion value.

Each surface's clear semi-diameter is half the printed freier Ø, except the cemented S6. Tracing the lens shows what those printed diameters are. The limiting faces are S1, S7, the stop and L5. With S6 left open, the peak beam footprint across the field traces to 9.05 mm there, exactly half the printed 18.1 mm, and it reaches 19.27, 16.57, 12.16 and 11.97 mm at S2 to S5 against printed 19.3, 16.6, 12.2 and 12.0 mm. These faces record the size of the beam rather than a mechanical limit.

Because a cemented interface cannot stop a ray and cannot carry a land in this model, S6 is set to the printed lens Ø27 mm so that the doublet draws as the square-edged pair in the sheet's section. Vignetting is unchanged at every field. The other faces carry flat lands out to the printed lens diameters of 42, 35, 27 and 17 mm, stopping at the printed facet diameters on S4 and S7. The lands add no aperture and cost no light. The straight edges drawn between those heights stand in for the facets; the model has no cone-shaped rim, so the silhouettes of L2 and L4 are approximate.

| Field | 0° | 2.1° | 4.2° | 6.3° | 8.5° | 10.6° | 12.7° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transmission at f/4 | 100 % | 100 % | 95.6 % | 89.1 % | 80.9 % | 66.2 % | 43.9 % |

Vignetting starts within the first few degrees, because the S7 opening is only slightly wider than the axial beam there, and the corner keeps 43.9 % of the axial light. On-axis spherical aberration is small and undercorrected: −0.08 mm at full aperture and −0.07 mm at 0.7 of the pupil. The Petzval sum is 0.00164 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 608 mm.

The radii are unsigned on the sheet. Their signs come from the Sinnbild, the row of curved symbols between the radii and the thicknesses, which shows S6 alone curving the other way. The closure above confirms that reading: any other sign would miss the printed focal length by a wide margin. The subscript "m" is read here as a mid-zone value, which is an inference; the printed values fall on the model's zonal curve near 0.5 of the pupil. The exit-pupil diameter is the one printed value that stays more than 1 % away, and it is not resolved here.

## Relation to Patent DD 23 651

**Patent:** DD 23 651, *Photographisches viergliedriges Teleobjektiv*.  
**Inventors:** Erich Fincke, Harry Zöllner, named as both inventors and holders.  
**Filed:** 17 November 1958.  
**Published:** 5 September 1962.

The patent's single example is a 100 mm f/3.5 lens with a 24° field, and its text says that 1:4 is achievable. It is the same five-element, four-group form, with the stop, rear lens and central shutter brought close together behind the front group. It is not this prescription. Scaled to equal focal length, the sheet differs from the patent example by up to 14.5 % in radius (S2 and S4) and by about 9 % in the two large spacings, L2's thickness and the long air space. L1 is a different glass: the patent uses the same 1.59133 / 58.2 glass for L1 and L2, while the sheet has SK5 and SK13.

The production design also sits outside two figures in the patent's text, which asks for an air space under 17 % of the focal length and a length from front lens to rear vertex under 57 %. The sheet gives 18.3 % and 58.3 %, and the patent example gives 16.77 % and 56.98 %. The sheet is still inside the wider ranges of claim 1, including an air space of 14 to 25 % of the focal length.

The calculation on the sheet is dated five months before the patent was filed, and it says it starts from "V 237". The zeissikonveb.de history describes V 237 as a 3.5/100 test lens, which suggests that the patent example belongs to that line and that the sheet records its re-calculation for series production. The patent itself does not name V 237, so this is an inference. The same history gives the calculation date as 16 June 1958, where the sheet reads 14.6.58.

The catalog change sheet fits that sequence. Its entries for the Werra pages replace "Objektiv 3,5/100 mm" with "Cardinar 4/100 mm"; the retained image carries no date, and the history places it in 1959.

The same history reports about 23,000 units between 1959 and 1969.
