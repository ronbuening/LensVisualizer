# CARL ZEISS JENA SPHAEROGON 100mm f/8 (US 2,126,126 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 2,126,126 A
**Filed:** 27 November 1936 (Serial No. 113,069); German priority 26 November 1935
**Granted:** 9 August 1938
**Inventor:** Willy Merté
**Assignee:** the firm Carl Zeiss, Jena (Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung)
**Title:** Photographic Objective
**Example details:** Figure 1 with Table 1, the first of two examples; dimensions in millimetres for a total focal length of 100 mm; 4 claims

The patent describes objectives for very large image fields, "for instance an angle of 180°", built from three air-spaced members: a divergent front member, a convergent middle meniscus and a divergent rear meniscus, the two menisci turning their concave sides toward the front. It gives two numerical examples. Figure 1 has a plano-concave front lens; Figure 2 replaces it with a meniscus and is not modelled here. Both are stated to be spherically corrected for an aperture of approximately 1:8.

The model follows Figure 1 / Table 1. All seven radii, the four thicknesses, the three air spaces (including the two diaphragm distances), and the four index and ν pairs are as printed. The computed paraxial focal length is 100.12 mm, 0.12 % above the stated 100 mm, which is within the rounding of the table.

**Link to a production lens.** The design is plausibly the first formula of the Zeiss Sphaerogon, the experimental 180° lens Merté developed at Jena in 1935 and 1936:

1. Marco Cavina's study of the Perimetar, Sphaerogon and Pleon lists a "Sphaerogon type #1 (for F=100mm)" with the same seven radii, thicknesses and four glasses as Table 1 (his single stop spacing of 112.64 mm is the sum of the patent's 110.28 and 2.36 mm).
2. Cavina ties that formula to the 1.6 cm and 1.9 cm f/8 Sphaerogon prototypes, four lenses in three groups, which covered about 165° in trials.
3. A figure from Merté's own later account, captioned with the German patent D.R.P. 672 393 of 1935, shows the same section with correction curves drawn out to 90° off axis.

The patent itself names no product, and no factory drawing of a built lens has been compared with this table, so the link is stated as plausible, not proven. The model is not a 16 mm or 19 mm production lens. Later Sphaerogons with six and eight elements are different formulas.

## Optical Architecture

Four elements in three members, negative – positive – negative, all spherical:

- **Front member (L1):** one large plano-concave negative lens, flat side to the object, 110.28 mm ahead of the diaphragm. It turns rays arriving from nearly the whole forward half-sphere into a cone that the small rear lenses can accept.
- **Diaphragm:** in the long air space, 2.36 mm ahead of the second member. Its position is printed.
- **Middle member (L2):** a positive meniscus, concave toward the front.
- **Rear member (L3 + L4):** a cemented pair forming a weakly negative meniscus, also concave toward the front, with a convergent cemented surface.

The three conditions of the claims hold for this prescription. The middle member's focal length (73.4 mm) is under two fifths of the front member's (346 mm in magnitude). The front vertices of the first and second members are 119.19 mm apart, more than three quarters of the focal length. The air space between the second and third members (1.31 mm) is under one tenth of that distance.

The system is a strong retrofocus: the paraxial back focus is 157.88 mm for a 100.12 mm focal length, and the length from the front surface to the last surface is 136.72 mm.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Plano-Concave Negative

nd = 1.62313, νd = 57.0. Glass: not named in the patent. f = −346.0 mm.

Flat front surface and a concave rear surface of radius 215.58 mm, 6.55 mm thick on axis. Figure 1 draws it roughly 240 mm across for the 100 mm focal length, more than twice the focal length, because the edge-of-field rays enter far from the axis. The patent notes that a meniscus front member (Figure 2) is advisable for especially great image angles, and that a front member of more than one lens can reduce the chromatic difference of magnification.

### L2 — Positive Meniscus, concave to the object

nd = 1.51746, νd = 58.9. Glass: not named in the patent. f = +73.4 mm.

Radii −42.70 and −21.20 mm, 6.81 mm thick. It carries most of the positive power and sits directly behind the diaphragm.

### L3 + L4 — Cemented Rear Member

L3, Biconcave Negative: nd = 1.55088, νd = 49.6. f = −25.0 mm.
L4, Biconvex Positive: nd = 1.60284, νd = 60.6. f = +31.8 mm.
Glass: not named in the patent. The pair together: f = −452 mm.

Radii −16.15, +100.88 and −23.08 mm, thicknesses 2.99 and 6.42 mm. The patent recommends making the rear member from two lenses of different refractive index joined by a convergent cemented surface, which is what the higher-index positive L4 behind the lower-index negative L3 provides. The 1.31 mm air space between L2 and L3 closes toward the rim, so L3's concave front face ends near 10 mm from the axis; Figure 1 bevels the pair from there out to its full diameter.

## Glass Identification

The patent gives an index and a ν value for each lens and no glass names. The text table heads the index "n_d"; the drawing sheet heads the same numbers "nD". The difference between the helium d and sodium D lines is far below anything visible in this model, and the values are traced as d-line data. Three are crowns (ν of 57 to 61) and L3 is the lowest-ν glass of the set (ν = 49.6). None matched a current catalogue glass closely enough to name.

## Field and Projection

The patent states the field ("up to approximately 180°") and no projection law. Tracing the real chief ray through the printed prescription gives these image heights for the 100 mm focal length:

| Half-field | Image height |
|---|---|
| 10° | 17.5 mm |
| 30° | 51.6 mm |
| 60° | 94.1 mm |
| 88° | about 111 mm |

The image of the full half-sphere is therefore a circle a little over 220 mm across, about 2.2 focal lengths. The mapping lies between the orthographic law (100 mm at 90°) and the equal-area law (141 mm at 90°). The data file declares the equal-area fisheye law, the nearer of the two the viewer supports, so distortion figures shown against that reference include a large real departure and should be read with that in mind.

The patent prints no lens diameters. Figure 1 is drawn to scale, and the rims in the model are read from it: L2 at 11.4 mm and the cemented pair at 13.8 mm from the axis. The front element is 114.4 mm on its flat face and 92.8 mm on its concave face, the heights the chief ray of an 88° half-field needs and within about 5 % of the drawing, which shows a 120.5 mm blank bevelled down to a 94 mm concave face. The viewer traces a half-field of 76°, the widest at which its chief-ray solve is dependable with these rims. At f/8 the rims pass the whole axial beam; at the widest traced field they pass about four fifths of the beam that fills the stop.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives one state, for a distant object, and describes no focusing movement. The model is infinity only. Its last spacing is the computed paraxial back focus, 157.88 mm; the patent prints no back focus.
