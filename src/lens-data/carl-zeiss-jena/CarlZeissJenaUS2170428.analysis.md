# CARL ZEISS JENA 100mm f/1.9 (US 2,170,428 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 2,170,428 A, *Four-Lens Photo-Objective*  
**Application Number:** Serial No. 209,640  
**Filed:** May 24, 1938 (German priority May 31, 1937)  
**Granted:** August 22, 1939  
**Inventors:** Robert Richter  
**Assignee:** Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung (printed as "the firm of Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany")  
**Example details:** one numerical example, printed in the drawing sheet's table and repeated in the specification; all values refer to a focal length of 100 mm; one claim

The patent covers photographic and projection objectives made of four single lenses separated by air, in which the
third lens is negative and the other three are positive. Its one example is described in the text as a projection
objective with a maximum relative aperture of 1:1.9. The patent claims very good definition over an angular field of
up to about 40°.

The model transcribes that example as printed. The two printed copies of the table (drawing sheet and specification)
agree in every radius, thickness, air space, index and Abbe number. No scale factor is applied: the patent states the
values for a 100 mm focal length, and the model keeps them in millimetres.

No production lens is documented for this example: neither the patent nor any catalogue, museum record or collector
reference found links it to a named Zeiss objective.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is four air-spaced singlets in four groups, in the sequence positive–positive–negative–positive:

| Element | Shape | Focal length in air |
|---|---|---:|
| L1 | Biconvex, nearly plano rear | +119.43 mm |
| L2 | Thick positive meniscus, convex to the front | +155.07 mm |
| L3 | Biconcave negative | −37.94 mm |
| L4 | Biconvex, steeper rear | +58.66 mm |

The patent's stated purpose is to reach a wide aperture without cemented surfaces. It notes that earlier objectives of
this kind relied on strongly curved cemented surfaces for their image quality, and it replaces them with a set of
conditions on the bendings, the thickness of L2, the glass of L3 and the two air spaces around L3 (see Conditional
Expressions below).

The power distribution is unusual. L1 and L2 together form a strong positive front pair (f = +64.78 mm). The negative
L3 almost cancels it: L1 to L3 together are nearly afocal (f ≈ −1015 mm). L4, 19 mm behind L3, then supplies nearly all
of the system's convergence. The Petzval sum is 0.00379 mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of about 264 mm or 2.6 times the focal
length.

The patent prints no diaphragm. The model places an iris midway in the 19 mm air space between L3 and L4, the usual
stop position for this positive–positive–negative–positive form. That position is an estimate, as are all clear
semi-diameters.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Biconvex Positive

`nd = 1.6138, νd = 56.3. Glass: BACD6 (Hoya) equivalent — dense barium crown. f = +119.43 mm.`

L1 carries a strongly curved front surface (r1 = +85.4) and an almost flat rear (r2 = −500). The claim requires the
front radius to be greater than 55 % of the focal length and smaller than the focal length; the example's 85.4 mm sits
inside that window. L1 and L2 are separated by only 0.5 mm on the axis.

### L2 — Positive Meniscus

`nd = 1.6138, νd = 56.3. Glass: BACD6 (Hoya) equivalent — dense barium crown. f = +155.07 mm.`

L2 is the thick element of the design: 19.0 mm on the axis, 19 % of the focal length, inside the claimed 12–30 %
range. Its front surface (r3 = +44.5) is the most strongly curved positive surface in the lens; its concave rear
(r4 = +70.0) stays within the claim's limit of at most the focal length. The patent drawing shows this element with a
bevelled rear rim, and the model draws a similar step from the 28 mm front rim to the 19 mm rear rim.

### L3 — Biconcave Negative

`nd = 1.7174, νd = 29.5. Glass: SF1 (Schott) — dense flint. f = −37.94 mm.`

L3 is the only negative element and the only flint. The claim restricts its glass to an index between 1.66 and 1.89
and an Abbe number between 23.0 and 33.0. Its rear surface (r6 = +34.3) is much more strongly curved than its front
(r5 = −135), and the claim requires that rear radius to exceed 25 % of the focal length. L3 is 4.5 mm behind L2 and
19.0 mm ahead of L4; the claim requires the rear air space to be the larger one and to stay below 30 % of the focal
length.

### L4 — Biconvex Positive

`nd = 1.6138, νd = 56.3. Glass: BACD6 (Hoya) equivalent — dense barium crown. f = +58.66 mm.`

L4 is the strongest positive element and the rear collector. Its rear surface (r8 = −46.8) is the more strongly
curved one, as the claim requires, and is convex with a radius smaller than the focal length. Because the front three
lenses are nearly afocal, L4's power sets the system focal length almost by itself.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent prints a refractive index headed nd (lowercase d, in both tables and in the claim) and an Abbe number ν for
each lens. Only two glasses are used:

| Elements | Printed nd / ν | Identification |
|---|---|---|
| L1, L2, L4 | 1.6138 / 56.3 | Hoya BACD6 equivalent (nd 1.61375, νd 56.38): dense barium crown of the 614/564 class |
| L3 | 1.7174 / 29.5 | Schott SF1 (nd 1.71736, νd 29.51): dense flint |

The SF1 match supports reading the printed indices at the helium d line (587.6 nm): SF1's index at the sodium D line
(589.3 nm) is 1.71715, which would print as 1.7172. The model therefore uses d-line indices for all four lenses. Both
glasses match their catalogue rows within the printed precision (Δnd ≤ 0.00005, Δνd ≤ 0.08). The patent does not name
its glasses, and BACD6 is a modern catalogue equivalent, not the 1937 melt. The catalogue rows supply the dispersion
curves; the trace keeps the printed nd. No partial dispersions are printed, and no claim about secondary spectrum is
made.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity prescription, in photographing order with the object (in projection, the screen)
on the L1 side. The model is infinity-only: no element moves, and the image plane is the computed paraxial back focus,
63.2102 mm behind r8. The patent prints no back focus.

## Conditional Expressions

The single claim states the following conditions. The example satisfies all of them.

| Condition in the claim | Example value |
|---|---|
| Front surfaces of L1 and L2 and rear surface of L4 convex, each radius smaller than f | 85.4, 44.5, 46.8 mm |
| r1 greater than 55 % of f | 85.4 mm |
| Rear surface of L2 concave, radius at most f | 70.0 mm |
| Rear surface of L3 concave, more strongly curved than its front, radius greater than 25 % of f | 34.3 mm against 135 mm |
| Air space L3–L4 greater than air space L2–L3 and smaller than 30 % of f | 19.0 mm against 4.5 mm |
| Thickness of L2 between 12 % and 30 % of f | 19.0 mm |
| Index of L3 between 1.66 and 1.89; Abbe number between 23.0 and 33.0 | 1.7174; 29.5 |

## Model Scope and Limitations

- **Focal length:** the computed paraxial EFL is 99.78 mm against the stated 100 mm (−0.22 %). Three radii are printed
  only to whole millimetres (r2, r5, r7), and rounding all printed values to their last digit moves the EFL by about
  0.2 mm, so the difference is within the table's rounding. No single misprinted digit accounts for it.
- **Aperture:** f/1.9 gives an entrance pupil radius of 26.26 mm and a paraxial stop semi-diameter of 16.61 mm. At full
  aperture the real marginal ray crosses the stop plane at 17.29 mm, higher than the paraxial ray because of the
  spherical aberration of the lenses ahead of the stop, so the iris trims roughly the outer 4 % of the pupil radius.
- **Lengths:** 60.7 mm from r1 to r8, 123.91 mm from r1 to the image.
- **Field:** at the patent's 40° full field (image height 36.3 mm) the chief ray clears every rim; the 22 mm rims of L4
  trim about a quarter of the oblique bundle on one side. With the estimated rims, rays can be traced to about 25° from
  the axis.

## Design Heritage and Context

The positive–positive–negative–positive sequence of four separated lenses is the arrangement of the Ernostar family of
high-aperture objectives. That comparison is an interpretation, not a statement of the patent. The patent's own
contribution is to obtain a good wide-aperture objective of this kind with no cemented surfaces, by the conditions
listed above.

Later four-component objective patents cite US 2,170,428, among them Eastman Kodak's US 2,432,387 (1947), Meopta's
US 2,543,856 (1951), Taylor, Taylor & Hobson's US 2,600,208 (1952), Bell & Howell's US 3,065,671 (1962) and
Voigtländer's US 3,212,400 (1965).
