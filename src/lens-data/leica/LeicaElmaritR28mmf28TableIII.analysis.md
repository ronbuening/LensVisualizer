## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 3,740,120 A  
**Application Number:** 236,948  
**Filed:** March 22, 1972  
**Granted:** June 19, 1973  
**Priority:** March 26, 1971, Germany P 21 14 729.5  
**Inventor:** Rudolf Rühl  
**Assignee:** Ernst Leitz GmbH  
**Title:** Wide-angle lens  
**Embodiment analyzed:** Table III (claim 3)

This prescription models the first optical generation of the LEICA ELMARIT-R 28mm f/2.8 (1970, product code 11204) from Table III of the patent. The patent numbers its embodiments as tables; it does not call this one “Example 3.” Which of the three tables was manufactured is not confirmed by the manufacturer.

The design-family association rests on several convergent observations:

1. Table III specifies eight air-spaced elements, f/2.8, and a field of ±38°. The patent expressly addresses 35 mm SLR cameras. Leica’s 1983 catalog gives the corresponding production lens eight elements in eight groups and a 76° field.
2. Leitz’s brochure with an 11/70 imprint announces code 11204 as available early in 1971 and illustrates the same eight-singlet topology. The announcement precedes the patent’s priority date; proximity in time supports context, not proof of numerical identity.
3. The PhotonsToPhotos reconstruction reproduces every Table III radius, axial distance, and glass pair after its stop split is recombined and dimensions are divided by 28. Its exact stop position and apertures are additional modeling choices.

Table III is the best-supported of the three tables against the patent figure, but production identity among them is not proven. Measured on the patent drawing, the front radius of L5 and the two radii of L6 are about 2.3 f, 1.1 f and 1.65 f. Table III prints 2.15, 1.14 and 1.61; Table I prints 1.48, 1.21 and 1.34, and Table II 1.47, 2.10 and 1.12. A launch drawing weakly favors Table III, whereas later cutaway artwork weakly favors Table I; neither is accurate enough to measure surfaces. The manufacturer’s pupil ratio of 2.06 does not distinguish the tables either, because each permits a matching stop within its printed diaphragm gap. The model is therefore a patent-based correlation, not a factory prescription.

## Optical Architecture

The lens is a retrofocus system of eight spherical singlets in eight air-separated groups. The functional front group, L1–L3, has negative net power; the functional rear group, L4–L8, has positive net power. Their separately calculated focal lengths in air are -33.443920 mm and +26.398416 mm. These functional groups differ from the eight physical air-separated groups used for the element/group count. The patent names them the front lens group FLG and the rear lens group RLG, places the diaphragm D inside the rear group, and marks the elements with Roman numerals; the diagram carries those signs (FLG, RLG, LI to LVIII), and this text writes the elements L1 to L8.

At the patent’s e-line indices, the complete model has Gaussian EFL 27.998831 mm and BFD 38.437332 mm, measured from surface 16 into image space. BFD/EFL is 1.372819, establishing the retrofocus description. The first-to-last glass-vertex track is 48.0956 mm. The image plane is placed at the printed back focus of 38.444 mm, giving a first-vertex-to-image distance of 86.5396 mm; it is not moved to the computed paraxial focus.

The patent emphasizes two low-index negative front menisci surrounding a positive biconvex element. Behind the main air separation, a positive meniscus precedes the diaphragm and a biconvex positive element follows it. A negative biconcave element then separates that first positive subgroup from the final positive meniscus and biconvex element. The diaphragm’s placement between L4 and L5 is a distinctive part of the claimed architecture (PDF pages 3–4, printed columns 1–4).

## Element-by-Element Analysis

The following indices and Abbe numbers are the patent’s ne and νe, referenced to the 546.074 nm e line; they are not d-line values. Each focal length is the isolated thick-element focal length in air computed from the final model. It is not an isolated estimate of that element’s aberration contribution in the assembled lens.

### L1 — Negative Meniscus

ne = 1.48914, νe = 70.22. Glass: N-FK5 (Schott catalog equivalent of FK5; production supplier unspecified). f = -95.38 mm.

L1 is a negative meniscus convex toward the object. Together with L3 it forms the diverging part of the front functional group. Its low index and comparatively high Abbe number directly satisfy the patent’s front-meniscus restrictions. The patent associates this choice with limiting front-group color dispersion; that argument is preserved as the patent’s rationale rather than as a measured chromatic-performance result.

### L2 — Biconvex Positive

ne = 1.67133, νe = 41.64. Glass: J-BASF6 (HIKARI catalog equivalent of BaSF6; production supplier unspecified). f = +70.97 mm.

L2 is the positive biconvex element between the two negative front menisci. Its standalone positive power partly offsets their negative power while the complete front group remains negative. The patent requires this element’s Abbe number to exceed 40. The J-BASF6 label is a match on the printed ne and νe, not identification of the historical supplier.

### L3 — Negative Meniscus

ne = 1.48914, νe = 70.22. Glass: N-FK5 (Schott catalog equivalent of FK5; production supplier unspecified). f = -30.29 mm.

L3 is the second negative meniscus convex toward the object. It uses the same glass as L1 but has substantially stronger isolated negative power. Its rear surface precedes the main separation between the front and rear functional groups. Power sign alone does not identify a particular coma or distortion contribution.

### L4 — Positive Meniscus

ne = 1.67341, νe = 46.82. Glass: N-BAF10 (Schott catalog equivalent of BaF10; production supplier unspecified). f = +72.54 mm.

L4 is a positive meniscus with its concave surface toward the diaphragm. The patent emphasizes that this element can remain a single component rather than a split or cemented construction. Its positive power begins the rear functional group; the stop lies in the following air gap.

### L5 — Biconvex Positive

ne = 1.69401, νe = 54.48. Glass: N-LAK9 (Schott catalog equivalent of LaK9; production supplier unspecified). f = +37.71 mm.

L5 is a positive biconvex singlet immediately behind the diaphragm. Together with L4 it forms the first positive subgroup of the rear system. Table III’s relative bending differs from the other numerical tables, but schematic manufacturer artwork is insufficient to turn that distinction into a unique production attribution.

### L6 — Biconcave Negative

ne = 1.79180, νe = 25.87. Glass: SF56A (Schott catalog equivalent of SF56; production supplier unspecified). f = -23.47 mm.

L6 is the biconcave negative singlet between the two positive rear subgroups. Its relatively high index and lower Abbe number contrast with the adjacent lanthanum-crown-class positive elements. This is a plausible dispersive counterweight in the architecture, but a catalog-equivalent dispersion curve does not establish the secondary-spectrum or apochromatic performance of the 1970 lens.

### L7 — Positive Meniscus

ne = 1.69401, νe = 54.48. Glass: N-LAK9 (Schott catalog equivalent of LaK9; production supplier unspecified). f = +55.85 mm.

L7 is a positive meniscus concave toward the diaphragm. It begins the final positive subgroup and shares its glass with L5 and L8. The small air gap in front of L7 is geometrically important: the facing surfaces bow toward one another, so their clear radii limit each other and cannot be enlarged independently.

### L8 — Biconvex Positive

ne = 1.69401, νe = 54.48. Glass: N-LAK9 (Schott catalog equivalent of LaK9; production supplier unspecified). f = +32.37 mm.

L8 is the final positive biconvex singlet. It completes the second positive rear subgroup and leads into the long image-space back clearance needed by the retrofocus arrangement. Its inferred semi-diameter leaves the thinnest edge in the system, 0.388 mm.

## Glass Identification and Selection

Five distinct glasses are used. A comparison of the patent’s ne and νe with the OHARA, HOYA, Schott, HIKARI, CDGM, and Sumita catalogs supports the following families and, in each, a catalog glass that reproduces the printed pair:

| Elements | Patent ne | Patent νe | Glass class and catalog equivalent (catalog ne, νe) |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| L1, L3 | 1.48914 | 70.22 | FK5-class low-index crown: N-FK5, Schott (1.489143, 70.225) |
| L2 | 1.67133 | 41.64 | BASF6-class barium flint: J-BASF6, HIKARI (1.671331, 41.596) |
| L4 | 1.67341 | 46.82 | BAF10/BAFN10-class barium flint: N-BAF10, Schott (1.673411, 46.829) |
| L5, L7, L8 | 1.69401 | 54.48 | LAK9-class lanthanum crown: N-LAK9, Schott (1.694013, 54.482) |
| L6 | 1.79180 | 25.87 | SF56-class dense flint: SF56A, Schott (1.791799, 25.873) |

The patent names no glass. Several current or discontinued catalog glasses are extremely close, some within the rounding of the printed values; that does not establish the supplier, melt, or composition used in the 1970 lens. The data label each element with the catalog glass listed above. The four Schott glasses agree with the printed values within 0.000003 in ne and 0.01 in νe; J-BASF6 agrees within 0.000001 in ne and is 0.04 low in νe. Every patent ne/νe pair is kept, referenced to the e line, and each label selects that glass’s catalog dispersion curve. The 1970 supplier and melts are not established.

No d-line six-digit code is used to identify an e-line glass, and no catalog nC, nF, ng, or partial-dispersion value is entered as if it were a patent value. Each element is traced at the printed ne; at other wavelengths its index is the printed ne plus the index difference of the named catalog glass, so color follows the catalog curve anchored at the patent value. The chromatic results describe that catalog-equivalent model, not measured 1970 melts, with no claim of anomalous dispersion, secondary-spectrum correction, or APO performance.

## Focus Mechanism

The model uses unit focusing: the eight elements keep their spacings and the whole optical assembly moves forward, so only the back focus changes. Table III supplies a single infinity prescription with eight fixed air spaces and no close-focus state.

Unit focusing is an attribution, not a patent statement. Erwin Puts’s notes on the Leica R lenses present the floating element as a feature of the redesigned Elmarit-R 28 mm f/2.8 of 1994, and Leica’s data sheet for that later eight-element, seven-group lens credits a floating element with its performance down to 0.30 m. No source found describes a moving subgroup in the 1970 lens.

| State | Object-to-image distance | Last gap (back focus) | Magnification |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Infinity | ∞ | 38.444 mm | 0 |
| Close focus | 0.30 m | 42.0387 mm | −0.128 |

The 3.5947 mm extension is calculated by paraxial imaging for the production minimum focus distance of 0.30 m; it is not a printed value.

## Conditional Expressions

Claim 3 specifies the following architecture and numerical restrictions, reproduced without changing the selected table:

- Main separation d6/f = 0.3330 < 0.35.
- Separation plus L4 center thickness, (d6+d7)/f = 0.5829 < 0.60.
- First vertex to the stop, divided by nominal f, is 1.15925 < 1.30. This uses the modeled stop position, which the patent does not print.
- L1 and L3 have ne = 1.48914 < 1.50 and νe = 70.22 > 65.
- L2 has νe = 41.64 > 40.

The explanatory text also says the glass-vertex track can be less than 1.75f and the front diameter can be less than 1.25f. The modeled track is 1.7177 times nominal f. Its inferred front diameter is 35.38 mm, or 1.263571f, so the latter optional capability is not reproduced. No aperture is tabulated for Table III; the diameter is a modeling choice, not a patent or manufacturer dimension.

## Model Scope and Limitations

All patent dimensions are scaled uniformly by 28. The system is all spherical, so there are no aspheric coefficients. The paraxial focal length reproduces the normalization f = 1 within the printed precision of the radii, distances, and indices. The paraxial back focus is 0.0067 mm shorter than the printed 38.444 mm, within the ±0.014 mm half-digit of the printed 1.373 f.

The patent does not print the stop position. The model places the stop 2.0930 mm behind surface 8, leaving 0.9142 mm before surface 9: 0.70 of the 3.0072 mm diaphragm gap, taken from the PhotonsToPhotos reconstruction. The patent figure draws the diaphragm at 0.72 ± 0.07 of that gap, which is consistent. The stop semi-diameter of 6.750736 mm is set by real-ray tracing of the axial f/2.8 beam; it is not independent confirmation of a factory diaphragm. The corresponding paraxial exit/entrance pupil-diameter ratio is 2.049255, against the manufacturer’s rounded 2.06.

No semi-diameter is printed. The values follow the patent’s single drawing, by way of the secondary source and a re-measurement of the 300 dpi page; seven of the eight elements agree with the drawing within 2%. The drawing shows L6 and L7 with square-cut rims, 8.47 mm and 8.97 mm high, meeting at the edge. The Table III spheres of facing surfaces 12 and 13 meet at 8.265 mm, so only one of them can pass that height. Surface 13 follows L7’s drawn rim at 8.82 mm, the same as surface 14, and surfaces 11 and 12 are both 8.25 mm, which squares L6 against L7 just inside the contact height. At 8.25 mm the two surface sags take 0.996 of the air gap and leave 0.006 mm of air at the rim; the data set the gap allowance to 1.00 for this lens because the pair is drawn in edge contact. The drawing also gives L1 and L3 a flat annular land behind the concave face: their outer rims are 17.8 mm and 11.9 mm high, while the concave spheres end at about 13.9 mm and 8.5 mm. The model stores those curve ends for surfaces 2 and 6 (13.85 mm and 8.3 mm), so the diagram closes each of the two elements with a straight edge where the drawing has a step. The steepest rim slope is 49.5°, and the thinnest element edge is 0.388 mm.

Real rays clear the full axial f/2.8 beam, and chief rays through the stop center pass the whole ±38° field; the 36×24 mm format corner is reached at 37.98°. The data declare the patent’s 76° field explicitly because a first-order estimate from the modeled apertures gives only ±35.4°. Rays are still clipped at the modeled apertures.

At f/2.8 a two-dimensional sample of the nominal entrance pupil transmits 100.0% at 22.8°, 94.9% at 30°, and 68.3% at 38°. These are geometric fractions for the inferred apertures, not measured relative illumination; they exclude cos⁴ falloff, coatings, absorption, diffraction, and factory tolerances.

The surface-by-surface Petzval sum is 0.006204587 mm⁻¹ at the e-line indices. This is the paraxial refracting-surface sum Σφ/(nn′), not a finite-field best-focus measurement.
