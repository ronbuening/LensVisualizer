# LEICA SUMMICRON 100mm f/2 (US 2,622,478 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** [US 2,622,478 A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2622478A/en)

**Inventors:** Gustav Kleineberg; Otto Zimmermann

**Assignee:** Ernst Leitz GmbH

**Filed:** 20 July 1950

**Published:** 23 December 1952

**German priority:** 9 January 1950

**Example details:** Example 2, Figure 2; the numerical table and Seidel coefficients on PDF page 3.

The patent gives two numerical examples at f = 100 and f/2, each with a 50° full field. Example 2 has seven elements in six groups. The table specifies no absolute length unit; the model treats its dimensions as millimetres. The name identifies the Summicron family while retaining the patent's f = 100 design; the production collapsible Summicron was a 5 cm f/2 lens, and its correspondence to a particular numerical example remains unsettled.

Example 1 has seven elements in five groups, including a cemented front meniscus pair, and Figure 1 shows an additional flint plate between the objective and the emulsion. Example 2 separates that meniscus pair and has no additional plate. Its seventh element remains a powered lens.

## Optical Architecture

The modified Gauss arrangement is **1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5+6 | 7**. Four separate front elements precede the diaphragm; a cemented negative-positive pair and a positive singlet follow it. The sole cemented interface is r10, between Elements 5 and 6. All thirteen refracting surfaces are spherical, and their radius signs are printed explicitly.

The patent describes high-index positive glasses, a split front positive member with a dispersing air lens, and an optional separation of the object-side meniscus into two elements. Elements 1, 3 and 6 share nd 1.6923 and ν 54.7. The five printed air spaces are 0.54, 0.20, 0.39, 20.02 and 1.96 mm. The diaphragm lies in the 20.02 mm space between r8 and r9.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent explicitly defines nd as the refractive index for the d-line, interpreted as helium d at 587.6 nm. The printed indices and Abbe numbers are retained throughout.

Element 4 matches the optical coordinates of CDGM QF3: its catalogue row gives nd 1.57502 and ν 41.31, with Δnd +0.00001 and Δν +0.01. Five other elements use spectral proxies:

- Elements 1, 3 and 6: N-LAK9 (Schott), Δnd −0.00130 and Δν +0.01.
- Element 2: SF12 (Sumita), Δnd −0.00186 and Δν +0.07.
- Element 7: S-LAM3 (Ohara), Δnd −0.00015 and Δν +0.03.

These differences are catalogue minus printed values at the patent's d-line. Each proxy lies within 0.002 in index and 0.25 in Abbe number. The present-day catalogue curves supply only the shape of the dispersion curve, so colour analysis for these elements follows real glass rather than an Abbe-number estimate. Neither the coordinate match nor the proxies identify the historical supplier or melt. Element 5 remains unmatched because no catalogue row falls within both tolerances; its dispersion is inferred from nd and ν. The patent supplies no additional spectral indices or measured partial dispersions.

The text discusses radiation from thorium-bearing optical glass and the use of flint glass to absorb it before it reaches the emulsion. This provides historical context for the Summicron association but does not identify the composition of every element in either numerical example or a production specimen.

## Focus Mechanism and Aperture

The model is focused at infinity. The patent gives no close-focus spacing, movement or minimum focusing distance. The image plane is the calculated paraxial plane, 55.44 mm behind the last surface; that back focus is not printed.

The diaphragm position is inferred within the printed 20.02 mm central space: 11.25 mm behind r8 and 8.77 mm ahead of r9, at z = 40.73 mm from the first vertex. Figure 2 places the drawn iris at about 0.51 of the gap, compared with 0.56 in the model. The printed coma coefficients refer to a pupil at r1 and cannot independently locate the physical diaphragm.

At nominal f/2 the paraxial stop radius is 15.468 mm. Finite rays give an entrance-equivalent aperture of about f/2.01 and an image-space sine equivalent of f/2.00. A finite marginal ray corresponding to f/2 would require a stop radius of about 15.532 mm. The f/2–f/22 stop sequence is illustrative; the patent prints no aperture ladder.

All clear semi-diameters are estimates: r1–r13 use 28, 27, 27, 25, 21, 21, 20, 19, 20, 20, 20, 22.4 and 22.4 mm. Figure 2 is schematic: its thicknesses and air spaces do not share a consistent scale. Its relative rim heights support the square edge of Element 3 and the common doublet rim. Element 3 clears the f/2 axial ray at r5 by about 0.08 mm. The doublet and rear singlet remain larger than the drawn proportions to preserve oblique illumination. Wide-open geometric transmission is 100% on axis, 69.2% at 16.7°, 53.6% at 19.4° and 16.4% at 25°. The smallest common-radius glass thickness is 2.76 mm. The front 0.54 mm air space leaves 0.38 mm at 27 mm radius; the 0.39 mm space leaves 0.23 mm at 20 mm; the central gap leaves 4.73 mm at 19 mm. The stop plane clears the r8 and r9 rims by approximately 1.71 and 2.33 mm. No flat lands are inferred. Barrel dimensions remain unknown.

## First-Order Properties and Stated Field

The calculated EFL is 99.96 mm against the printed f = 100, a difference of −0.04 mm. The first-to-last-surface distance is 69.95 mm, and the first-surface-to-image distance is 125.39 mm. No source back focus or total image distance is printed.

The printed Seidel P (image curvature) and A (spherical aberration) columns reproduce surface by surface. At the patent's normalization, the calculated P sum is 0.1997 against the printed 0.1995; every A entry agrees within 0.0006. This independently supports the printed radii, indices and spacings.

The modeled field follows the printed 50.0° full angle, or ±25.0°. At 25.0° the real chief ray reaches an image semi-height of 45.97 mm, giving an inferred image-circle diameter of 91.94 mm at this model's dimensions. The geometry permits chief rays to approximately 26.6°. The iris sets the axial aperture, and the 25.0° chief ray reaches the image. The rear elements vignette the oblique bundle, and one marginal extreme is unresolved. Field reach alone does not establish complete pupil illumination or measured production performance.

## Historical and Commercial Context

The association with the collapsible Leitz Summicron 5 cm f/2 of 1953–60 is plausible at the patent-family level. A [Leitz Summicron brochure](https://www.pacificrimcamera.com/rl/01536/01536.pdf) reportedly states that the Summicron's basic design is protected by US 2,622,478. The Leica Wiki's [entry for Gustav Kleinberg](https://wiki.l-camera-forum.com/leica-wiki.en/index.php/Gustav_Kleinberg) associates a seven-element, six-group Kleineberg–Zimmermann patent filed on 20 July 1950 with that lens. The patent's discussion of thorium glass also fits the family history.

Example 2 agrees with the production lens's seven-element, six-group count, but that count alone cannot establish the production prescription. Which numerical example entered production remains unsettled. The later Rigid/Dual Range Summicron prescription from DE 939,956 B has different radii and remains a separate design.
