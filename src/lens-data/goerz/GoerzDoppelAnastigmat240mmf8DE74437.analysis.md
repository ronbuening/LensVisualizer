# Goerz Doppel-Anastigmat 240mm f/8 — DE 74437 C

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** DE 74437 C (Kaiserliches Patentamt, Patentschrift Nr. 74437, Klasse 57: Photographie)<br>
**Effective date:** 20 December 1892 ("Patentirt im Deutschen Reiche vom 20. December 1892 ab")<br>
**Issued:** 4 April 1894<br>
**Assignee:** C. P. Goerz in Schöneberg bei Berlin, the patentee; the document names no inventor<br>
**Title:** *Sphärisch, chromatisch und astigmatisch corrigirtes Objectiv*<br>
**Example details:** the one numerical example, unnumbered, on page 2 (f = 240 mm, largest effective aperture
30 mm); Fig. 3 of the drawing sheet shows it in its barrel at natural size

The patent opens with the problem it sets out to solve. Since 1886 the Jena glassworks had supplied crowns of
higher index than the flints they are cemented to, the combination that removes astigmatism. Lenses built that way,
among them Hartnack's Anastigmat computed by Miethe in 1887, could not also correct spherical aberration, because
that correction needs a crown of lower index than its flint. The patent credits Paul Rudolph's Zeiss Anastigmats
(D.R.P. 56109) with first showing that a fast anastigmat could be made, and says its own inventor took a different
route: a double objective whose two halves are each three cemented lenses, carrying one cemented surface of each
kind.

It describes two such halves. In the first (Fig. 1) a negative flint b is enclosed between two positive crowns, one
(a) of higher and one (a¹) of lower index than the flint; in the second (Fig. 2) a positive crown is enclosed
between two negative flints, one of higher and one of lower index. The patent finds the second form leads to
unfavourable lens shapes and glasses and keeps only the first. Claim 1 protects a double objective whose halves are
each corrected on their own by three cemented lenses with one collective cemented surface (for the astigmatism) and
one dispersive cemented surface (for the spherical aberration), in either arrangement; claim 2 protects one such
half used alone as a photographic objective. The patent adds that the symmetry of the example is not a feature of
the type: the halves could differ in form, or follow Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 respectively, though it calls such variants
pointless in practice because the simpler symmetrical arrangement meets every condition.

The patent prints a single numerical example, with no number, and Fig. 3 draws it in section at natural size in a
barrel with a plate stop through a slot between the cells. The document names only C. P. Goerz as patentee. The
design is Emil von Höegh's: von Rohr (1899) lists the patent under his name, and von Höegh, who had worked on
photographic objectives in Paul Wächter's workshop in Friedenau, brought the design to Goerz. The firm sold the type
as the Doppel-Anastigmat, later as the Dagor. The example's own ratings, 240 mm and 1:8, are not those of a
catalogued series (Series III was rated 1:7.7, Series IV 1:11), so the file carries no mount or format and names
the example in its title.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is a symmetrical double lens of six elements in two cemented groups: two identical triplets face
each other across an 11.00 mm air space. Each cell is 13.751 mm thick on the axis and the vertex length is
38.502 mm. The patent gives no numerical stop position; Fig. 3 draws the stop plate in the middle of the air space,
and the model centres the stop there.

| Surface | Index step | Surface power (mm⁻¹) | Patent's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| r1 = +45.835 | air → 1.61310 | +0.01338 | outer collective face |
| r2 = −54.324 | 1.61310 → 1.56804 | +0.00083 | collective cemented surface (astigmatic correction) |
| r3 = +19.853 | 1.56804 → 1.51497 | −0.00267 | dispersive cemented surface (spherical correction) |
| r4 = +49.088 | 1.51497 → air | −0.01049 | inner face, concave toward the stop |

The rear cell mirrors these four surfaces. The example traces to an EFL of 240.242 mm, 0.10 % above the stated
240 mm, with a paraxial back focus of 223.265 mm against the patent's central-ray focus of 223.275 mm. Each cell has
a focal length of +420.0 mm: the single lens of claim 2, at 1.75 times the focal length of the pair. The
Petzval sum is 1.140 × 10⁻³ mm⁻¹, a Petzval radius of 877 mm or 3.65 f.

The patent prints its radii without signs. The claim, the Fig. 1 cell and the Fig. 3 section fix them: the outer lens
is biconvex, the enclosed flint biconcave, the inner crown a meniscus concave toward the stop.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 / L6 — Biconvex Positive, outer crown

nD = 1.61310, nG′ = 1.62683; no nC or nF, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nG′ only); von Rohr's heavy barium
silicate crown. f = +41.7 mm.

This is the patent's crown a, of higher index than the flint. Its strongly curved outer face carries most of the
cell's positive power, and its rear face meets L2 with a small fall in index across a surface concave toward L1: the
weak collective cemented surface the claim assigns to the astigmatic correction. With the printed radii the lens
reaches a knife edge at 18.73 mm; at the 18 mm rim it keeps 0.58 mm, the thin edge Fig. 3 draws. L6 is its mirror
image.

### L2 / L5 — Biconcave Negative, enclosed flint

nD = 1.56804, nG′ = 1.58182; no nC or nF, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nG′ only); von Rohr's baryta light
flint. f = −25.4 mm.

The flint b of the claim, of intermediate index, is the negative member of both cemented pairs. Its rear face,
r3 = 19.853 mm, is the most strongly curved surface of the system and the dispersive cemented surface: the index
falls into L3 across a face concave toward L3. Fig. 3 finishes L2 with a flat annulus from the knife edge of L3 out
to the rim, inside the cell mount; the model ends its rear face at the 14.7 mm inner rim. L5 mirrors it.

### L3 / L4 — Positive Meniscus, inner crown

nD = 1.51497, nG′ = 1.52663; no nC or nF, so no νD. Glass: Unmatched (patent nD/nG′ only); von Rohr's soft silicate
crown. f = +61.5 mm.

The patent's crown a¹, of lower index than the flint, sits next to the stop, convex toward L2 and concave toward the
stop. Its inner face is the main negative contribution to the Petzval sum. The lens runs to a knife edge at
15.10 mm, just outside the inner lip of the cell mount that Fig. 3 draws at 14.73 mm. L4 is its mirror image.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent defines each glass by two indices, for the solar D line and for the hydrogen line it prints as "H8"
under the column head nG′. The second is G′ = Hγ (about 434.0 nm), the line of the printed FG′ foci. The patent names
no glass types beyond crown and flint.

| Glass | nD | nG′ | nG′ − nD | Elements | Von Rohr's name (p. 375) | ν̄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| a | 1.61310 | 1.62683 | 0.01373 | L1, L6 | Schweres Barium-Silikat-Crown (heavy barium silicate crown) | 44.7 |
| b | 1.56804 | 1.58182 | 0.01378 | L2, L5 | Baryt-Leichtflint (baryta light flint) | 41.2 |
| a¹ | 1.51497 | 1.52663 | 0.01166 | L3, L4 | Weiches Silikat-Crown (soft silicate crown) | 44.2 |

G′ is neither the F line nor the mercury g line the schema stores as ng, and the patent prints no nC, so no Abbe
number can be formed. The data file keeps nD and records nG′ in comments only, with no catalog glass as a proxy; the
model traces at nD alone and shows no colour error. Von Rohr's ν̄ = (nD − 1)/(nG′ − nD) is computed from exactly these
pairs (44.65, 41.22, 44.17), and is given here only as context.

The index order a > b > a¹ makes the two cemented surfaces work in opposite senses, as the claim requires: a dense
crown against a lighter flint at the collective surface, a light crown against a denser flint at the dispersive
surface. Relative to its power the flint disperses most toward the blue (ν̄ 41.2), the two crowns less and nearly
alike (44.7 and 44.2), so the one flint serves as the achromatizing partner of both. The patent's own computation (page 2) gives the axial colour as the difference of the G′ and D
foci: −0.210 mm for central rays, +0.040 mm in the F:12 zone and +0.053 mm at F:8. The paraxial value traced from the
printed nG′ outside the engine, −0.212 mm, matches the first.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity prescription. The model is infinity-only: the last gap is the computed paraxial
back focus, and no focusing motion or close-focus spacing is modelled.

## Aperture, Field and Rims

The patent states a largest effective aperture of 30 mm, "the eighth part of the focal length", and computes its
marginal ray at F:8. The model opens the iris to f/8 at the traced EFL: an entrance-pupil radius of 15.02 mm and a
stop radius of 13.13 mm. The f/8 axial beam reaches 15.02 mm at r1 and 14.13 mm at r8, inside the 18 mm rims, and
13.37 and 13.22 mm at r3 and r4, inside 14.7 mm. The plate stop Fig. 3 draws has an opening of about 23.5 mm, a
smaller stop than the 26.3 mm the largest aperture needs.

Traced on axis, the longitudinal spherical aberration at f/8 is −0.08 mm at the rim of the pupil, −1.92 mm at 0.85,
−2.11 mm at 0.7 and −1.41 mm at 0.5; the marginal ray crosses zero just outside the pupil, near 1:7.97, and the
deepest zone is −2.15 mm at a height of 11.3 mm. The patent's own foci put the F:12 zone 1.92 mm inside the
central focus and the F:8 ray 0.17 mm beyond it; the exact trace gives 2.04 mm and 0.11 mm inside, within 0.13 mm in
the zone and 0.28 mm at the rim.

The patent prints no field angle. It computes astigmatism with a chief ray at 30°, where it finds the sagittal and
meridional image points 1.2 mm apart with the meridional image flat, a residue it judges no worse than the axial
zone. The model traces to ω = 36°, the half-field von Rohr computed for this example. The chief ray passes there
with heights of 11.71 mm at r1 and r8 and 4.91 mm at r4 and r5, and the paraxial rim limit is 46.7°. Oblique beams
are cut at the rims: a meridional trace through the model passes about 89 % of the stop-filling beam at 20°, 66 % at
30° and 47 % at 36°.

The clear radii are measured from Fig. 3, which the patent says is drawn at natural size and which is to scale along
the axis within 0.2 mm. The outer surfaces take the patent's 36 mm free lens opening, 18 mm; the drawn glass is
36.96 mm across with a narrow barrel bead over its rim. The inner surfaces take the drawn inner lips of the two cell
mounts, 14.7 mm, just inside the knife edge of the inner menisci.

## Design Heritage and Context

Moritz von Rohr's *Theorie und Geschichte des photographischen Objektivs* (1899) prints this example reduced to
f = 100 as Fig. 138, "E. von Höegh: Doppel-Anastigmat", with r = 19.1, 22.7, 8.3 and 20.5, d = 3.1, 0.8 and 1.9,
a cell-to-stop distance of 2.3 on each side and the patent's three nD values, computed for 1:8.0 and ω = 36°. The
patent's values divided by 2.4 and rounded to 0.1 mm give his table, except r2 = r7: the patent's 54.324 mm reduces
to 22.64, where he prints 22.7. The patent's own focal data confirm its reading: 54.524, which would round to 22.7,
puts the paraxial focus 0.29 mm beyond the printed central focus. Von Rohr reports full spherical correction near
1:7.8 with a zone of not quite −1.0 mm and astigmatism strictly corrected at 30°; this transcription, reduced to
f = 100, gives zero near 1:7.97 and a zone of −0.90 mm.

Von Rohr places the cell in his form 2aα of Rudolph's anastigmatic principle and reads it as two achromats sharing
the middle lens: a new achromat outside (dense crown with a lighter flint) and an old achromat toward the stop (light
crown with a denser flint), the inner one of longer focal length. Von Höegh had published on the spherical
correction of telescope objectives in 1886; he presented the new objective to the Berlin Verein zur Förderung der
Photographie on 15 May 1893.

Goerz issued it in two series, Series III at 1:7.7 as a universal lens and Series IV at 1:11 for reproduction, and
licensed Ross & Co. in London to make it. Von Rohr discusses the priority question with Rudolph at length: Rudolph's
Zeiss anastigmat single lens of the same type dates from the end of 1891, his English application of 22 April 1893
preceded Goerz's complete specification of 2 June 1893, and von Rohr concludes that the two found the type
independently, Rudolph seeking the best single cell and von Höegh the best doublet.

The symmetry removes coma, distortion and lateral colour at unit magnification and keeps them small at infinity, so
the design work lies in the spherical, chromatic and astigmatic correction of the single cell, the three corrections
the patent's title names; the same separate correction is what lets each cell serve alone as a longer lens.
