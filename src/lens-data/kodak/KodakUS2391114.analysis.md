# KODAK 100mm f/2.25 (US 2,391,114 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 2,391,114 A, _Rapid Objective_

**Inventors:** George H. Aklin

**Assignee:** Eastman Kodak Company

**Filed:** November 30, 1943

**Published:** December 18, 1945

**Example details:** Example 2, Figure 2; the numerical table appears on PDF pages 1 and 2.

Example 2 describes a 100 mm f/2.25 photographic triplet. Example 1 is a different f/2.5 prescription; the patent says it covers a slightly larger angular field. No manufactured product is documented for Example 2.

The printed prescription gives EFL 99.92 mm and paraxial back focus 73.493 mm, compared with the stated 100 mm and 73.5 mm. The paraxial image plane lies 135.19 mm behind the first vertex. These differences fit the table's printed precision.

## Optical Architecture

Three air-spaced spherical singlets form a positive–negative–positive triplet. The outer elements are biconvex, with their stronger surfaces facing outward; the central element is biconcave, with its weaker surface facing forward. The first-to-last vertex distance is 61.7 mm.

The patent favors relatively weak positive elements: their standalone focal lengths sum to about 120.74 mm, or 1.207 times the stated focal length, within the claimed 1.15–1.30 range. All six radii satisfy the magnitude limits in claim 2.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### Element 1 — Biconvex Positive

nD = 1.755, νD = 47.2. Glass: Unmatched. Standalone focal length in air: +66.01 mm.

The front element collects the incoming beam. Its stronger front surface and weaker rear surface distribute the positive power ahead of the negative middle element. Its center thickness is 16.1 mm.

### Element 2 — Biconcave Negative

nD = 1.720, νD = 29.3. Glass: Unmatched. Standalone focal length in air: −35.92 mm.

The more dispersive negative element balances the power and chromatic contributions of the outer positive elements. Its weaker front surface faces the first air space. The 8.1 mm center thickness and the front/rear clear-radius estimates of 27/23 mm give a tapered section.

### Element 3 — Biconvex Positive

nD = 1.734, νD = 51.1. Glass: TAC4 (Hoya), a catalog equivalent. Standalone focal length in air: +54.73 mm.

The rear element converges the beam toward the image. Its stronger rear surface faces the image space, and its center thickness is 17.9 mm. The modeled clear radius is 25 mm.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent defines N at the D line (taken as sodium, 589.3 nm) on PDF page 3. The high-index positive glasses, including nD = 1.755 in the front element, are consistent with lanthanum-type crowns; the patent does not identify their composition, supplier or melt.

Hoya TAC4 supplies the rear element's dispersion curve: its D-line values are nD = 1.73387 and νD = 51.05, close to the printed pair. Its name identifies an optical equivalent, without establishing Kodak's supplier or melt. All elements retain the printed nD/νD values; the first two remain Unmatched and use Abbe-based dispersion estimates. The patent gives no individual C-, F- or g-line indices or partial-dispersion data.

## Focus Mechanism

The model is focused at infinity. The patent gives no close-focus distances, group movements or focusing law. All three groups remain fixed.

## Inferred Stop, Clear Apertures and Image Plane

The patent does not dimension the diaphragm. The model places it 7.2 mm into the 10.2 mm rear air space, leaving 3.0 mm before the last element and putting the stop 40.8 mm behind the first vertex. Figure 4 supports a rear-space stop: at image height 27 mm, the modeled sagittal focus shift is −0.45 mm versus about −0.48 mm in the drawing. A front-space stop gives a tangential shift of +0.2 mm at 36 mm image height, against about −1.4 mm in the patent. These comparisons support the rear air space without fixing the exact stop position.

The estimated stop radius is 16.04 mm. It gives f/2.25 paraxially, while exact axial rays give an entrance-equivalent working aperture of about f/2.32. A real marginal ray at the nominal f/2.25 entrance radius would require a stop radius of about 16.57 mm. The aperture estimate remains a limitation of the model.

Clear semi-diameters of 28/28/27/23/25/25 mm are estimates. Figure 1 suggests smaller rims, about 22.9/22.9/21.2/18.1/22.9/22.9 mm, including a chamfer at the rear of the middle element. Substituting those readings reduces the modeled 15.0° transmission from about 91% to 70%; this question remains open. The present rear rim of the middle element clears the stop plane by 0.532 mm, and the minimum glass edge thickness is 7.58 mm.

## Aberration Curves and Field

Figure 3 compares spherical aberration and the sine condition with a prior-art triplet. At entrance-ray heights 8, 12, 16, 20 and 22 mm, modeled longitudinal spherical aberration is −0.22, −0.43, −0.55, −0.27 and +0.23 mm. Approximate readings of the patent's curve are −0.29, −0.48, −0.53, −0.31 and +0.20 mm. The patent emphasizes improved intermediate-zone spherical aberration and coma; the model follows the general shape of the plotted spherical-aberration curve.

Figure 4 marks 15.0°, 18.0° and 24.0°. The model retains a 15.0° half-field; the larger plot markers do not establish usable coverage. At 15.0°, the real chief ray reaches image height 26.74 mm. The inferred 53.44 mm image circle retains about a 0.1% margin inside that chief-ray diameter; it is not a published film format or product dimension.
