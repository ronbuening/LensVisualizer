# KODAK 100mm f/6 (US 1,880,394 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** [US 1,880,394 A, _Photographic Objective_](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/1880394)

**Inventors:** Fred E. Altman

**Assignee:** Eastman Kodak Company

**Filed:** December 11, 1930

**Published:** October 4, 1932

**Example details:** Sole numerical example; drawing on PDF p. 1, prescription on PDF p. 2, additional focal lengths and claims on PDF p. 3.

The patent describes an f/6 objective for aerial cameras, emphasizing correction of astigmatism over a relatively narrow field. It gives a focal length of 100 units without assigning a physical unit; the model treats each unit as one millimetre. The computed D-line focal length is 100.012 mm, 0.012 mm above the stated value. No production lens is documented for this prescription.

## Optical Architecture

Five elements form three air-spaced groups, **1 | 2+3 | 4+5**: a positive front singlet, a negative middle doublet and a positive rear doublet. R4 and R7 are the cemented interfaces. All eight refracting surfaces are spherical. The table prints radius magnitudes; their signs follow the drawing and agree with the printed positive and negative element powers.

The middle doublet combines a positive high-index crown with a stronger, lower-index negative element. The patent describes this combination as hyperchromatic: its colour correction compensates the rest of the objective while retaining the negative power needed for field flattening. The rear doublet combines another low-index negative element with a strong positive crown. The calculated middle and rear doublet focal lengths are −25.83 and +52.85 mm, compared with the printed −25.82 and +52.85.

PDF p. 3 also prints a focal length of −148.12 for the “front component.” Interpreting this as the assembly through R5 gives −148.08 mm; the front singlet alone is positive. The small differences in the printed component and element focal lengths remain without altering the table.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent prints D-line indices, G′ indices and Abbe numbers for every element. The model retains the D line, taken as sodium at 589.3 nm. None of the three distinct index/Abbe pairs matches a catalogue glass within the printed precision, so all five glass identities remain unresolved. The patent names no glass supplier or melt.

Element 3 uses E-FEL1 (Hoya) as a spectral proxy: catalogue minus printed values at sodium D are ΔnD = −0.00192 and Δν = +0.01, within the proxy tolerances of 0.002 in index and 0.25 in Abbe number. The printed index and Abbe number are kept; the catalogue curve supplies only the dispersion shape, allowing colour analysis to follow a real glass curve rather than an Abbe-number estimate. This does not identify Kodak's historical supplier or melt. Elements 1, 2, 4 and 5 remain Unmatched because no catalogue row falls within those tolerances at the printed line.

## Focus Mechanism and Aperture

The model is focused at infinity; the patent supplies no close-focus data or focusing mechanism. The image plane is the calculated paraxial back focus, 88.64 mm behind R8; the patent prints no back-focus distance. The first-to-last surface distance is 22.21 mm and the total distance to this image plane is 110.85 mm.

The patent places the diaphragm between the middle and rear doublets but gives no numerical station or bore. The drawing supports a station 3.11 mm behind R5 and 4.43 mm before R6, retaining the printed 7.54 mm air space. This position places the zero-astigmatism node at about 14.0° in a D-line Coddington trace, consistent with the text's statement that it lies below 15° from the axis. A position 4.6 mm behind R5 instead gives about 15.9°.

The paraxial iris radius is 6.977 mm for f/6.00. An axial real ray launched at the nominal entrance-pupil radius reaches a stop height of about 7.014 mm, so the real-ray and paraxial aperture definitions differ slightly. The stop plane clears the adjacent glass. The f/8–f/22 settings are conventional additions, rather than settings printed by the patent.

## Field Coverage and Model Limits

The patent describes a field of about 15° and contrasts its astigmatic node with the approximately 23° off-axis node of ordinary aerial objectives. Its explicit references to angles from the optical axis support reading 15° as a half-field. The model therefore covers ±15°, or 30° in total.

At 15.0°, the real chief ray reaches an image height of 26.80 mm on the paraxial image plane. Twice that height gives the recorded 53.6 mm image circle. This is derived from the stated angular field at the model's dimensions; no film format or mount is specified. The calculated astigmatic fields remain within about 0.11 mm of the image plane out to this angle.

All clear semi-diameters are estimates: R1/R2 10.6 mm, R3/R4 9.2 mm, R5 8.1 mm, R6 7.7 mm and R7/R8 8.6 mm. Both doublets taper toward the iris, following the drawing's curve ends at about 88% and 89% of their respective outer heights. The drawing is not uniformly to scale, so these proportions do not establish absolute clear diameters. The axial f/6 ray reaches 6.88 mm at R5 and 7.29 mm at R6; both rims also pass the 15.0° chief ray. At the field edge the front and rear rims remove about 14% of the meridional bundle width on one side. Across the full pupil, geometric transmission is about 96.8% at 10°, 93.1% at 12.5° and 86.6% at 15°; these figures describe ray survival, without absorption, reflections or angular illumination falloff. The narrowest shared glass edge is about 0.204 mm in element 2; elements 1 and 5 have edges of about 0.380 and 0.422 mm. The minimum shared air clearance is about 4.176 mm, and the steepest rim angle is about 35.9°.

The patent claims zonal spherical-aberration variation below 0.004% of focal length at f/6. This is a source performance statement; neither the first-order agreement nor a calculated geometric MTF establishes measured performance or a complete spectral correction.
