# KODAK 100mm f/1.9 (US 2,432,387 Design)

## Patent Reference / Design Identification

**Patent:** [US 2,432,387 A — Lenses for Cinematography](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2432387A/en)\
**Inventors:** Philip E. Creighton\
**Assignee:** Eastman Kodak Company\
**Filed:** 9 February 1946\
**Published:** 9 December 1947\
**Example details:** Example III, Figure 4; numerical table repeated in column 2 of the specification.

The patent describes an economical, fast cinematography objective. Example III is one of three numerical examples corresponding to the four-element section in Figure 1. Claim 4 gives a rounded version of its prescription in focal-length units. The Figure 4 table prints EF = 100 mm and f/1.9; both Example III tables print BF = 63.9 mm.

The design is possibly related to the four-element 25 mm f/1.9 Cine Ektar. G. H. Aklin's 1960 account describes that lens as a lower-priced alternative to the seven-element 25 mm f/1.4 Cine Ektar. Kodak attribution, four air-spaced elements, relative aperture and cinematography use support the association, but no source identifies which of Creighton's examples, if any, entered production. The title therefore uses the patent's 100 mm design scale. Aklin's separate six-element 25 mm f/1.9 design belongs to US 2,682,198 A and is a different prescription. [Aklin, Journal of the SMPTE 69(4), 1960](https://journal.smpte.org/periodicals/Journal%20of%20the%20SMPTE/69/4/20/07268486.pdf).

## Optical Architecture

Four air-spaced singlets form four groups, with positive / positive / negative / positive power. All eight refracting surfaces are spherical. The first two elements are menisci convex toward the object; the third is biconcave and the fourth biconvex. The patent permits a cemented component as a variation, but Example III contains no cemented pair.

The air spaces are 0.7, 9.3 and 13.2 mm. The distance between the first and last lens vertices is 73.0 mm. The inferred stop splits the last air space into 10.0 mm after R6 and 3.2 mm before R7, placing it 46.6 mm behind the first vertex. Figure 1 does not show a diaphragm, so neither this station nor the physical iris opening is established by the patent.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### Element 1 — Positive Meniscus

nD = 1.588, νD = 53.4. Glass: Unmatched (nD 1.588 / ν 53.4). Focal length in air: +115.01 mm.

The front element begins convergence of the incoming beam. Its radii are +61.0 and +576.0 mm, and its center thickness is 12.3 mm. The weak rear surface gives it the meniscus form described by the patent. Its two estimated clear semi-diameters are 31 mm.

### Element 2 — Positive Meniscus

nD = 1.611, νD = 57.2. Glass: Unmatched (nD 1.611 / ν 57.2). Focal length in air: +259.28 mm.

The second meniscus contributes weaker positive power between the front collector and the negative element. Its radii are +48.2 and +63.5 mm, with a 10.6 mm center thickness. The estimated front and rear clear semi-diameters are 29 and 26 mm.

### Element 3 — Biconcave Negative

nD = 1.720, νD = 29.3. Glass: S-TIH18 (Ohara), spectral proxy. Focal length in air: −41.56 mm.

This is the sole negative element and the most dispersive glass in the prescription. It balances the surrounding positive elements. Its radii are −193.0 and +35.7 mm, placing the stronger curvature toward the rear, as the patent describes. Its center thickness is 3.7 mm; estimated front and rear clear semi-diameters are 23 and 20.4 mm.

### Element 4 — Biconvex Positive

The patent prints nD = 1.697, νD = 56.1. Glass: H-LaK12 (CDGM), a present-day catalog equivalent; the glass Kodak used is unknown. Focal length in air: +55.36 mm.

The thick rear element supplies strong positive power after the stop. Its radii are +84.5 and −63.0 mm, with a center thickness of 23.2 mm and estimated clear semi-diameters of 27 mm. Using absolute radii and the patent's stated F = 100 mm, F/|R7| + F/|R8| = 2.771, matching the printed 2.77 and the claim's 2.7–2.8 range.

## Glass Identification / Selection

The patent explicitly defines its indices for the sodium D line, about 589.3 nm, and prints an Abbe number for every element. Those values remain unchanged. Element 3 uses S-TIH18 (Ohara) as a spectral proxy, with catalog-minus-printed differences at sodium D of ΔnD = +0.00129 and ΔνD = −0.08. Its catalog curve supplies only the dispersion shape, so colour analysis follows a real glass while retaining the printed index and Abbe number. Elements 1 and 2 remain Unmatched because no catalog row lies within index 0.002 and Abbe number 0.25 at sodium D.

Element 4 retains H-LaK12 (CDGM) as a catalog equivalent supplying a spectral proxy: nD ≈ 1.69669 and νD ≈ 56.17, with ΔnD ≈ −0.00031 and ΔνD ≈ +0.07. Neither catalog name identifies Kodak's historical supplier or melt.

The negative third element has substantially greater dispersion than the positive elements. The patent provides no partial-dispersion measurements or anomalous-dispersion designation. Chromatic and broadband results consequently depend on estimated dispersion for the unmatched glasses; sodium D-line geometric results have the strongest source support.

## Focus Mechanism

The model is focused at infinity. The patent gives no close-focus distances, movement law or finite-conjugate states. The stop radius derived from the stated f/1.9 is 17.667 mm, corresponding to a 35.334 mm opening at the inferred station.

An exact axial ray for nominal f/1.9 would reach that stop plane at radius 18.004 mm. The adopted opening admits an entrance radius of 25.844 mm, corresponding to about f/1.94 by either entrance-pupil diameter or image-space sine. These are real-ray aperture effects at the inferred stop, while the paraxial aperture remains f/1.90.

The image plane is at the calculated paraxial back focus, 0.104 mm beyond the printed BF. The printed 63.9 mm may describe best focus: longitudinal spherical-aberration offsets at relative pupil heights 1, 0.85, 0.7 and 0.5 are about +0.04, −0.46, −0.51 and −0.33 mm. ## Field, Clear Apertures and Model Limits

The patent states fields up to ±12.0° or ±15.0° without assigning either limit to a particular example. The model uses the lower limit, ±12.0°, or 24.0° full field. At the image plane the real 12.0° chief ray reaches 21.097 mm height, giving the catalog image-circle diameter of 42.19 mm. This diameter is derived from the stated field, not a documented film format. No mount or camera format is established.

The axial beam and the 12.0° chief ray clear every modeled rim. Part of the outer bundle is vignetted by the front element: the first surface cuts about 20.0% of one side of the meridional bundle at 12.0°. This is a width estimate, not an area-weighted illumination measurement. At 10.0° and 15.0° the corresponding first-surface estimates are about 13.0% and 32.0%; the chiefs still clear.

All clear semi-diameters are estimates based on Figure 1's proportions, with the f/1.9 axial beam and 12.0° chief ray kept clear. The drawing is not uniformly to scale: the first element is tallest, the second and fourth similar, and the third shortest. The front and rear elements have square rims; the second and third elements have smaller rear curve endpoints, joined to their front rims by sloping edges. R6 ends at 20.4 mm beneath R5's 23 mm rim; its sag is about 6.40 mm, leaving the inferred stop beyond the glass. No flat lands are inferred from the drawn bevels. Wide-open geometric transmission is about 98.5%, 94.6% and 90.0% at 8.0°, 10.0° and 12.0°, respectively.
