# Audit log — KODAK 100mm f/8 (US 1,658,365 Design)

Checks made when the file was first prepared: transcription from the patent facsimile; an independent paraxial calculation and an independent spherical ray trace; a 1001-point scan of glass and air intervals across the aperture; the repository surface validator; and a search of the existing lens files for the same prescription.

No geometry override. All sampled physical intervals are positive, with a minimum glass thickness of 1.059 mm and a minimum air gap of 1.368 mm at the assumed 8 mm semi-diameters. Maximum rim slope 0.355. No total internal reflection on the f/8 axial rays. The independent ray trace and the application's trace agree on axial focus to better than 1e-9 mm for 18 tested rays.

Patent 1,658,365 was not found in any existing lens file, and its six radii do not appear together elsewhere.

No claim is made that the model matches a commercial lens.

See the analysis for the drawing-sheet R1 discrepancy, the signs of the radii, the assumed iris and rims, the use of D index and ν only, vignetting, and the patent's 32° field statement.

The Kodar f/7.9 of Pocket Kodak cameras (about 1925 to 1934, described by collectors as a triplet) is the nearest candidate product, but no source links it to this patent and no construction diagram was found; the product link is unproven.

## 2026-11-18 — Semi-diameter pass against the patent figure

Source: the single drawing on sheet 1 of US 1,658,365 (the patent's only figure, for its only example), measured from the native 300 dpi scan.

Scale: the optical axis lies on row 1409.5. Along the axis, the outer outline runs from 868 to 1412 px and the outline centrelines from about 872 to 1408 px, against a 14.8 mm vertex length: about 36.2 to 36.8 px/mm; 36.5 px/mm was used. Each span was checked against the table at that scale: L1 about 2.7 mm (table 2.6), S1 about 1.8 mm (1.8), L2 about 1.4 mm (1.2; the heavy outline adds width), S2 about 6.3 mm (6.6), L3 about 2.6 mm (2.6). The drawing is therefore close to scale axially, within about 10 % on every span. A side-by-side of the drawing and the model at that scale, with an overlay, shows the elements lining up along the axis.

Figure reading: all three elements are drawn square-cut, with flat tops and bottoms at one height. The outer ink runs from row 1180 to row 1640 on all three (230 px each side of the axis); the outline centreline is about 225 px. That is 6.2 mm (6.15 to 6.3 mm depending on the outline edge used), the same for every element.

The drawn height is no more than the f/8 axial beam at the front surface, so the figure draws the elements about as small as the stated aperture allows. The difference from the model (22 %) is real, but rims at the figure value cost light at the patent's stated 32° field. Geometric transmission at f/8 (the application's vignetting curve, out to the new 124.7 mm image circle) with the figure rims of 6.3 / 6.2 / 6.2 mm, against the 8 mm rims: 87.0 against 100 % at 8.7°, 65.0 against 97.4 % at 17.4°, 39.4 against 83.1 % at 26.1°, 30.5 against 74.8 % at 29.0°, 21.4 against 64.1 % at 31.9°; worst loss 44.3 points at 29.0°. Restoring L1 alone wins back 21.0 points and L3 alone 20.6 points. L2 alone at 6.2 mm costs 6.1 points at 17.4°; at 6.8 mm it costs 0.4 points. A uniform 7.8 mm still costs 4.6 points at 31.9°; L1 or L3 alone at 7.8 mm cost 2.7 and 2.0 points. So no set of figure-directed rims meets the rule that a cut may cost at most 3 points at any field, except reductions of 0.1 to 0.2 mm, which are within measurement noise. L2 alone could come down to about 6.8 mm for free, but the figure draws L2 at the same height as L1 and L3, and a smaller L2 between two 8 mm elements would put a step in the section that the drawing does not show. All rims stay at 8 mm.

Field before and after: engine half-field 32° both; the chief ray clears every rim to 42.3° (front surface limiting). Transmission at the patent's 32° is 64.1 %, unchanged.
