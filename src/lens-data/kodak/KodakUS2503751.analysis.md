# KODAK 100mm f/2.5 (US 2,503,751 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 2,503,751 A, _Photographic Objective of the Cooke Triplet Type_

**Inventors:** Walter Litten, George H. Aklin, Fred E. Altman

**Assignee:** Eastman Kodak Company

**Filed:** September 28, 1948

**Published:** April 11, 1950

**Example details:** Example 2; Figure 3 table on PDF p. 1 and repeated table in column 4 on PDF p. 3.

The patent gives seven numerical examples. Example 2 specifies a focal length of 100 mm, aperture f/2.5 and field of ±14°. Its distinguishing feature is a front positive element more than twice as thick as the rear positive element, together with a rear air space more than twice the front air space. The patent associates the thick front element with reduced zonal spherical aberration.

No production lens is documented for Example 2. The patent assigns cine-camera uses to Example 1 and a projection use to Example 5, which do not identify a product for this prescription.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is an all-spherical Cooke triplet with three air-spaced elements in three groups: positive, negative, positive. There are no cemented interfaces. The front and rear elements are biconvex, with their stronger surfaces facing away from the central biconcave element.

The six radii, three element thicknesses, two air spaces and three sodium D-line index/Abbe pairs follow the two printed Example 2 tables. The first-to-last vertex distance is 52.61 mm. The computed effective focal length is 100.0361 mm, 0.0361% above the stated 100 mm. The patent prints no back focus; the image plane is the computed paraxial infinity focus, 67.6793 mm behind the last vertex.

The stop is inferred within the printed 17.66 mm rear air space, 11.00 mm after surface 4 and 6.66 mm before surface 5, at axial coordinate 40.65 mm. Its inferred radius is 13.6207 mm. Figure 1 illustrates Example 1 and establishes the general rear-air-space stop arrangement, but supplies no Example 2 stop coordinate or diameter.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### Element 1 — Biconvex Positive

`nD = 1.734, νD = 51.1. Glass: Unmatched (nD 1.734 / ν 51.1), Kodak borate crown. Standalone focal length: +53.94 mm.`

The front crown supplies positive power. Its 13.65 mm center thickness is 2.58 times the rear crown's 5.30 mm thickness. The patent identifies this thickness relationship as particularly helpful in reducing the zonal aberration of Example 2.

### Element 2 — Biconcave Negative

`nD = 1.689, νD = 30.9. Glass: Unmatched (nD 1.689 / ν 30.9), catalogue dense flint. Standalone focal length: −34.81 mm.`

The central negative element balances the positive elements' chromatic and field contributions. The patent distinguishes its catalogue glass from the high-dispersion fluosilicate glasses used for the negative elements in several other examples. Its index is 0.045 below the average index of the two positive elements.

### Element 3 — Biconvex Positive

`nD = 1.734, νD = 51.1. Glass: Unmatched (nD 1.734 / ν 51.1), Kodak borate crown. Standalone focal length: +63.96 mm.`

The rear crown completes the positive–negative–positive power distribution. Its outward-facing rear surface is stronger than its front surface. The patent describes balancing the powers of the front and rear positive elements as part of controlling distortion.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent explicitly defines the index at the sodium D line, approximately 589.3 nm; these values are retained as D-line coordinates. It identifies the positive glasses as borate crowns then produced by Eastman Kodak, and the central negative glass as a type listed in manufacturers' catalogues (PDF p. 4, column 6). It does not identify exact melts or suppliers for the catalogue flint.

No available catalogue curve meets both the adopted absolute index tolerance of 0.0001 and Abbe tolerance of 0.15 at the D line. Hoya TAC4 is close to the crowns: its calculated nD is 1.733872 and νD is 51.0452, differing by −0.0001284 and −0.0548. Ohara L-TIM28 is close to the central flint at nD 1.689286 and νD 31.0147, differing by +0.0002865 and +0.1147. Both miss the index tolerance, so all elements retain Unmatched labels.

The patent gives no additional spectral indices or partial-dispersion values for Example 2. Chromatic calculations based only on its D-line index and Abbe number do not establish the historical glasses' full dispersion or measured lens performance.

## Focus Mechanism and Aperture

The prescription represents infinity focus only. The patent gives no close-focus distances or focusing movement for Example 2.

The inferred diaphragm gives a paraxial f-number of exactly 2.500. Exact axial tracing through that opening gives an entrance-pupil-equivalent aperture of about f/2.561 and an image-sine equivalent of f/2.556. These definitions differ slightly from the nominal paraxial aperture. The retained opening passes the axial beam without a clear-rim limit.

## Field and Model Limits

The field is the printed ±14°, or 28° in total. At the paraxial image plane, the real chief ray at 14° reaches an image height of 24.8918 mm and clears every rim. Twice that height is 49.7835 mm, recorded as a 49.78 mm image circle. This is the design-field diameter at the model's dimensions; no camera format or mount is documented. Distortion there is approximately −0.2%.

The clear semi-diameters are inferred: 25/25 mm on the front element, 22/20 mm on the negative element and 20/20 mm on the rear element. Off-axis vignetting remains; a meridional estimate gives about 78% transmission at 14°, which is not a measured light-transmission figure. The rear element has only about 0.93 mm edge thickness at its modeled rim. Figure 1 belongs to Example 1, so it does not establish Example 2's rim dimensions.

The summary table rounds R4/−R3 and −P2·F to 0.43 and 2.79, while the printed radii give approximately 0.425 and 2.784; the radii are retained. The other stated relationships include t1/t3 = 2.58, s1/s2 = 0.38 and overall length/F = 0.526.
