# KODAK 100mm f/8 (US 1,658,365 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 1,658,365 A, *Objective*  
**Application Number:** Serial No. 691,050  
**Filed:** February 6, 1924  
**Granted:** February 7, 1928  
**Inventors:** Fred E. Altman  
**Assignee:** Eastman Kodak Company  
**Example details:** The single "preferred example", tabulated on specification page 1 and drawn on the drawing sheet; dimensions in decimals of the focal length.

The KODAK 100mm f/8 (US 1,658,365 Design) follows the only numerical example of US 1,658,365. The patent describes an objective of three air-spaced single elements, the outer two positive and the inner one negative, intended for quantity production for folding hand cameras. It is stated to work at f/7.5 to f/8 and to cover 32° from the axis.

Eastman Kodak Company is the assignee, and the stated purpose and aperture fit the inexpensive triplets Kodak fitted to folding cameras in the following years, such as the Kodar f/7.9 that Science Museum Group collection records list on Pocket Kodak cameras of about 1925 to 1934; no source found ties this patent to the Kodar or to any other named lens, so no production lens is identified.

The patent prints its dimensions with the focal length as the unit. Every radius, thickness and air space is multiplied by 100 in the data file, giving a nominal 100 mm lens.

## Optical Architecture

The design is a Cooke-type triplet: positive, negative, positive, in three groups of one element each. The patent treats the first two elements as one front component and the third as the rear component, with the diaphragm and shutter in the larger air space between them.

Altman's stated aims are practical ones:

- All surfaces are kept as flat as possible, for cheap grinding. The patent claims every radius longer than two-tenths of the focal length; the shortest in the example is 0.239.
- The overall length is short, 0.148 of the focal length, so that the lens fits a compact folding camera. The larger air space is reduced to 0.066, which the patent calls an extent "not hitherto attempted" and credits with most of the lens's wide coverage with even illumination.
- The smaller air space is kept between 0.015 and 0.025 of the focal length (0.018 in the example), so that the spacing ring is not too thin and the lens is tolerant of small assembly errors.
- The elements are thick enough that their edges do not chip easily.
- The rear element is a meniscus, convex outward, with a focal length well below that of the whole objective, which makes the front pair strongly dispersive (a weak negative component).

The patent places both principal points ("Gauss points") in the larger air space, 0.002 and 0.009 behind the rear surface of the negative element. The model computes them at about −0.001 and +0.007 from that surface at the D line, the same within the table's rounding.

The patent does not print a stop position. The model places the stop at the midpoint of the 6.6 mm rear air space, 3.3 mm behind the negative element. The clear semi-diameters are not printed either; the model uses a uniform 8 mm (see Field Coverage and Limitations).

## Focal Length and the G′ Line

The table is headed "focal length = 1.000", but the prescription computes to 99.75 mm at the D line after scaling, 0.25 % short. The difference is a matter of wavelength, not a misprint. The patent's own element focal lengths are reproduced exactly when the G′ indices are used:

| Quantity | Patent | Computed at G′ | Computed at D |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Element 1 focal length | +0.343 | +0.3431 | +0.3504 |
| Element 2 focal length | −0.284 | −0.2844 | −0.2933 |
| Element 3 focal length | +0.747 | +0.7471 | +0.7631 |
| Whole objective | 1.000 | 0.9998 | 0.9975 |

Focal lengths quoted for the violet G′ line were usual for lenses meant for blue-sensitive plates and film. The front component's focal length is very sensitive to rounding; it computes to −4.58 at G′ against the printed −4.64.

The model keeps the printed D indices and dispersive ratios, so its focal length is the D-line value, 99.75 mm, with a computed paraxial back focus of 91.23 mm. The patent prints no back focus.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Biconvex Positive

`nd = 1.611, νd = 57.2.` Glass: Unmatched (barium crown, nD 1.611 / V 57.2); the patent names no glass maker. `f = +35.04 mm` at the D line.

The front collective element. Its front radius, 23.9 mm, is the strongest curve in the lens; its rear surface is nearly flat (−197 mm). The patent notes that focus may be adjusted by moving this element toward and away from the second, so the lens can be used on a fixed lens board. The model does not include that movement.

### L2 — Biconcave Negative

`nd = 1.575, νd = 42.7.` Glass: Unmatched (light flint, nD 1.575 / V 42.7). `f = −29.33 mm` at the D line.

The dispersive element, separated from L1 by 1.8 mm. Its stronger surface (24.7 mm) faces the diaphragm space. The patent claims a flint index below 1.60 for this element.

### L3 — Positive Meniscus, Convex to the Image

`nd = 1.611, νd = 57.2.` Glass: Unmatched (barium crown, nD 1.611 / V 57.2), the same glass as L1. `f = +76.31 mm` at the D line.

The rear component, a single meniscus with both surfaces concave toward the diaphragm (−146.4 mm and −35.6 mm). The patent asks for a focal length between forty and ninety per cent of that of the objective, about three-quarters in the example.

## Glass Identification and Selection

The patent prints, for each glass, the index for the D line, the index for the G′ line and the dispersive ratio ν. It names the types only as barium crown for the positive elements and light flint for the negative one.

| Element | D index | G′ index | ν | Patent description |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| L1, L3 | 1.611 | 1.624 | 57.2 | Barium crown |
| L2 | 1.575 | 1.593 | 42.7 | Light flint |

No current catalog glass matches either pair closely enough to be used as a label, and the patent names no glass maker, so the elements are labelled as unmatched with their printed values. The same 1.611 / 57.2 crown appears again in Altman's 1942 patent US 2,279,384 (the Kodak Enlarging Ektar), which points to a stock glass rather than a value chosen for this design.

The model's colour behaviour comes from the D index and ν alone. The printed G′ indices (434.0 nm) cannot be stored, since the model's blue reference is the g line at 435.8 nm, but they agree with what it infers: G′ minus D comes to 0.0136 for the crown and 0.0176 for the flint, against the printed 0.013 and 0.018, within the rounding of three-decimal values.

## Focus Mechanism

The model is fixed at infinity. The patent gives no close-focus data. It mentions only that the front element may be moved in a screw-threaded mount to adjust focus.

## Source Notes

The lettered table on the drawing sheet shows R1 as 2.39 and leaves out the air space S2, while the typeset table prints R1 = .239 and S2 = .066, which the text confirms (greatest curvature on a radius of .239; overall thickness .148); the model follows the typeset table.

The table prints the radii without signs. The signs used follow the drawing and are confirmed by the printed element focal lengths; for example, element 1 reaches +0.343 only as a biconvex lens.

## Field Coverage and Limitations

The patent states that the objective fully covers 32° from the axis at f/7.5 to f/8. The model's image circle, 124.7 mm, is that angle at the computed focal length (2 × 99.75 mm × tan 32°), and the model traces to it. With the assumed 8 mm rims the chief ray passes at 32° (it meets the front surface 5.6 mm from the axis). Geometric transmission at f/8 stays above 97 % to about 17°, then falls, limited by the front element, to about 83 % at 26° and 64 % at 32°.

The drawing is close to scale along the axis and draws all three elements square-cut at one height, about 6.2 mm at the model's scale. That is no more than the f/8 axial beam at the front surface (6.23 mm). Rims at that height would leave the axial beam intact but cut transmission at 29° from about 75 % to about 31 %, against the patent's stated aim of even illumination, so the model keeps the larger rims. Because the rims are assumed either way, the modeled vignetting is not evidence about the real lens.

The patent says each objective is individually fitted to the camera it is used on, mounted on a fixed lens board and focused by turning the front element in its thread; the catalog therefore lists it as a fixed-lens camera objective. No film format is assigned, since no source ties the prescription at this focal length to a film size.

Computed distortion is small: the 32° chief ray lands 62.6 mm from the axis against 62.3 mm for an undistorted image.
