# EMIL BUSCH 210mm f/11 (US 759,537 Design)

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 759,537 A, *Photographic Objective*  
**Application Number:** Serial No. 77,445  
**Filed:** October 3, 1901  
**Granted:** May 10, 1904  
**Inventors:** Karl Martin  
**Assignee:** Rathenower Optische Industrie-Anstalt vormals Emil Busch A.-G.  
**Example details:** One numerical example (specification p. 1, lines 75–85), drawn in Figure 1; Figure 2 shows two such sets combined into a double objective. Values are printed in millimetres at the stated focal distance of 210, so no scaling is applied.

Karl Martin, of Rathenow, assigned the patent to the Busch optical works there. The specification sets out to make a two-lens objective that is corrected spherically, chromatically and for astigmatism while using ordinary glasses: a flint of index about 1.60 for the negative lens and a crown of index about 1.50 for the positive lens. The patent contrasts this with the then-common belief that such a lens needed either a very heavy flint or a crown of higher index than the flint.

The single worked example gives four radii, two lens thicknesses, one air space, the nD and nG′ index of each glass, and the value Δ/2 = 3.79. For this example the text states:

1. An opening of f/11, at which the system is exactly corrected for spherical aberration.
2. A remaining spherical zone error of 0.25 mm, quoted for a focal distance of 100 mm.
3. A chromatic error that is slightly overcorrected, by 0.5 per mille of the focal distance.
4. Astigmatism corrected for a field of 68°.
5. An equivalent focal distance of 210.

The patent names no production lens; its relation to a Busch product is set out under Design Heritage and Context.

## Optical Architecture

The objective is two separated menisci with the diaphragm in front. Both lenses are concave toward the object:

- L1, a thick negative meniscus of the higher-index flint, faces the stop with its deep concave surface.
- L2, a positive meniscus of the lower-index crown, sits behind a 3.31 mm air space. Its front surface is almost flat and its rear surface strongly convex.

The air space between them is thicker at the rim than on the axis, so it works as a negative air lens. The claims describe it in exactly these terms. In the model L1 has a focal length of −88.22 mm and L2 +73.84 mm, and the pair combines to 210.19 mm.

The patent's design rule is about bending. Each surface's "curve" is ρ = F/R, and the rule is that the difference in curve between the two faces of the negative lens and that between the two faces of the positive lens should sum to about zero. For the example the patent prints:

- ρ = −6.02 and ρ₁ = −3.16, so ρ − ρ₁ = −2.86.
- ρ₂ = −0.35 and ρ₃ = −4.01, so ρ₂ − ρ₃ = +3.66.
- The sum Δ = +0.8.

The patent says earlier two-lens objectives have a value three or four times larger, which is why they could not flatten the astigmatic field. The printed ρ values match the radii with F ≈ 134.7. That is the focal length of the Figure 2 double objective built from two of these sets 7.58 mm apart (134.77 mm in a paraxial trace). The bending was evidently worked out for the double lens.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

### L1 — Negative Meniscus, concave to the object

nD = 1.6031 (sodium D), nG′ = 1.62386; no Abbe number printed. Glass: unmatched, the patent's "0.318" (Schott O. 318). f = −88.22 mm.

L1 is the "sufficiently hollow" negative meniscus that the text says compensates the astigmatism of the positive lens. Its front radius, −22.36 mm, is strongly curved, and the lens is 6.15 mm thick on the axis. Its deep concave front lies just behind the diaphragm. The front surface's distance from the stop is the main control of the oblique beams' astigmatism. That is why the stop position matters so much in this design (see Stop Position and Field Correction).

### L2 — Positive Meniscus, concave to the object

nD = 1.4967 (sodium D), nG′ = 1.50627; no Abbe number printed. Glass: unmatched, the patent's "0.802" (Schott O. 802). f = +73.84 mm.

L2 carries all the positive power. Its front radius, −383.35 mm, is nearly flat and its rear radius is −33.64 mm; the lens is 5.73 mm thick. Because its index is lower than the flint's, the patent's argument is that chromatic correction comes from the two glasses' different dispersions at an ordinary index ordering.

## Glass Identification and Selection

| Element | Printed nD | Printed nG′ | Patent label | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | 1.6031 | 1.62386 | 0.318 (Schott O. 318) | Negative flint meniscus |
| L2 | 1.4967 | 1.50627 | 0.802 (Schott O. 802) | Positive crown meniscus |

The table prints each glass's index for the sodium D line and the hydrogen G′ line (434 nm), not an Abbe number. The numbers 0.318 and 0.802 beside them are Schott catalogue designations of the period, O. 318 and O. 802. The Austrian counterpart, AT 8364 B, names them as glasses from the Schott list.

No catalogue glass matches both printed indices, so both labels stay unmatched. The model carries nD only, traces at a single wavelength and shows no colour error. The difference nG′ − nD is 0.02076 for L1 and 0.00957 for L2. Read against modern dispersion curves, that suggests Abbe numbers near 38 and 65, an ordinary light flint and crown. Those figures are estimates and are not used. The printed G′ indices still check the transcription: in a paraxial trace the G′ focus lies 0.110 mm beyond the D focus, which is 0.52 per mille of the focal length. The patent states an overcorrection of 0.5 per mille.

## Stop Position and Field Correction

The table ends with Δ/2 = 3.79. Figure 2 places the diaphragm midway between two of these sets, with their negative menisci facing it, so Δ/2 is the distance from the stop to the first vertex of each set. Figure 1 shows the single set with its diaphragm the same distance in front. Measured on the drawing, which is to scale (its stop opening matches f/11 within 1 %), the distance is about 3.8 mm. The model uses the printed 3.79 mm.

The stop position decides whether the patent's astigmatic correction appears. Coddington traces of the model at 34° half-field, the edge of the stated 68° field, give:

- With the stop at 3.79 mm: sagittal focus +0.70 mm and tangential focus +0.17 mm from the paraxial image plane. The astigmatic difference crosses zero near the edge of the stated field.
- With the stop 3.00 mm ahead of the first vertex instead, the tangential focus would lie at +16.5 mm.

The patent's anastigmatic flattening therefore depends on the printed stop distance.

## Focus Mechanism

The patent gives only the infinity setting. The model has no focus movement. The image plane is the computed paraxial back focus at the D line, 250.14 mm behind the last surface; the patent prints no back focus.

## Model Scope and Limitations

| Quantity | Patent | Model |
|---|---|---|
| Focal length | 210 | 210.19 mm (+0.09 %) |
| Aperture | f/11 | f/11, stop radius 9.55 mm |
| Spherical aberration at f/11 | exactly corrected | marginal ray within 0.01 mm of the paraxial focus |
| Spherical zone error (scaled to f = 100) | 0.25 mm | about 0.19 mm at the 0.7 zone |
| Chromatic error (G′ vs D) | 0.5 ‰ overcorrected | 0.52 ‰ overcorrected |
| Field with astigmatism corrected | 68° | astigmatic difference −0.5 mm at 34° |
| Back focus | not printed | 250.14 mm (computed) |

The clear radii are not printed; they are measured from Figure 1, which is drawn to scale. L1's concave front face ends at 10 mm, and a flat land continues to the element's 14.4 mm edge. That land stands 1.4 mm behind the stop plane, as the figure draws it. L1's rear face runs to the same 14.4 mm edge, and L2 is square-cut at 16 mm. No rim cuts the f/11 axial beam, and the chief ray clears every rim to 55° off axis. The model's 283 mm image circle comes from the stated 68° field at the printed focal distance of 210; the patent names no plate size. The chief ray reaches the edge of that circle 37.4° off axis, a little beyond 34° because of the lens's barrel distortion. About 85 % of the full beam passes at 34°, and about 80 % at the circle's edge. The tracing field, 46.6° half-angle, comes from the model's geometry, not from the patent.

## Design Heritage and Context

Martin published the idea in 1902 as "Ein neuer, lichtstarker Anastigmat aus einem normalen Glaspaar" (*Jahrbuch für Photographie und Reproduktionstechnik* 16, pp. 68–72), a communication from the Busch works. The 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* (article "Photography", vol. 21) credits Martin with showing that separated lenses of the older glasses can give full correction. It records Busch's 1902 "Omnar" built on this principle:

- Series III at f/7.7, followed by Series II at f/5.5 with a 75° field and Series I at f/4.5.
- A symmetrical doublet whose halves each pair a negative flint meniscus of higher index with a positive crown of lower index, separated by a negative air space.
- A back half that can be used alone.

That is the arrangement of the patent's claims and of Figure 2. The Austrian counterpart, AT 8364 B (filed 13 September 1901, published 25 July 1902), covers the same two-lens objective.

The Figure 1 example is therefore one half of an Omnar-type doublet, used alone with the stop in front as a convertible's single component would be. Two of these sets at the printed separation compute to 134.8 mm at f/5.5 for the same stop. No Busch catalogue found ties the 210 mm f/11 figures to a listed Omnar size, so the link is to the design family rather than to a specific product.
