# NIKON AI NIKKOR 50mm f/1.8

## Patent Reference and Design Identification

**Patent:** US 4,139,265, _Modified Gauss Type Photographic Lens_\
**Inventors:** Sei Matsui\
**Assignee:** Nippon Kogaku K.K.\
**Filed:** January 6, 1977, with Japanese priority of January 16, 1976\
**Published:** February 13, 1979\
**Example details:** Figure 1, Example 1 in columns 3–4, and claim 1.

Nikon's dealer drawing identifies the optics of the original 1978 long-barrel AI Nikkor 50mm f/1.8 with unusual precision.

1. The dealer sheet lists six elements in five groups and a 46° field. Its section shows three separate front menisci, two rear menisci cemented together and a biconvex rear singlet. Figure 1 shows the same sequence. Of Matsui's four examples, only Example 1 opens to f/1.8. Examples 2–4 are f/2.
2. The barrel is marked 50 mm, but Nikon dimensions the focal length at 51.6 mm. A scale of ×0.516 takes the patent's 100 mm prescription to that focal length. The resulting glass-and-air stack measures 34.851 mm and its back focus is 37.605 mm. Nikon gives 34.85 mm and 37.6 mm respectively.
3. The two focal points are 96.678 mm apart and the principal planes are 6.523 mm apart. Nikon gives 96.65 mm and 6.55 mm. Agreement in the stack length, back focus and principal-plane positions strongly supports Example 1 as the long-barrel design. [Nikon dealer sheet, dimensioned section](https://www.pacificrimcamera.com/rl/00594/00594.pdf#page=59)

Nikon dates the original AI lens to 1978, the year after the AI Nikkor 50mm f/2, and the compact AI-S to 1980, when it went on sale in Japan with the Nikon EM. [Nikon, Tale 2](https://imaging.nikon.com/imaging/information/story/0002/) The compact lens also has six elements in five groups. Its narrower diaphragm space and flat rear face on the first element distinguish it in section. Nikon credits the compact design to Souichi Nakamura and states that the Series E 50mm f/1.8 shares its basic optics. Nikon names no designer for the 1978 lens; the patent names Sei Matsui. [Nikon, Tale 60](https://imaging.nikon.com/imaging/information/story/0060/) · [Compact AI-S section](https://imaging.nikon.com/imaging/information/story/0060/img/img1.jpg)

The 1978 lens was made until 1982 and for a short time alongside the last AI 50mm f/2. Among Nikon's 50mm f/1.8 lenses it alone lacks the AI-S notch on the mount flange. [JAPB, data sheet](https://japb.net/gear/gear-review-index/ds_nikkor-ai-50f18/) Richard Haw reports that a later, short-lived long-nose AI-S version kept this optical design in a different barrel. [Richard Haw, repair notes](https://richardhaw.com/2020/05/23/repair-nikkor-50mm-f-1-8-ai/)

## Optical Architecture

Matsui's modified double Gauss uses six spherical elements and four glasses. The front two elements converge the beam. The concave faces of L3 and L4 oppose one another across the diaphragm, with nearly equal radii of 16.20 mm and −16.50 mm. Columns 2–3 link this symmetry to the correction of coma and lateral colour. The longer back focus leaves room for the reflex mirror. Neither the patent nor Nikon's section identifies an aspherical or ED element.

The patent tabulates the radii, axial spacings, glasses, f/1.8, the 46° field and a back focus of 0.729f, but leaves the clear radii and the iris position undimensioned. Figure 1 puts the iris about 57% of the way across the L3–L4 gap. At that position a calculated 10.06 mm stop radius gives f/1.8. The clear radii are estimated from Figure 1, scaled by its drawn vertex-to-vertex length. L2 is square-edged at 14 mm, as drawn. The front of L3 stays at 12.2 mm, inside the drawn rim, because its face would meet the rear of L2 at about 12.95 mm. The rear of L3 is widened slightly to pass the full axial cone. As in Figure 1 and Nikon's section, flat lands carry the concave rear face of L3 out to 12.2 mm and the concave front face of L4 out to 12.4 mm. The film plane lies at the paraxial back focus calculated from the prescription. At infinity the corner chief ray reaches the 35 mm frame at a half-field of 23.0°, consistent with the specified 46° picture angle.

## Element-by-Element Analysis

Focal lengths below are those of the individual elements in air.

### L1 — Positive Meniscus

nd = 1.79631, νd = 40.8. Glass unmatched. f = +64.11 mm. The strongly curved front face collects the incoming light. A 0.100 mm air space separates the shallow rear face from L2.

### L2 — Positive Meniscus

nd = 1.78797, νd = 47.5. Hoya TAF4 catalog equivalent. f = +66.71 mm. L2 adds positive power ahead of L3. The 1.000 mm space between them allows their facing surfaces to have different curvatures.

### L3 — Negative Meniscus

nd = 1.74000, νd = 28.2. Hoya FD3 catalog equivalent. f = −32.52 mm. The rear face supplies most of this element's negative power. It is the front of the two nearly symmetric concave surfaces around the stop.

### L4 — Negative Meniscus

nd = 1.74000, νd = 28.2. Hoya FD3 catalog equivalent. f = −26.88 mm. L4 places the same dispersive glass on the opposite side of the stop. Its shallow rear surface, r8, is the cemented interface with L5.

### L5 — Positive Meniscus

nd = 1.74443, νd = 49.4. Ohara S-LAM60 spectral proxy. f = +33.95 mm. The two cemented glasses have similar indices and quite different dispersions. L5's convex rear face supplies most of its positive power. Together L4 and L5 form a weak negative doublet, f = −383.74 mm.

### L6 — Biconvex Positive

nd = 1.79631, νd = 40.8. Glass unmatched. f = +50.47 mm. The final singlet sits 0.100 mm behind L5 and brings the beam toward the film. Its stronger curvature faces the image, as Nikon's section shows.

## Glass Identification and Selection

Example 1 gives d-line indices and Abbe numbers. L1 and L6 share one glass pair, and L3 and L4 share another. Conditions (7) and (8) allow Matsui to use the more dispersive glass in the two negative elements and lower dispersion in all four positive elements. Hoya TAF4 and FD3 match the printed pairs of L2, L3 and L4 at the printed precision and supply their dispersion curves. L5's pair, which recurs in Examples 2 and 3, matches no catalog glass at the printed precision. It lies 0.0012 in index and 0.06 in Abbe number from Ohara S-LAM60, inside the site's spectral-proxy tolerance, so the model keeps the printed index and borrows that glass's dispersion. The patent gives no trade names or suppliers. The L1/L6 pair has no catalog glass within that tolerance; the nearest, Hoya NBFD2, differs by 0.34 in Abbe number.

## Focus Mechanism

The original lens focuses manually on a helicoid, with about 210° of focusing-ring rotation. The dealer sheet gives a range from infinity to 0.45 m. A measured example reaches 1:6.8 (0.147×) and has seven straight diaphragm blades, with marked stops from f/1.8 to f/22. [Richard Haw, illustrated disassembly](https://richardhaw.com/2020/05/23/repair-nikkor-50mm-f-1-8-ai/) · [JAPB, measurements of the original AI lens](https://japb.net/gear/gear-review-index/ds_nikkor-ai-50f18/)

Matsui gives only the infinity spacings and specifies neither a finite conjugate nor a reproduction ratio. The optical model therefore shows infinity only. A calculation with the whole scaled lens moved forward shows how the product's range compares. Focusing an object 0.45 m from the film plane takes 7.70 mm of extension and gives 0.149× (1:6.7). The same distance taken from the front of the lens would give 0.121× (1:8.3). The measured 1:6.8 therefore fits a distance scale counted from the film plane.
